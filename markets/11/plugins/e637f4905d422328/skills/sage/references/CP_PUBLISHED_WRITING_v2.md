Most
&#x20;of us were taught that capitalism is a modern invention. Just a clever&#x20;
system that emerged from the Enlightenment and refined through centuries
&#x20;of economic thought. Markets, money, growth... all inevitable outcomes&#x20;
of human progress.

It’s a comforting story, one that suggests design, intention, and evolution toward something better.

But that story starts too late.

Long
&#x20;before corporations, before accounting turned numbers into power,&#x20;
before law made ownership permanent, there was a simpler rule… one so&#x20;
old it still echoes in playgrounds and proverbs:

**Finders keepers.**

Whoever
&#x20;arrived first claimed the land. The water. The trees. The animals.&#x20;
Eventually, the people. What could be taken was taken. What could be&#x20;
held was owned. What could be defended was declared legitimate.

Over time, force hardened into custom. Custom became law. And law learned to dress itself as morality. It’s said that *possession is nine-tenths of the law*. That’s a polite euphemism for the oldest rule of banditry:

***What I can take, I own.***

This
&#x20;was the first durable economic game humanity played. Not because it was
&#x20;just or wise, but because it worked. It scaled. It survived.

Let’s call it **Game A**.

Not because it’s the best game, but because it was the first one powerful enough to take over the world.


### **Game A: Capture First, Charge Later**

[image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!pPuF!,f_auto,q_auto\:good,fl_progressive\:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F89342df6-a188-4a55-b597-b55a44d033b0_1536x1024.png)

 ([image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!pPuF!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto\:good,fl_progressive\:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F89342df6-a188-4a55-b597-b55a44d033b0_1536x1024.png))

Game
&#x20;A is often confused with trade, markets, or private property. But those
&#x20;are just what the game looks like in action. The core move is simpler:

**Capture first. Charge later.**

The Earth began as commons: land, water, food, shelter, energy. No owners. No prices. Just life sustaining life.

Game
&#x20;A emerged when pieces of that shared world were taken from the commons&#x20;
and claimed on private ledgers. At first through violence and conquest.&#x20;
Later through something more durable: language, accounting, and law.

Once
&#x20;ownership could be recorded outside human memory—on clay tablets,&#x20;
parchment, ledgers—the game became permanent. Trade turned into math.&#x20;
Math turned into contracts. Contracts turned into obligations&#x20;
enforceable by force.

That shift slowly conditioned how wealth flows.

From
&#x20;that point forward, rewards moved away from those who did the work and&#x20;
toward those who controlled access. Resource owners collected profit.&#x20;
Landowners collected rent. Money creators collected interest.

Everyone else paid to live.

This pattern became so normalized we stopped noticing it. But once you name it, it’s hard to unsee:

**Capitalism doesn’t begin with markets.**

**It begins with ownership positions that generate ongoing claims.**

### &#x20;**From Things to Time**

At first, Game A focused on capturing physical things: land, minerals, tools, livestock.

But the real breakthrough came when the game learned to capture something more abstract: **human activity**.

Labor. Time. Creativity. Coordination.

Early
&#x20;trade was cooperative. Communities needed ways to remember&#x20;
contributions and obligations as they grew beyond face-to-face trust.&#x20;
Accounting emerged as the solution with tallies, tokens, and ledgers&#x20;
that made reciprocity scalable.

But the moment obligations could be counted, they could be gamed.

A
&#x20;surplus written down could be claimed later. A debt recorded could be&#x20;
enforced. A promise could be transferred to someone who never made it.

The
&#x20;ledger stopped being merely a record of what happened. It became a tool
&#x20;for making things happen: numbers commanding real labor and resources.

This is where power entered the ledger.

Trade,
&#x20;as it functions inside Game A, is not neutral or fair exchange. It's&#x20;
mediated by unequal positions: who controls access, who needs it now,&#x20;
who can afford to wait. When one person needs water, medicine, or&#x20;
shelter and another owns the resource, "trade" is just a polite word for
&#x20;ransom.

Buying isn’t merely exchange, it’s a legal method&#x20;
of capture. Money buys already-enclosed commons and already-captured&#x20;
activity. What looks like choice often turns out to be permission,&#x20;
granted at a price.


### **Credit: The Capture of Time Itself**

[image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!8OtC!,f_auto,q_auto\:good,fl_progressive\:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe3988a33-7d5f-4883-a36b-9a3194050750_1536x1024.png)

 ([image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!8OtC!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto\:good,fl_progressive\:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe3988a33-7d5f-4883-a36b-9a3194050750_1536x1024.png))

If ownership captures space, credit captures time.

Credit is commonly described as lending or finance. Structurally, it’s something more precise:

**Credit is temporal power.**

Whoever
&#x20;controls the creation and distribution of credit commands present&#x20;
activity. Credit is purchasing power: it buys labor, resources, and&#x20;
future production right now. Nations issue credit directly into&#x20;
circulation through spending. Banks create it by taking on&#x20;
debt—borrowing new money into existence simultaneously as a liability&#x20;
and as an asset (the loan contract). It’s all just credit and debt, and&#x20;
both enter the market the same way: as new money that can buy whatever&#x20;
is available.

But here’s where the mechanism of capture reveals itself.

When
&#x20;a nation spends credit into existence, no repayment is required. The&#x20;
money circulates to facilitate exchange and enable coordination. When a&#x20;
bank creates money through lending, it enters the market with an&#x20;
obligation attached. The money functions identically, buying the same&#x20;
goods and commanding the same labor, but this money is debt, it must be&#x20;
repaid, with interest, regardless of what happens next.

Once a&#x20;
debt is recorded, it doesn’t matter why it happened or whether it’s&#x20;
still fair... it must be paid. The debt becomes an immortal claim,&#x20;
detached from the circumstances that created it.

Let that sink in for a moment. Two forms of money creation. Same purchasing power in markets. Completely different futures.

This is how credit captures the future.

Not because money itself binds tomorrow’s labor, but because of the *form*
&#x20;it takes when it enters circulation. Debt-based money reaches forward&#x20;
in time, converting future activity into present obligation. It ensures&#x20;
that tomorrow’s work services yesterday’s ledger entries.

The
&#x20;obligations become tradeable, transferable, and enforceable across&#x20;
generations. They survive the people who made them. They compound. They&#x20;
never forget.

Once credit exists in this form, capture no longer&#x20;
has to happen upfront. It can be pre-authorized, pre-priced, and&#x20;
pre-enforced.

This is how Game A escaped natural limits—not by&#x20;
creating abundance, but by monetizing obligation. The system learned to&#x20;
harvest activity that hasn’t even occurred yet.

But here’s where it gets interesting.

The real power isn’t just in whether money demands repayment. It’s in *who gets it first* and *what they do with it*.

Sovereign
&#x20;credit—even without repayment obligations—flows overwhelmingly to&#x20;
ownership positions. Nations fund corporations, subsidize asset holders,
&#x20;backstop banks, and guarantee property values. That newly created money
&#x20;doesn’t distribute evenly. It concentrates in hands that already&#x20;
control access to resources.

Those ownership positions then&#x20;
recycle that credit back into more ownership: buying land, acquiring&#x20;
competitors, and financializing everything that can be turned into a&#x20;
rentable asset. **The machine extracts, the money flows, and assets compound.**

Meanwhile,
&#x20;banks create money by issuing debt—and collect interest on money they&#x20;
conjured from nothing. Just for having the legal privilege to create&#x20;
currency through lending, they harvest ongoing claims on future labor.

Nations fund ownership structures that capture the future. Banks capture it by making money itself a rentable commodity.

The capture happens in the flow, in the structure, in who gets access to credit creation and under what terms.

The state doesn’t capture the future.
**Assets do.**

### **The Mathematical Design of Inequality**

[image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ulO-!,f_auto,q_auto\:good,fl_progressive\:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F26e75ff6-fb5f-4a81-8442-665ad00f817e_1536x1024.png)

 ([image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ulO-!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto\:good,fl_progressive\:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F26e75ff6-fb5f-4a81-8442-665ad00f817e_1536x1024.png))

Game A isn’t unequal because people are greedy. It’s unequal because it’s **mathematically designed that way**.

- Assets are captured narrowly
- Currency units are distributed broadly
- Debt is enforced asymmetrically

Those
&#x20;who control scarce assets gain automatic claims on future activity.&#x20;
Those who don’t must sell their time to access what already exists. Over
&#x20;time, this dynamic compounds—regardless of intention, regardless of&#x20;
individual virtue.

This is the quiet brilliance and brutality of the system.

Capitalism doesn’t primarily organize production. It organizes **ownership positions**. And once those positions exist, they reach forward into the future, harvesting activity before it occurs.

As
&#x20;finance evolved, this became even more abstract. Assets became stores&#x20;
of value. Derivatives became bets on bets. Every piece of the&#x20;
commons—every human activity—was transformed into something tradeable.&#x20;
The complexity obscures the pattern, but the pattern remains simple:

1. **Capture** — Take it from the commons
2. **Enclose** — Lock it behind ownership claims
3. **Charge** — Set the price for access
4. **Collect** — Money flows to the owner
5. **Repeat** — Use collected money to capture more

This is the game. This is what drives everything else.

But here’s what most people miss: We’ve built an entire economic system with **Capital**
&#x20;at the center, as if accumulated wealth is the source of all value. But
&#x20;capital is just transformed resources. The real power, the actual sun&#x20;
around which everything orbits, is **Credit**: the ability to create purchasing power, to authorize activity, to make futures possible.

Credit is the fuel. It powers the game.

Right
&#x20;now, that fuel flows primarily through debt and into ownership&#x20;
positions—feeding the engine of capture, extraction, and accumulation.&#x20;
But credit could just as easily fuel life itself: enabling&#x20;
participation, securing needs, empowering creation.

**It’s a design decision. And we are the designers.**

The same fuel. A different game.

Whoever controls credit creation controls what gets built, what gets valued, and ultimately, what gets to live.

History
&#x20;is replete with this pattern: conquest dressed as civilization,&#x20;
extraction justified as progress, banditry legitimized through law. It’s
&#x20;visible in every empire, every corporate expansion, every resource grab
&#x20;playing out in headlines today. The same circuit, endlessly repeated,&#x20;
now operating at planetary scale.

**And this ongoing circuit threatens to engulf us all in another world war… over ownership positions.**

Money could have remained a neutral tool for cooperation, a simple scoreboard for exchange.

Instead, through enclosure, banking, and law, it became a mechanism for turning life into numbers… and numbers into control.


### **Corporations: When Capture Became Immortal**

[image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!cW9V!,f_auto,q_auto\:good,fl_progressive\:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8c0413b7-b259-46c7-acc6-ba99a8e1d12f_1536x1024.png)

 ([image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!cW9V!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto\:good,fl_progressive\:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8c0413b7-b259-46c7-acc6-ba99a8e1d12f_1536x1024.png))

For
&#x20;a long time, Game A was limited by human scale. Individuals died.&#x20;
Families fragmented. Wealth dissipated across generations. Power had&#x20;
natural limits.

Then came the corporation.

Originally a&#x20;
practical strategy of people pooling resources to undertake projects too
&#x20;large or risky for individuals alone. But over time, the wrapper became
&#x20;the point.

A corporation is not a building or a business. At its legal core, it’s this:

**A fictional person that can own things, enter contracts, and live forever.**

This entity:

- Separates responsibility from ownership, shielding the humans behind it
- Limits losses and liability to what’s inside the container
- Allows ownership to be divided, traded, and inherited without disruption
- Persists beyond any individual human life

That last part changed everything.

An
&#x20;immortal entity—with no nervous system, no mortality, no inherent&#x20;
obligation to human flourishing—entered the economy. It optimized&#x20;
relentlessly. It accumulated without end. It survived while humans&#x20;
didn’t.

A machine with an appetite but no satiation point.

**Moloch**, to use the language of my tribe.

Once
&#x20;corporations were normalized, capture no longer had to reset each&#x20;
generation. It could compound forever. Wealth became effectively&#x20;
immortal. Game A was no longer a temporary imbalance… it became a&#x20;
perpetual machine.


### **Nations: Making Banditry Legal**

[image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!U66W!,f_auto,q_auto\:good,fl_progressive\:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0b4f8370-d4ec-4bf1-bce0-95c9dd8ba107_1536x1024.png)

 ([image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!U66W!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto\:good,fl_progressive\:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0b4f8370-d4ec-4bf1-bce0-95c9dd8ba107_1536x1024.png))

A
&#x20;nation is a peculiar thing. It has no body, no conscience, no product&#x20;
to sell. It doesn’t grow food or build homes. Yet it creates money,&#x20;
taxes, regulates, enforces contracts, and binds generations not yet&#x20;
born.

Here’s what most people miss: **nations and corporations co-evolved.** They didn’t emerge separately and then interact. They shaped each other into mutually reinforcing tools.

Elites
&#x20;with capital needed legal infrastructure to make Game A permanent and&#x20;
enforceable at scale. Nations became that infrastructure—the rule-making
&#x20;entity that transforms capture into legitimate ownership, makes&#x20;
extraction legally binding, and ensures debts get paid by force if&#x20;
necessary.

Through money creation, legal frameworks,&#x20;
contract enforcement, and property rights, nations define what counts as
&#x20;ownership, which debts must be honored, who can participate in markets,
&#x20;and under what conditions. They don’t merely manage economies: **they construct the game board.**

And here’s the sleight of hand that makes it all work:

Nations
&#x20;have the power to create money: real credit that requires no repayment.
&#x20;They are monetary sovereigns, the source of the currency itself. But&#x20;
this power is carefully hidden behind an elaborate theatrical&#x20;
performance designed to make money seem scarce, borrowed, or dependent&#x20;
on savings.

The public is taught that national spending works like
&#x20;household budgets: you must earn before you spend, borrow if you&#x20;
overspend, and eventually pay it all back. This is completely false. A&#x20;
sovereign nation that issues its own currency creates money by spending&#x20;
it into existence. The constraint isn’t money—it’s real resources and&#x20;
political will.

But if people understood this, they might start&#x20;
asking uncomfortable questions. Like why nations spend so much funding&#x20;
ownership positions—corporations, banks, asset holders—while claiming&#x20;
scarcity when it comes to public needs. Or why banks get the legal&#x20;
privilege to create money through lending and collect interest on it,&#x20;
while everyone else must work for currency that already exists.

So
&#x20;the illusion is maintained. Nations perform fiscal “responsibility.”&#x20;
They issue bonds as if borrowing from savers. They talk about debt&#x20;
ceilings and balanced budgets. All theater designed to obscure the real&#x20;
game.

**The nation’s actual function is twofold:**

First,
&#x20;it makes the capture, extraction, and trade of assets legal and&#x20;
enforceable. Property rights, corporate charters, banking regulations,&#x20;
contract law: all the infrastructure that lets ownership positions&#x20;
persist and compound across generations.

Second, it spends new&#x20;
credit into existence to operate the system, while taxation functions to
&#x20;reclaim and regulate that credit—binding citizens into the legal and&#x20;
monetary order that enforces ownership and debt.

Corporations&#x20;
capture. Citizens labor. Banks issue debt. And the nation monopolizes&#x20;
the power that makes it all legal, enforceable, and fundable.

Quite convenient for the machine.

Corporations exist to generate profit for owners.

Nations exist to make that profit extraction legitimate, permanent, and backed by force.

It’s
&#x20;a partnership with constant tension, but a partnership nonetheless. As&#x20;
Max Weber understood, politics is about who enforces the rules. Power,&#x20;
once centralized, is predictably used to preserve itself.


### **When the Game Went Global**

As economies globalized, the line between nation and corporation blurred.

Nations
&#x20;began orienting themselves around “competitiveness”—measuring success&#x20;
by GDP growth, as if countries were companies. But citizens are not&#x20;
shareholders with exit rights; they are subjects bound by law and&#x20;
obligation.

Corporations grew larger than cities,&#x20;
then nations, then entire regions. Capital moved freely across borders,&#x20;
while people remained bound to them.

Gradually, something subtle shifted.

"Citizens" became "taxpayers." Then "consumers." Then "human capital", a euphemism that reveals the game's true logic.

Political
&#x20;voice narrowed. Creative self-direction eroded. Participation was&#x20;
reduced to purchase decisions, while real choices were made elsewhere…&#x20;
on ledgers most people never see.

The game didn’t stop. It accelerated.

Only the scale changed.


### **What the Game Is Really Made Of**

[image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5NwF!,f_auto,q_auto\:good,fl_progressive\:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff7b5b59c-d66a-42b6-87de-4ee1908f6881_1536x1024.png)

 ([image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5NwF!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto\:good,fl_progressive\:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff7b5b59c-d66a-42b6-87de-4ee1908f6881_1536x1024.png))

Strip away the institutions, jargon, and mythology, and every society is built from four elements:

1. **Ecosystems** — the source of all real wealth
2. **Activity** — human labor and creativity
3. **Capital** — transformed resources that amplify activity
4. **Rules** — agreements about access, ownership, and purpose

Game
&#x20;A fails because it treats ecosystems as inputs to be depleted, humans&#x20;
as costs to be minimized, and rules as tools for extraction rather than&#x20;
coordination.

We don’t live in a natural economy. We live inside a dense web of **legal fictions**: ownership titles, corporate persons, national borders, financial claims.

All invented.
All enforced.
None inevitable.

The
&#x20;most dangerous illusion is believing this system is simply “how the&#x20;
world works”, as if it emerged from natural law rather than accumulated&#x20;
human choices, most of them made by people with something to gain.


### **Where This Leaves Us**

Game A worked... until it didn’t.

It
&#x20;scaled production. Concentrated power. Extracted extraordinary value&#x20;
from the Earth. But it was never designed to be sustainable, equitable,&#x20;
or life-affirming. And now the bill is coming due: ecologically,&#x20;
socially, psychologically.

This doesn’t mean humanity failed.
**It means we’re still playing a primitive game, only now with advanced tools.**

The
&#x20;game optimized for capture and accumulation in a world of apparent&#x20;
abundance. But that abundance was always temporary and borrowed from&#x20;
future generations, extracted from living systems that can’t regenerate&#x20;
fast enough.

Now we’re running into the hard limits. Not&#x20;
because we lack technology or intelligence, but because the rules of the
&#x20;game themselves are breaking down under their own momentum.


#### **A note on what this isn’t:**

This analysis isn’t new. Marx observed it in *Capital*:
&#x20;“The means of production monopolized by a certain section of society.”&#x20;
Lenin called it finance capital—the merger of industrial and banking&#x20;
power into extraction machines. Ellen Meiksins Wood traced it to the&#x20;
enclosure of the English commons. Elinor Ostrom proved commons&#x20;
governance can work without enclosure or privatization. David Harvey&#x20;
named it “accumulation by dispossession.” Thomas Piketty demonstrated it
&#x20;mathematically: r > g, return on capital perpetually exceeds growth.
&#x20;Michael Hudson showed how finance becomes parasitic, extracting rent&#x20;
through debt. Yanis Varoufakis argues we’ve transcended capitalism&#x20;
entirely into technofeudalism: cloud fiefs replacing factories.

The pattern is unmistakable: **ownership positions that extract value without producing it.**

Here’s what matters: It’s not about WHO owns. It’s about ownership AS A MECHANISM for perpetual extraction.

Whether
&#x20;capitalist or communist, if you maintain the structure where ownership&#x20;
positions generate unearned claims on future activity, you get the same&#x20;
outcome: inequality, extraction, capture. The Soviet experiment didn’t&#x20;
fail because socialism is impossible, it failed because they replaced&#x20;
private capitalists with party bureaucrats while keeping the extractive&#x20;
structure intact. Different owners, same game.

What’s different now isn’t the pattern. It’s the scale… and the existential stakes.

# How Money Really Works

### Part 2: The Definitive Guide to Credit, Debt & the Illusion of Scarcity — The Money Trick: How Banks Captured the Ledger

[Common Planet Foundation](https://substack.com/@commonplanet) and [Remzi Bajrami](https://substack.com/@remzib)

Feb 27, 2026

Audio read by the author in standard and faster-paced versions:

“Money has become the life-blood of the community… The monetary system is the distributory mechanism.” — Frederick Soddy

If
&#x20;corporations captured the Earth’s resources, banks captured something&#x20;
even more fundamental: the power to measure and control access to them.&#x20;
They did so with one of the most misunderstood inventions in human&#x20;
history: Money.

#### The Trick

Most people imagine money as a thing. This is understandable. It’s easier to think about owning *real* things than abstractions. But money is not a thing. **Money is credit.** A number on a ledger. An accounting entry that tracks claims on society’s resources.

That’s it. That’s all it ever was.

It’s
&#x20;not valuable in itself. It’s valuable as a unit of exchange for things&#x20;
of real value. It’s pure accounting for coordination. It’s just&#x20;
information.

### How Credit Actually Works (And Who Controls It)

All modern money falls into exactly two categories. Everything else is just an asset.

1. **Permanent (base) credit** - Created by governments. No debt obligation. Circulates until deleted through taxation.
2. **Temporary credit** - Created by banks. Borrowed into existence. Must be repaid with interest. Disappears when repaid.

Crypto advocates might object here. But digital tokens aren’t money, they’re speculative abstract assets you trade for money.

As
&#x20;we explored in Part 1: “Nations have the power to create money: real&#x20;
credit that requires no repayment. They are monetary sovereigns, the&#x20;
source of the currency itself. But this power is carefully hidden behind
&#x20;an elaborate theatrical performance designed to make money seem scarce,
&#x20;borrowed, or dependent on savings.”

This theatrical performance is what we’re about to dissect.

Governments
&#x20;can, and do, create credit money at will. When a central bank spends&#x20;
into the economy, it doesn’t borrow from anyone. It marks up numbers on a
&#x20;ledger. The credit exists because the government says it exists.

But how does this actually work? Let’s trace the exact flow.

#### How Permanent Credit Actually Flows

##### **Step 1: Congress Authorizes Spending**

Credit
&#x20;is a creature of law, so Congress doesn’t need to “find” money. It&#x20;
authorizes the creation of money through spending bills.

If&#x20;
Congress spends (adds) $7 trillion for the year and collects (deletes)&#x20;
$5 trillion in revenue (mostly via taxes), that’s +$2 trillion permanent
&#x20;credit into the economy. This is usually called the “deficit”, and&#x20;
you’ll see why soon.

##### **Step 2: The Treasury General Account (TGA)**

The
&#x20;U.S. Treasury has one main account at the Federal Reserve: the Treasury
&#x20;General Account. This is the government’s checking account. Not at a&#x20;
commercial bank, but at the Fed itself. Really it’s just an accounting&#x20;
entry, mostly for political reasons.

**Nations do not “possess” or “save” their own money… they command issuance.**

Think
&#x20;of the board game Monopoly. The “bank” in the game works exactly like a
&#x20;sovereign currency-issuing government. The money sitting in the bank&#x20;
box isn’t “in play” yet, it’s just paper waiting to be issued. Players&#x20;
don’t worry about whether the bank “has enough money.” If the bank runs&#x20;
low on bills, you just add more scraps of paper or adjust the numbers.&#x20;
The constraint isn’t the physical bills: it’s the game rules and what&#x20;
players agree to accept.

The Treasury account works the same way.&#x20;
Money sitting there isn’t counted as part of the money supply yet&#x20;
because it hasn’t been spent into the economy. And just like Monopoly,&#x20;
there’s no real limit. The government creates money digitally—just&#x20;
numbers in computers. Those digital dollars can be exchanged for&#x20;
physical bills (Federal Reserve Notes) if needed, but the bills are just
&#x20;tokens representing the numbers. It’s digital numbers all the way down.
&#x20;Pure accounting.

In Monopoly, everyone knows the bank can’t run out. In real life, we’ve been deceived to believe the opposite.

##### **Step 3: Taxes Flow In**

You can think of taxes as either:

- Money recycled back into the Treasury account, or
- Money deleted and then recreated when spent

Either framing works since the balances are identical. **Taxes delete money.** They don’t “fund” anything! They simply remove money from circulation.

##### **Step 4: Treasury Issues Bonds**

Operationally,
&#x20;current law requires Treasury to issue bonds essentially equal to the&#x20;
“deficit”, but this is a self-imposed rule, not a funding necessity.

That
&#x20;+$2 trillion now sitting in bank accounts represents potential spending
&#x20;power. If it flows into consumption or investment too quickly, it&#x20;
drives up prices. So bonds drain the $2T by converting spendable&#x20;
deposits into locked-up savings that earn interest but can’t immediately
&#x20;bid up prices.

When you buy a Treasury bond, you’re transferring&#x20;
liquid deposits from your bank into a time-locked interest-bearing&#x20;
account at the Fed. The same way your bank considers your deposit their&#x20;
liability, the Fed too records these as a liability and calls it “debt.”

**It’s crucial to know these bonds are not borrowing in any real sense.**

The
&#x20;money doesn’t leave the economy. It’s still there, but locked into a&#x20;
bond, sitting on someone’s balance sheet as an asset, earning interest.&#x20;
It’s in the game. Just temporarily parked.

So why issue bonds at all? Three reasons:

- Drain money from the banking system to manage interest rates & inflation
- Create safe assets for wealth storage with guaranteed interest
- Shape the illusion that government spending is “financed” by savings

In other words, the banking cartel gaslights the public so it can collect guaranteed interest directly from government.

##### **Step 5: The Fed Credits the Treasury**

Most
&#x20;people think when taxes are paid and bonds are sold, the proceeds flow&#x20;
into the TGA and fund spending. What's actually happening is different.&#x20;
Taxes deleted money. The bond money is still in the game, just parked.&#x20;
The Fed credits the TGA with those amounts. That’s it. The Fed is the&#x20;
government’s bank, not a constraint on it.

**The key insight:** Money is created by keystrokes. Money is deleted by reverse keystrokes. Debt is an illusion at the sovereign level.

##### **Step 6: Treasury Spends**

The Fed processes every payment when the government pays contractors, employees, or Social Security recipients:

1. Treasury instructs the Fed to make payment
2. Fed debits the Treasury account
3. Fed credits the recipient’s bank’s **reserve** account
4. The bank credits the recipient’s **deposit** account

**Now the money is “in the game.”** It’s circulating in the economy.

#### **The “National Debt”**

What we call the national debt is simply the accumulation of all those bonds: time-locked savings accounts at the Fed.

It’s not debt in the household sense. It’s the record of:

- How much permanent credit the government has created
- Minus how much it has taxed back
- Held in interest-bearing form by bondholders

The
&#x20;government pays interest on these bonds. But the government creates the
&#x20;money to pay the interest. It’s a circular flow designed to provide&#x20;
guaranteed income to bondholders: wealthy individuals, funds, banks, and
&#x20;foreign governments.

##### **Why Don’t Nations Save Their Own Currency?**

A
&#x20;government that creates dollars doesn’t need to save dollars. That&#x20;
would be like the Monopoly bank “saving” money in separate piles.

Real savings for a nation would be:

- Real capital (infrastructure, technology, educated workforce)
- Foreign currency reserves (for international trade)
- Strategic resources and other assets

But
&#x20;saving your own currency, numbers that you create on demand, is beyond&#x20;
absurd. Yet we’re told governments must “save” or “borrow” to spend. The
&#x20;theatrical performance continues.

#### **Reserves: The Base Money**

When the Fed credits the Treasury account and then settles Treasury payments, it creates what it calls **reserves**: the base layer of money in the system.

Reserves are 100% digital numbers:

- Held only by banks (individuals cannot hold reserves)
- Used to settle payments between banks
- Created and deleted by the Fed
- Permanent credit (until taxed back or drained through bond sales)

Nations
&#x20;that issue their own currency cannot “run out of money”. The constraint
&#x20;is never money… it’s real resources, inflation, and political will. But
&#x20;never money itself because numbers are infinite.

Taxes don’t fund
&#x20;government spending. This is perhaps the hardest myth to kill. Taxes&#x20;
regulate demand, create a need for the currency, and reclaim some of&#x20;
what was created. The government creates money first, then taxes it&#x20;
back. Not the other way around.

This would be obvious if we&#x20;
thought about it for ten seconds. But we don’t. We prefer the household&#x20;
budget metaphor. It’s comforting. It’s wrong. But it’s comforting.

Reserves are "base" money. Next: how temporary credit — bank money — is created on top of this permanent base.

### How Banks Create Money

Most money creation has been legally mandated to private banks.

Here’s
&#x20;how it works: You want a $300,000 mortgage. You go to a bank. The bank&#x20;
does not check its vault. It does not gather deposits. It does not call&#x20;
other customers to see if they’d like to lend you their savings.

Instead, the bank opens a spreadsheet. It types “$300,000” next to your name.

That’s it.

New money now exists. Money that, thirty seconds earlier, did not exist anywhere in the universe.

The
&#x20;bank created a liability (your deposit), which you’ll use to buy a&#x20;
house. And it created an asset (your contract to repay), which it&#x20;
considers valuable enough to hold on its books. This is called double&#x20;
entry bookkeeping.

The bank created this money at zero cost. It&#x20;
gave up nothing. It sacrificed nothing. It produces purchasing power out
&#x20;of nothing, and now you owe it back… with interest.

They call this lending, but it’s really alchemy with collateral.

When
&#x20;people say “banks lend out deposits,” they are describing a fairy tale.
&#x20;Banks create deposits when they make loans. The loans come first. The&#x20;
deposits follow.

The Bank of England confirmed this in 2014.
&#x20;The Bundesbank confirmed it. The Federal Reserve has confirmed it.&#x20;
Every major central bank now admits: **banks create money when they lend.**

And yet, somehow, the myth persists. Perhaps because the truth is too unsettling.

#### The Power Worth Fighting For

This power to create credit—who controls it and under what terms—has always been worth fighting for.

The American Revolution was sparked by exactly this issue.

In
&#x20;1764, the British Crown moved to choke colonial credit. The Currency&#x20;
Act stripped the colonies’ paper money of its status as legal tender,&#x20;
forcing colonists to conduct more and more of their trade in gold coin&#x20;
which was scarce, taxed away, and shipped back to Britain.

The&#x20;
colonial economy contracted sharply. Not because the colonists became&#x20;
lazy or incompetent, but because they’d been cut off from the means of&#x20;
exchange. Credit dried up. Trade slowed. Prosperity turned to hardship.

The
&#x20;famous Boston Tea Party was about the East India Co’s monopoly and the&#x20;
principle of taxation without representation. But it didn’t happen in a&#x20;
vacuum: colonists were also living through years of monetary squeeze,&#x20;
cut off from issuing the credit their economy ran on. Benjamin Franklin&#x20;
had spent years arguing that the colonies’ own paper money was the&#x20;
engine of their prosperity, and he fought the Currency Act directly.

The
&#x20;famous Boston Tea Party wasn’t just about tea, it was about taxes&#x20;
draining away the gold they needed to trade, while being forbidden from&#x20;
creating their own credit to replace it. Benjamin Franklin later wrote&#x20;
that the restriction on colonial credit was the deeper cause of the&#x20;
Revolution.

The Revolution was many things. But economically, it was a war over who gets to create credit.

To
&#x20;win the war, the colonists borrowed gold from France and crucially&#x20;
began issuing their own credit again called Continental currency. They&#x20;
seized back the power to create money.

That war never really&#x20;
ended. It just moved from battlefields to political bank charters, from&#x20;
kings to central banks, from overt control to legal systemic design.

The
&#x20;question remains: who should have the power to create the credit that&#x20;
mobilizes society’s real resources and either enables life or restricts&#x20;
it?

Right now, the international banking cartels have captured that power. And as we’ll see, that choice has consequences.

#### Let’s Start a Bank

To understand what banks really are, let’s become one.

But
&#x20;first, a comparison. Because understanding what makes banks special&#x20;
requires seeing what normal companies do and what banks do differently.

**If You Start a Normal Company:** You
&#x20;file paperwork to get a tax ID and become a legal person who can open a
&#x20;bank account. After you raise capital you can hire people, buy things,&#x20;
and sell things. Now you’re playing the great game of capitalism.

**If You Want to Start a Bank:** Completely different rules.

But
&#x20;before we walk through the steps, here’s something important to&#x20;
understand: our bank operates as two separate businesses under one roof.

**Business #1: Credit Creation (Making Money)**
&#x20;When the bank issues a loan, it does not transfer existing deposits. It
&#x20;creates a new deposit. The money did not previously exist. We grow the&#x20;
money supply.

**Business #2: Custody & Settlement (Moving Money)**
&#x20;The bank holds deposits, processes payments, clears checks, settles&#x20;
transfers between banks, and manages reserves. We manage existing money&#x20;
as a financial utility.

The first business manufactures&#x20;
money. The second business moves it. They operate together, under the&#x20;
same roof, but they are fundamentally different powers.

##### **Step 1: Get a Charter**

You
&#x20;need a banking charter. Not a business license. Regulators want to&#x20;
know: Do you have enough capital? Do you understand risk management? Can
&#x20;you operate safely inside the payments system?

If approved, you’re in. You’re now a member of the Federal Reserve System.

##### **Step 2: Raise Capital**

Let’s
&#x20;say we need $1 billion. Investors put up real money. That billion&#x20;
becomes our bank’s capital, our equity, our skin in the game.

Here’s where it gets interesting.

##### **Step 3: Join the Fed (Central Bank)**

With
&#x20;our charter and capital, we must now purchase stock in one of twelve&#x20;
regional Federal Reserve Banks. If we’re in New York, we buy into the&#x20;
New York Fed. San Francisco? The San Francisco Fed.

This isn’t optional. It’s required. We pay in 3% of our capital to own shares in the regional Fed, with another 3% on call.

What
&#x20;do we get for this? On that paid-in capital stock, the Fed pays a&#x20;
statutory dividend (historically 6%, now capped at the lesser of 6% or&#x20;
the 10-year Treasury rate for the largest banks). It’s not tradeable&#x20;
stock. It doesn’t control the Fed directly. But it is a guaranteed&#x20;
return on capital.

##### **Step 4: Open Our Reserve Account**

With
&#x20;our charter and capital in place, we deposit our billion dollars at the
&#x20;Fed. This creates our reserve account, which is really just a savings&#x20;
account at the Federal Reserve.

System-wide, the banking system&#x20;
holds reserve balances at the Fed that earn interest. Those reserves&#x20;
exist because deposits exist — they’re the settlement layer underneath&#x20;
all bank transactions. And the Fed pays interest on those reserve&#x20;
balances. Currently around 3.65% (but as high as 5.4% in 2024).

Think about that. You don’t receive interest on the public’s reserve balances. Banks do.

How
&#x20;much do we pay you, our customer? As little as possible, of course.&#x20;
Presently less than 1%. Why can we get away with this? Because&#x20;
regulation is largely written by the industry, enforced by former&#x20;
insiders, and structured around bank profitability. Who do you think&#x20;
shaped all the rules on banking in most countries?

##### **Step 5: Get Deposit Insurance**

Before
&#x20;we can accept customer deposits, we buy insurance from the FDIC at less
&#x20;than 0.1% of our deposits. The FDIC guarantees that if we lose more&#x20;
than our capital can cover, depositors won’t lose their savings.

This
&#x20;is the foundation of public trust. Customers don’t worry about whether&#x20;
their money is safe. They assume it is. The FDIC made that assumption&#x20;
permanent.

##### **Step 6: Operating the Bank**

We&#x20;
pay our own salaries, bills, and dividends the way any company does, out
&#x20;of our deposits. The plumbing is the same whether it’s us paying&#x20;
someone or one of our customers paying someone. When the recipient banks
&#x20;with us, the money never leaves the building; we just credit their&#x20;
account. When they bank elsewhere, we settle with that other bank, and&#x20;
only the day’s net moves in reserves.

When a customer wants&#x20;
physical cash, we swap our reserves (earning 3.65% at the Fed) for bills
&#x20;and coins (earning nothing). Cash just sits in our ATMs or vault, dead&#x20;
weight.

This is why banks restrict cash withdrawals and require&#x20;
advance notice for large transactions. It’s not about security. It’s&#x20;
about interest. When your money sits as reserves at the Fed, we profit.&#x20;
When you take it as cash, we don’t.

Excess cash? Swapped back to&#x20;
the Fed for reserves immediately. The system is designed to keep your&#x20;
money digital, where it earns us passive income. Much of the proposed&#x20;
stablecoin legislation would require issuers to back their tokens with&#x20;
cash and short-term Treasuries, much of it held through the banking&#x20;
system. It’s marketed as “safety,” but look closer: it’s another&#x20;
mechanism to push more deposits towards the banks, earning us more&#x20;
interest on reserves.

##### **Step 7: The Magic Part**

But here’s what makes banking special:

**We don’t need to use deposits to make loans.**

We
&#x20;can make roughly $20 billion in loans against that $1 billion in&#x20;
capital; the exact figure depends on how risky those loans are.

How? We create new deposits. We don’t lend out existing money. We create it.

A customer wants a $500,000 mortgage. We simply type “$500,000” into the customer’s account. New money. Created by us.

On our books:

- Asset: $500,000 mortgage contract (the customer’s promise to repay)
- Liability: $500,000 deposit (the customer’s account balance)

The
&#x20;customer buys the house. That $500,000 moves to another bank. Our&#x20;
reserves drop by $500,000 to settle the payment. But we still have the&#x20;
$500,000 mortgage as an asset, and the customer still owes us $500,000&#x20;
plus interest.

**And here’s the crucial distinction: we&#x20;
created that $500,000 deposit out of nothing. But we cannot create&#x20;
reserves. Only the Fed can do that.**

If we run low on&#x20;
reserves, we borrow them overnight from other banks (through the federal
&#x20;funds market) or from the Fed itself. Reserves are just for settling&#x20;
payments between banks. They’re not the constraint on lending.

The
&#x20;constraint is capital: our required equity relative to the loans we&#x20;
make. As long as we maintain proper capital ratios as per the Bank for&#x20;
International Settlements (BIS.org), we can keep creating new money&#x20;
through lending.

##### **Step 8: The Returns**

So let’s tally what we get for our $1 billion:

1. **6% guaranteed dividend** from our Fed stock ownership
2. **Interest on reserves**: roughly 3.65% from the Fed
3. **Interest on loans:** typically 5-8% on the $20 billion in loans we can create
4. **Fees:** loan fees, overdraft fees, account fees, wire transfer fees, ATM fees

The
&#x20;returns can be extraordinary. We earn a dividend on the capital we were
&#x20;required to invest, interest on the reserves our deposits generate, and
&#x20;interest on the new money we created from nothing. The original billion
&#x20;just sits there, quietly collecting.

Of course, we also carry&#x20;
real costs and real risk. We pay interest to depositors. Some of our&#x20;
loans go bad. We answer to capital rules and compliance regimes. And if&#x20;
we manage any of it badly enough, we fail; banks do, all the time. But&#x20;
here’s the asymmetry that actually matters: when our bets pay off, the&#x20;
gains are ours. When they go catastrophically wrong, the public catches&#x20;
the fall, through deposit insurance, emergency lending, and bailouts.&#x20;
The upside is private. The downside gets socialized.

#### **The System**

Now
&#x20;multiply this by every bank in America. Thousands of them. All creating
&#x20;money through lending. All collecting interest on reserves. All&#x20;
receiving guaranteed dividends.

And the Fed itself? It holds&#x20;
trillions in government bonds, purchased with money it created. It&#x20;
collects interest from the U.S. government on those bonds. It pays&#x20;
interest to banks. It pays dividends to member banks. And then, if&#x20;
there’s anything left, it sends the remainder to the Treasury.

Since
&#x20;2022, there’s been nothing left. Interest rates rose. The Fed now pays&#x20;
banks more in interest than it earns from its bond holdings. Instead of&#x20;
going bankrupt, it books a “deferred asset” (an accounting IOU to&#x20;
itself) and continues operating.

***An institution that creates money cannot go broke. It can only create more money.***

Now
&#x20;you understand what a bank is. It’s not a building or a vault. It’s a&#x20;
license to create money; a public power, exercised by private&#x20;
institutions, and backstopped by the public when it fails.

#### The Price of Everything

Here’s something most people never think about:

**Every price you pay contains layers of tribute to ownership positions.**

When you buy a loaf of bread, you’re not just paying for wheat, labor, and energy. You’re paying:

- **Interest** on the bakery’s business loan
- **Rent** on the bakery’s leased property
- **Interest** on the farmer’s equipment loans
- **Rent** on the farmland (to absentee landowners)
- **Royalties** on patented seeds (to agribusiness monopolies)
- **The resource toll** on every input extracted from land owned by someone who did nothing but hold title
- **Interest** on the trucking company’s vehicle financing
- Executive salaries, bonuses, travel, dining, club memberships, and corporate perks at every company in the chain
- **Profit** to shareholders at every stage of the supply chain
- **Taxes** embedded in every business cost, marked up and passed straight to you

At every single stage of production, someone is extracting value simply for *owning*
&#x20;something: not for doing anything, not for creating anything, but for&#x20;
having captured a strategic position in the production chain.

These aren’t market prices reflecting real costs. These are **tribute payments** embedded in the price structure itself.

And what made all of this extraction possible? The power to create credit and the choice to issue it only as debt.

At every stage, someone created money as debt and now extracts tribute forever.

T**he debt IS the money.** The system doesn’t just allow extraction... it requires it! The tribute is baked into every transaction.

You’re
&#x20;not buying bread. You’re paying interest to banks and rent to a dozen&#x20;

transfers.

#### The Mathematical Trap

When
&#x20;a bank creates $100,000 for your loan at 5% interest, it creates&#x20;
$100,000 in new deposits. But you owe $105,000. Where does the extra&#x20;
$5,000 come from?

Not from this loan.

It comes from the rest
&#x20;of the money already circulating: other people’s loans, government&#x20;
credit spending, and the bank’s own interest income flowing back out as&#x20;
wages, dividends, and spending, where borrowers can earn it and hand it&#x20;
back. The same dollars get spent and re-earned many times; the interest&#x20;
is a flow moving through the system.

And so, the system as a whole
&#x20;becomes structurally dependent on continuous credit expansion, and it&#x20;
steadily channels income toward whoever owns the debt. 

When&#x20;
credit stops expanding, servicing the existing debt gets harder across&#x20;
the board, and the strain shows up as rising defaults, fire-sales, and&#x20;
recession.

When you repay a loan, the principal disappears. The&#x20;
bank’s asset and its liability both vanish. Money is destroyed. But the&#x20;
interest you paid? That stays. That’s the bank’s profit. That’s real.

So
&#x20;the money supply shrinks every time loans are repaid… unless new loans&#x20;
are made to replace them. Scarcity is not accidental. It is engineered&#x20;
into the base code of the system.

The Fed’s stated inflation target isn’t zero, and it certainly isn’t negative.

It’s 2%.

That
&#x20;floor exists because a debt-based system can’t survive flat prices, let
&#x20;alone falling ones: when nominal claims stay fixed and wages fall,&#x20;
debts get heavier in real terms and defaults cascade.

Galbraith&#x20;
called this “the innocent fraud.” Innocent because everyone participates
&#x20;without realizing what they’re doing. Fraud because it is, nonetheless,
&#x20;a fraud.

&#x20;because scarcity narratives prevent redistribution. If the government&#x20;

&#x20;everyone believes money is scarce, any government spending becomes a&#x20;

&#x20;it’s deeper than that. When government spending employs people, it&#x20;

Rising wages mean workers capture more of the economic pie. But&#x20;
wealth-holders don’t just want to be rich, they want to be *relatively* richer than workers. Scarcity narratives prevent that shift.

**Creditors benefit**
&#x20;because debt must be repaid in full, while inflation erodes its value.&#x20;
Creditors prefer tight money, high interest rates, austerity, and&#x20;
unemployment. Anything that protects their nominal debt claims.

**Workers lose**
&#x20;because unemployment is deliberately maintained to prevent wage growth.
&#x20;The Fed raises rates explicitly to slow hiring. They say this openly.&#x20;
“Cooling the labor market” means “keeping workers desperate.”

**Debtors lose** because they pay interest on money created at zero cost. The asymmetry is total.

This is not a moral judgment. This is description. The system works exactly as designed. For some people.

#### The Inflation Bogeyman

*“But won’t printing money cause inflation?”*

Maybe. Maybe not. Depends.

Japan
&#x20;ran massive deficits for three decades. Result? Deflation. The U.S.&#x20;
tripled the monetary base after 2008. Result? Barely any inflation. The&#x20;
Fed created trillions during COVID. Result? Inflation, yes, but mostly&#x20;
in assets that rich people own and in sectors suffering supply chain&#x20;
disruptions.

Weimar Germany and Zimbabwe had hyperinflation. But&#x20;
they also had collapsed productive capacity, political chaos, and&#x20;
foreign-denominated debts.

**Inflation happens when money creation exceeds productive capacity.** That’s the rule.

If
&#x20;you create money and use it to employ idle workers building useful&#x20;
things, you get more goods and more money with no inflation. If you&#x20;
create money and people bid for the same limited supply of goods, prices
&#x20;rise.

Bank lending creates inflation when it flows into housing&#x20;
speculation and stock buybacks. Public spending creates value when it&#x20;
flows into infrastructure and education.

Hyperinflation requires&#x20;
systemic collapse, not just money creation. Yet we’re told to fear&#x20;
public investment while ignoring private speculation.

Why? Because the people warning about inflation benefit from scarcity.

#### Modern Monetary Theory (MMT)

Some economists have formalized this understanding into MMT. It observes:

1. Currency-issuing governments cannot run out of their own money
2. Taxes don’t fund spending—they regulate inflation and demand for currency
3. The real limit is inflation, not deficits
4. Unemployment is a policy choice, not economic necessity
5. Bond issuance is about interest rate management, not financing

Critics call this radical. It’s not. It’s description.

MMT
&#x20;doesn’t advocate printing unlimited money. It advocates understanding&#x20;
how money actually works, so we can have honest debates about real&#x20;
resource constraints instead of fake debates about accounting.

The radical thing isn’t MMT. The radical thing is pretending the current system is natural, inevitable, or fair.

austerity. “We can’t afford it” cuts education, healthcare,&#x20;

It’s not a bug. It’s the core feature.

&#x20;our current system, scaled coordination to billions. Banks gained the&#x20;

&#x20;centuries, was always self-terminating; it just took this long to reach
&#x20;its end.

&#x20;hold it. The ledger belongs to everyone. And for the first time, credit
&#x20;can flow directly between people, issued by communities for real&#x20;
purposes, deleted when spent, and all while **accountable to ecological limits**.

Coin

self-terminating terminate. The question is whether we consciously&#x20;
design what comes next... or stumble into it.

And for the first time in human history, we have the tools to build a different one.

Parts
&#x20;1–3 traced how we got here: ownership captured the commons, banks&#x20;

possible when neither capture holds. This part answers the question&#x20;
every serious person eventually asks about any good idea:

**If this is possible, why hasn’t it already happened?**

The

answer is the most important thing to understand about the moment we’re&#x20;
in.

### **Centuries of Trying**

In
&#x20;every generation there are those of us who question the mores of&#x20;
ownership, who resist the saddles of debt and imagine more worthy&#x20;
systems. We build cooperatives, communes, mutual banks, worker councils,
&#x20;free towns, gift economies, commons trusts. Many work beautifully — for
&#x20;a while, at a certain scale, inside a protective pocket of the world.

And
&#x20;yet, most attempts eventually run into the same wall. The Paris Commune
&#x20;lasted 72 days. The great mutualist movements of the 19th century were&#x20;
absorbed or crushed. Experiments in decentralized credit, communal land,
&#x20;or worker-owned production often find themselves forced to operate&#x20;
within the gravity of the old ledger... or harden back into the same&#x20;
hierarchy they set out to replace.

Their successes are significant. Their reach is foreshortened.

Not
&#x20;for lack of vision, or courage, or intelligence. Those are never in&#x20;
short supply. The reach of these experiments is limited by the walls of a
&#x20;container too small for our hearts. They can extend only so far as our&#x20;
available forms of dependence allow. Our structures of support and our&#x20;
tokens of exchange limit and goad. We have been centered on centers — of
&#x20;decision, power, money, and resource — rather than centered in one&#x20;
another.

Our ideas are not wrong. Something more fundamental is missing.

### **Every Civilization Is Built on Its Trust Layer**

Before
&#x20;we can answer why Creditism couldn’t exist before, we need a deeper&#x20;
frame: every civilization in history has been shaped — more than it knew
&#x20;— by the *technologies of trust* available to it.

Large-scale
&#x20;human cooperation has always depended on shared systems of record and&#x20;
belief — structures that allow millions of strangers to coordinate as if
&#x20;they know one another.

In oral cultures, trust was organized&#x20;
around kinship and reputation, and extended only as far as memory could&#x20;
reach. The invention of writing let trust scale across distance and&#x20;
time… but only as far as the scribes and priests who controlled the&#x20;
texts. The printing press decentralized the written word and broke&#x20;
religious organizations’ monopolies on truth. The internet decentralized
&#x20;communication and modified the gatekeeping of who got to speak.

Each
&#x20;of these shifts didn’t just change technology. It changed what kinds of
&#x20;power were possible, what kinds of institutions made sense, and what&#x20;
kinds of coordination became available to ordinary people.

And
&#x20;they did not arrive gradually. They arrived as phase shifts. Nobel&#x20;
laureate Ilya Prigogine, studying complex systems far from equilibrium,&#x20;
showed that such transitions are not incremental: they are sudden,&#x20;
nonlinear, and irreversible. Systems don’t improve. They *reorganize*.
&#x20;Writing didn’t improve oral culture; it reconfigured the human&#x20;
landscape, moving the seat of memory from the person to the page. The&#x20;
printing press didn’t reform religious institutions; it undermined a&#x20;
source of their power.

We stand at the doorway of one of these phase shifts now.

When
&#x20;the trust layer shifts, the power structures built on top of the old&#x20;
one don’t just weaken, they lose their justifications. Banks exist&#x20;
because someone had to maintain the ledger. Governments enforce&#x20;
contracts because without them, agreements couldn’t be reliably upheld&#x20;
at scale. Those functions are no longer exclusively theirs. The *monopoly* on trust that justified their control over economic life is dissolving — not overnight and not painlessly. Structurally.

Consider
&#x20;what our current trust layer actually requires at scale: millions of&#x20;
people whose entire working lives are spent managing the friction of a&#x20;
system that struggles to coordinate itself without them — calculating&#x20;
risk, denying claims, auditing compliance, enforcing debt, defending&#x20;
enclosures. Not because these people lack talent to do something else&#x20;
nearer their hearts, but because our architectures demand this of them.

When the trust layer shifts, that entire apparatus dissolves.

And into that space steps something new.

### **Money Isn’t the Point. Memory Is.**

Economists
&#x20;tell us scarcity is the fundamental problem. There isn’t enough, so we&#x20;
must ration, compete, and build systems to decide who gets what.

Scarcity is real: of energy, materials, time, attention. But scarcity is not the *binding* constraint
&#x20;on human coordination. The planet is not running short on sunlight,&#x20;
intelligence, creativity, or human care. A handful of people with modern
&#x20;tools can outproduce entire villages from a century ago. The productive
&#x20;capacity of civilization is extraordinary and growing.

The binding constraint is something else entirely.

**It is the technologies of trust.**

Money, despite what textbooks say, is not just a store of value or a medium of exchange. A monetary system is a *social memory system*
&#x20;encoding which kinds of actions are defined as valuable. It records who
&#x20;contributed what, and what claim that contribution earns on future&#x20;
production. Anthropologists have consistently shown this: money began as
&#x20;a ledger of obligations, not a pile of coins. Strip away ideologies and
&#x20;money is a single sentence: *this person contributed something we define as valuable, and they can claim something back.*

The
&#x20;problem is that for most of history, maintaining that ledger reliably&#x20;
required trusted intermediaries. Kings minted coins. Banks kept&#x20;
accounts. Governments enforced contracts. Armies made sure we couldn’t&#x20;
opt out. But paper records are only as reliable as the people who&#x20;
control them. Records were altered. Debts were often forgiven for the&#x20;
powerful and brutally enforced for the poor. Currencies collapsed. Banks
&#x20;failed. Entire economic systems periodically imploded when the trust&#x20;
layer broke.

Some of this was abuse. Some of it was the system&#x20;
solving for the only thing it could: stability through hierarchy. Often&#x20;
it was both at once. In the absence of distributed trust, centralization
&#x20;wasn’t merely a conspiracy; it was a survival strategy that also became
&#x20;a stranglehold.

**Whoever controls the ledger controls the economy.**

So
&#x20;economies evolved around limiting the supply of credit — partly because
&#x20;coordination at scale was genuinely hard, and partly because access to&#x20;
the record system became a tool of control. Scarcity is sold as&#x20;
stability, because for most of civilized life, a version of that&#x20;
scarcity was structurally real. Inflation is the monster. Deficits are&#x20;
moral failures. Debt is discipline.

But strip away the rhetoric and the logic underneath is brutally simple: *control what people need to live, and you own them.*
&#x20;Economists call it “participation in the economy.” The rest of us call&#x20;
it living. And the people most forcefully enforcing those beliefs tend&#x20;
to be, disproportionately, the people who benefit from them.

This
&#x20;is the paradox at the heart of modern life. A world of extraordinary&#x20;
productive capacity, organized around a ledger system that no longer&#x20;
needs to be scarce — but is kept that way because the technology to&#x20;
coordinate differently did not yet exist.

Until now.

### **The Missing Technology**

For
&#x20;most of recorded history, the dominant ways of coordinating at scale&#x20;
settled into one of two shapes. We could centralize the ledger — trust a
&#x20;king, a bank, a church, or a government — and accept that whoever held&#x20;
the pen could change what it said. Or we could stay local — small enough
&#x20;that reputation enforced the record — and accept that cooperation would
&#x20;stop at the edge of that circle.

There were always exceptions.&#x20;
Federations like the Haudenosaunee Confederacy coordinated across&#x20;
nations without a single sovereign. Guilds, trade networks, and&#x20;
Indigenous governance systems found ways to extend cooperation through&#x20;
layered trust and shared protocols. Göbekli Tepe suggests large-scale&#x20;
cooperation predates the very idea of the state. These weren’t&#x20;
anomalies. They were proof that the binary was never total.

But the *dominant*
&#x20;pattern — the one that scaled into empires, into markets, into the&#x20;
modern global economy — hardened into that binary; into a moloch.&#x20;
Centralize, or stay small. And every fairer alternative that tries to&#x20;
scale beyond its original community eventually hits the same structural&#x20;
wall: the moment cooperation outruns mutual recognition, someone has to&#x20;
take control of the record. And the moment someone controls the record,&#x20;
the system bends around them. Almost every time.

That’s the wall.

And the wall just broke.

Not gradually. Not philosophically. Technically.

Over
&#x20;the past two decades, something qualitatively new has emerged: the&#x20;
ability to maintain shared records that are distributed, transparent,&#x20;
and resistant to unilateral alteration. No king. No bank. No central&#x20;
authority holding the pen. Blockchain architectures introduced the first
&#x20;working proof. Newer agent-centric approaches like Holochain — where&#x20;
each participant carries their own ledger and integrity is validated&#x20;
peer-to-peer rather than through a single global chain — point toward&#x20;
designs that scale without the energy costs or consensus bottlenecks of&#x20;
earlier systems.

Together, they open a third option that simply did not exist before.

Not perfect trust. **Verifiable coordination without a single controlling authority.**

This
&#x20;is not an incremental improvement. It is the dissolution of a&#x20;
constraint that shaped nearly every civilization that tried to scale.&#x20;
Remove the constraint, and the institutions built on top of it lose&#x20;
their load-bearing justifications. Banks don’t disappear because people&#x20;
vote them out. They disappear the same way scribes lost power when the&#x20;
printing press arrived: the function stops requiring them.

None
&#x20;of this eliminates the trust problem. Distributed systems can still be&#x20;
gamed. Incentives can still be corrupted. Governance can still be&#x20;
captured. What changes is *where the failures live*,
&#x20;and therefore what can be done about them. Instead of being hidden&#x20;
inside institutions where they are invisible, they become visible,&#x20;
contestable, and redesignable at the protocol level. Trust stops being a
&#x20;fixed constraint that forces us toward centralized control. It becomes a
&#x20;design space.

For the first time, a third option becomes buildable at planetary scale.

And once you see it, you can’t unsee it.

### **The Trust Layer Is Being Rebuilt — Either Way**

The
&#x20;old trust layer is not holding. It cannot hold. The institutions built&#x20;
on it were designed for a world where centralized record-keeping was the
&#x20;only option, and that world is ending. What replaces it is being&#x20;
constructed right now — in central banks, in corporate boardrooms, in&#x20;
legislative committees, in open-source repositories, in founding&#x20;
circles. All at once. All in competition for the same foundation.

The question isn’t whether the ledger gets rebuilt. It is being rebuilt.

The only question is: **by whom, and on whose terms.**

That
&#x20;is what makes this moment structurally different from every prior&#x20;
reform attempt. Past movements fought for fairer allocation within a&#x20;
ledger someone else owned. This time, the ledger itself is up for grabs.
&#x20;The foundation of the next century of economic life is being poured&#x20;
now, while most people are still arguing about the last one.

And poured concrete sets.

Systems scientists have a name for moments like this: a *bifurcation point*.
&#x20;A phase where a system has lost its old stability but has not yet&#x20;
settled into its new one. At maximum instability, the system is at&#x20;
maximum sensitivity. Small coherent acts — building, organizing,&#x20;
participating, seeding patterns — carry structural weight they simply do
&#x20;not carry during stable periods. Not because individuals become more&#x20;
powerful. Because the architecture of what comes next is still soft. It&#x20;
is still being written. For a short window, the pen is actually shared.

That window is now. It will not stay open indefinitely. Once the new ledger hardens, it hardens for a generation.

This
&#x20;is not an essay about an idea you might someday act on. It is a&#x20;
description of a construction site. You are inside it. So is everyone&#x20;
else. The only variable is what gets built, and who holds the pen.

### **The Fork**

Two paths are live. Both are already being built.

**Path One: Extraction, perfected.**

The
&#x20;same institutions that captured the last ledger are racing to build the
&#x20;next one. Central banks are piloting or launching digital currencies in
&#x20;more than 130 countries. The Bank for International Settlements — the&#x20;
central bank of central banks — is coordinating the architecture.&#x20;
Treasury departments, finance ministries, and legislators are writing&#x20;
rules that route the new digital infrastructure back through licensed&#x20;
banks, so every transaction on the next ledger generates interest income
&#x20;for the same institutions that ran the last one.

The language is new. The control is not.

The technology changes. The pen stays in the same hands.

Programmable
&#x20;money that can be frozen, restricted by category, or expire on schedule
&#x20;is already operational. Social credit systems — sometimes explicit,&#x20;
more often embedded in payment processors, platform bans, and debanking —
&#x20;already determine who can participate in the digital economy. Tokenized
&#x20;real-world assets are pulling land, housing, and natural resources onto
&#x20;programmable rails owned by private issuers. The word&#x20;
“decentralization” is being loudly attached to products that centralize&#x20;
everything that matters.

Better technology. Same game. New masks on familiar faces.

And it is not holding the world together. Researchers at Cambridge have formally defined what’s visible in real time as a *polycrisis*:
&#x20;the causal entanglement of breakdowns across multiple global systems&#x20;
simultaneously, each amplifying the others. Governance, energy,&#x20;
information, economics, and ecology are failing in sync, overwhelming&#x20;
institutions designed to handle any one of them in isolation. The World&#x20;
Economic Forum’s survey of more than a thousand global experts found&#x20;
that fewer than one in ten expect a return to the previous rules-based&#x20;
international order.

The trust layer isn’t theoretically&#x20;
weakening. It’s measurably fracturing. And the people rebuilding it are,
&#x20;overwhelmingly, the people who broke it.

**Path Two: The Ledger as Commons.**

The
&#x20;ledger is treated as shared infrastructure rather than a private asset.
&#x20;Access emerges from participation, not permission. Rules are&#x20;
transparent by default. Credit flows as recognition of contribution —&#x20;
not as debt issued downward from institutions that charge you for the&#x20;
privilege of existing in the economy.

This path is harder. It is&#x20;
also not guaranteed. It requires solving problems that centralized&#x20;
systems avoided by design: identity without a passport office,&#x20;
coordination without coercion, governance without a hierarchy to&#x20;
capture. Most attempts will fail. Many will quietly recreate the&#x20;
structures they set out to replace, and won’t notice until it’s too&#x20;
late. Some will be captured by the very forces they were built to&#x20;
escape.

That fragility is not a weakness in the argument. It is&#x20;
proof that the argument is real. Open systems don’t win because they are
&#x20;morally prettier. They win only if they are actually built, defended,&#x20;
and governed well enough to hold.

But what Elinor Ostrom demonstrated at small scales — that commons can be governed without centralized control, and governed *well*
&#x20;— is now becoming architecturally possible at planetary scale. Her&#x20;
Nobel work dismantled the assumption that shared resources inevitably&#x20;
collapse without private ownership or state enforcement. She showed the&#x20;
opposite: that communities, given the right design principles,&#x20;
consistently outperform both market and state solutions. What was once&#x20;
field research is now a design brief.

### **Identity Without a Passport Office**

Every piece of the Creditism architecture depends on something that simply didn’t exist until now: **verifiable record-keeping you can trust without trusting the people running it.**

Without
&#x20;that, we cannot run a flow currency created when earned and deleted&#x20;
when spent. We cannot guarantee every person on Earth a Personal Credit&#x20;
flow without fraud. We cannot democratize governance over the commons&#x20;
without the result being rewritten by whoever shows up with more lawyers
&#x20;and more capital.

Prior attempts at fairer systems kept hitting the wall of scalable trust. Not ideology. Engineering.

That’s what makes Creditism an *infinite* game: any group of people can start it, the rules evolve by mutual practice, and no single player can own the board.

The identity problem — *how do you know who’s a person without a passport office?*
&#x20;— was one of the last load-bearing obstacles. A flow of credit that&#x20;
reaches every human is only as fair as its ability to reach exactly one&#x20;
account per human. Without that, the system breaks through arithmetic,&#x20;
not ideology. A thousand fake accounts drain a thousand real people’s&#x20;
share.

New approaches are now emerging: decentralized&#x20;
identity protocols built not on institutional certification, but on&#x20;
networks of real people recognizing one another — with transparent,&#x20;
auditable mechanisms for evaluating those connections over time. Not a&#x20;
single source of truth. A living web of mutual recognition that no&#x20;
single actor can forge or control.

This is a quiet inversion of&#x20;
something very old. For most of modern life, your personhood in the&#x20;
system has been something granted from above — by a government that&#x20;
issues the document, by a bureau that validates the number, by an&#x20;
institution that decides whether your identity counts. You are a person&#x20;
because the state says so, and only on the terms the state sets.

What replaces that is not a better database. It is a different answer to the question of what makes a person countable.

> **You aren’t a person because a government database says so.** **You are a person because other people — real, verified participants in the same network — recognize you as one.**
>
> — Adam Stallard

Each
&#x20;connection is a digital handshake. Layered across thousands of&#x20;
participants, those handshakes become something institutional ID never&#x20;
was: a fabric of recognition that scales without concentrating.&#x20;
Confirmed not by an authority looking down, but by a community looking&#x20;
sideways.

# Credit Is Democracy, Money Isn't.

### Every dollar is a vote — and the election was rigged before you were born.

[Common Planet Foundation](https://substack.com/@commonplanet), [Remzi Bajrami](https://substack.com/@remzib), and [Eli Capracotta](https://substack.com/@themillionthings)

May 22, 2026

*Part 6 of the Creditism series.*

There are two ballots in your life.

Common’s
&#x20;Substack is a reader-supported publication. To receive new posts and&#x20;
support my work, consider becoming a free or paid subscriber.

One you mark every few years for politicians who mostly don’t represent you. They count it. Then mostly ignore it.

The
&#x20;other you cast every day, every hour, with every transaction, every&#x20;
paycheck, every bill, every purchase. It counts whether you have one&#x20;
ballot or a billion of them. And the people with billion-ballot stacks&#x20;
are casting them while you sleep.

We call the first one democracy.

We call the second one the economy.

But they’re the same thing.

They’re
&#x20;both choice-making technologies. They both decide what gets built, who&#x20;
gets fed, what kind of world becomes possible. And one of them has been&#x20;
quietly determining the outcome of the other for as long as anyone alive
&#x20;can remember.

Not rigged by a single villain. Rigged by inherited rules — rules that compound power before most of us ever get to play.

In
&#x20;Parts 1 and 2 of this series, we named the game: how ownership captured
&#x20;the commons and how banks captured the ledger. In Part 3, we sketched&#x20;
the architecture of what comes next. In Part 4, we showed why it became&#x20;
buildable now. In Part 5, we walked through a day inside it.

This essay is about something simpler, stranger, and harder to unsee:

**Money is a vote. The economy is an election. And we have been losing it without ever being told it was happening.**

## **The Vote You Already Understand**

A vote is a curious object.

You
&#x20;cannot eat it. You cannot store it. You cannot inherit it. You cannot&#x20;
sell it on a secondary market. You cannot lend it out at interest.

You
&#x20;receive it because you exist as a participant in the society. You use&#x20;
it to register a choice. Once cast, it deletes. Its purpose is completed
&#x20;in the act of expressing it.

In the ideal — unevenly realized, we know — votes have a few features worth pausing on:

They are issued **equally** to participants. They are issued **regularly**, not once and forever. They are **non-transferable** — I cannot buy yours. They are **non-accumulative** — hoarding does not make them stronger. They **delete on use** — they don’t become someone else’s property. And a society **cannot run out** of them; it creates them when needed.

Now imagine, for a moment, if votes worked the way money works.

Imagine
&#x20;some people were born with ten thousand votes and others with none.&#x20;
Imagine families that had inherited millions of votes across centuries.&#x20;
Imagine a market where corporations could buy votes wholesale, package&#x20;
them into derivatives, lend them out at interest, and use them as&#x20;
collateral. Imagine politicians who openly explained, *“Some people simply worked harder for their votes.”*

We would call this rule by accumulated power. We would not call it democracy.

This is, almost exactly, how money works.

And we call it freedom.

## **Money Is Voting Power**

Money is described in textbooks as a medium of exchange, a store of value, a unit of account.

These descriptions are technically true and almost entirely beside the point.

Money
&#x20;is a choice-making unit. Every dollar you spend is a small vote on what
&#x20;the economy should produce more of, who should be employed doing it,&#x20;
what should be valued, and what direction civilization should bend. When
&#x20;you buy bread, you vote for bakeries. When millions buy coffee, they&#x20;
vote a global supply chain into existence. When trillions of dollars&#x20;
flow toward fossil fuels and real estate speculation and military&#x20;
hardware, they vote — relentlessly, every second — for that being the&#x20;
world we live in.

The problem is not that the economy listens to votes.

The problem is *whose* votes it listens to.

The
&#x20;economy does not respond to need. It responds to purchasing power. And&#x20;
because most people make economic choices under survival pressure, even&#x20;
the votes they do cast are constrained choices, not free ones — picking&#x20;
the cheap food because the good food costs too much, taking the job&#x20;
because the rent is due, signing the lease because the alternative is&#x20;
the street.

A hungry child without money casts no effective vote&#x20;
for food. A homeless family casts no effective vote for shelter. A&#x20;
teacher, nurse, farmer, caregiver, or artist may produce immense real&#x20;
value — but if that value does not translate into enough money, their&#x20;
economic voice barely registers.

Meanwhile, one billionaire can cast more economic votes in a single afternoon than millions of people will cast in a lifetime.

This
&#x20;is not metaphor. Nor rhetorical flourish. It is a plain description of&#x20;
what is structurally happening to the world every minute.

Which is
&#x20;why we get a civilization where empty homes and homeless people&#x20;
coexist. Where food is destroyed while children go hungry. Where&#x20;
brilliant minds are told there is “no money” for the work that matters.&#x20;
Where industries that produce sickness flourish while those that produce
&#x20;health are starved. Where the biosphere — the actual basis of every&#x20;
economy that has ever existed — receives essentially zero votes.

We
&#x20;vote politically as citizens. We vote economically as consumers. The&#x20;
two systems are wired together in a way no civics teacher mentioned: the
&#x20;economic vote almost always overwhelms the political one.

We vote for clean air. Fossil capital votes against it every day with billions.

We vote for affordable housing. Real estate capital votes for scarcity every day with ownership and finance.

We
&#x20;vote for peace. The military-industrial complex votes for war every&#x20;
quarter with contracts, lobbying, and institutional dependency.

Our formal democracy says: **one person, one vote.**

Our economic democracy says: **one dollar, one vote.**

And then we wonder why The People keep losing.

## **Whoever Must Play, Cannot Play**

The philosopher James Carse drew a distinction worth noting here.

Some games are *finite*.
&#x20;They have rules, players, winners, losers, an end. Chess. Football.&#x20;
Elections. Capitalism, as currently designed, behaves like a finite game
&#x20;played for ownership.

Other games are *infinite*.
&#x20;The point of an infinite game is not to win. It is to keep the game&#x20;
going. To bring more players into play. The rules can change. The&#x20;
boundaries can move. The purpose is the play itself.

Carse said something that hits harder the more you sit with it:

> *“Whoever must play, cannot play.”*

If
&#x20;you are forced to play — by debt, by hunger, by the fact that you have&#x20;
to pay for the privilege of existing on this planet — then you are not&#x20;
playing. You are being played. The freedom isn’t real. The choice isn’t&#x20;
real. The vote, in any meaningful sense, isn’t real either.

This&#x20;
is what the current economy does to most people. It is not a game we&#x20;
joined. It is a game we were enrolled in at birth, and the rules — the&#x20;
ones that determine whether we have a home, food, time, dignity, a&#x20;
future — were written long before we got here, by people who are mostly&#x20;
dead, on terms designed to compound forever — into hands like theirs.

This isn’t democracy. It isn’t even an economy in the classical sense. It is a coercion system with markets attached.

The
&#x20;first move toward an actual democracy is to make playing optional — by&#x20;
guaranteeing that the choice to participate is a real choice, not a&#x20;
survival demand.

The second move is to give everyone real ballots.

## **Personal Credit: The Ballot You Cast for Your Life**

Here is the heart of it.

Personal Credit, in Creditism, is the democratic distribution of economic choice-making units.

It
&#x20;is not welfare. It is not charity. It is not a handout from a&#x20;
benevolent state. It is the recognition of something simpler: you are a&#x20;
participant in the society. You inherited the language, the roads, the&#x20;
knowledge, the medicine, the farms, the science, the songs, the soil.&#x20;
You contribute to it just by being here. Your existence is not a debt.

Therefore:
&#x20;you receive a baseline flow of choice-making units. Every month.&#x20;
Without condition. The same way a citizen of a democracy receives a&#x20;
vote.

You use Personal Credit to access what the society produces:
&#x20;housing, food, transportation, education, care, art, leisure. You don’t
&#x20;apply. You don’t beg. You don’t justify yourself to anyone. You just&#x20;
live, the same way a citizen exercising the franchise just shows up at&#x20;
the booth.

That baseline is the floor, not the ceiling. Above it,&#x20;
contribution adds — when you do work, the system credits you for it, at a
&#x20;rate matched to the kind of work and the experience you bring. Same&#x20;
hourly rate for the same work at the same experience. But total flows&#x20;
differ from person to person, because hours and contributions differ.&#x20;
Equal categorically. Fair quantitatively. Not identical the way UBI is —
&#x20;and the difference matters.

But here is the deeper structural break: Personal Credit is designed not to behave like money currently behaves.

When you spend it, it disappears.

I know that sounds strange at first. Most people, hearing this, immediately think: *but then how does the baker get paid?* It’s a fair question, and it has a clean answer.

Picture
&#x20;a baker. She bakes ten loaves. The system credits her for the work —&#x20;
directly, in recognition of what she’s contributed. Now you walk in and&#x20;
choose a loaf. You spend some of your credit. That credit disappears. It
&#x20;doesn’t go to the baker. She doesn’t need it to — she was already&#x20;
credited. Your credit was for access. Hers was for contribution. Two&#x20;
flows, two purposes.

The baker is credited for baking. The teacher
&#x20;is credited for teaching. The builder for building. The healer for&#x20;
healing. Their work registers directly, not through a chain of&#x20;
extraction that pulls value upward through landlords, lenders,&#x20;
executives, and shareholders.

The dollar in capitalism is a chain&#x20;
that links producer and consumer through a long ladder of rent-takers.&#x20;
The credit in Creditism is a clean signal: a vote for what you need,&#x20;
expressed once, then dissolved.

### **Why Credit Must Not Become Capital**

This is the design pillar most people miss on first read, so it deserves its own beat.

If
&#x20;credits could accumulate indefinitely, they would eventually become&#x20;
capital again. People would hoard them, lend them at interest, stake&#x20;
them as collateral, wield them as leverage over other people. Within a&#x20;
generation or two, the whole grim story would re-run on different&#x20;
infrastructure. New ledger, same hierarchy, slightly nicer logo.

This is why deletion isn’t a quirky feature. Remove it and Credit becomes capital again.

A vote does not become capital because a vote *deletes when used*. That single design property is what keeps democracies from collapsing into rule by accumulated political wealth.

Credit,
&#x20;properly designed, has to delete for the same reason. It has to behave&#x20;
like a vote, not like property. The moment it can be hoarded across&#x20;
generations, it stops being democratic and starts being dynastic. Again.

Deletion is what makes Credit democratic.

## **Community Credit: The Ballot We Cast Together**

Some choices belong to us individually. What I eat for dinner. Where I want to spend an afternoon. Which book I’d rather read.

Other
&#x20;choices belong to us collectively. Whether the river gets restored.&#x20;
Whether there’s a clinic. Whether the bridge gets repaired. Whether&#x20;
children are educated. Whether elders are cared for. Whether the&#x20;
watershed is protected. Whether the air is breathable in fifty years.

These
&#x20;are not consumer choices. They are civic choices. They have always been
&#x20;civic choices. And we have, somewhere along the way, been kept from&#x20;
making them.

In the current system, collective needs are funded&#x20;
through taxation, bonds, philanthropy, and bureaucratic allocation — a&#x20;
slow, captured, often humiliating process that takes badly-distributed&#x20;
money and tries to claw some of it back to fund the basic conditions of&#x20;
civilization. We distribute terribly first, then redistribute painfully&#x20;
later. The fights over redistribution are bitter precisely because the&#x20;
original distribution was unjust.

Community Credit takes a different approach: skip step one.

Issue
&#x20;choice-making power to communities directly, in proportion to the&#x20;
people in them, for the work that holds the shared world together.&#x20;
Communities can be organized at multiple scales — neighborhood, city,&#x20;
region, network, or planetary commons — depending on what kind of&#x20;
decision is being made.

This is not government spending in the way
&#x20;we currently know it. There is no distant treasury pretending to be&#x20;
broke while asking permission from bond markets to fund a school. There&#x20;
is direct civic capacity, distributed at the scale of the decisions to&#x20;
be made.

A neighborhood receives Community Credit and decides —&#x20;
democratically, with real power — to fund a childcare cooperative, a&#x20;
community kitchen, a food forest, an elder-care circle, a repair&#x20;
workshop, a youth program. A region directs CC toward watershed&#x20;
restoration, public transit, cultural infrastructure, hospitals,&#x20;
ecological work. A planetary commons directs credit toward climate&#x20;
stabilization, biodiversity, open-source medicine, response to&#x20;
displacement.

Each person also receives a personal allocation of&#x20;
Community Credit they can direct toward whatever projects they believe&#x20;
deserve support. Civic life becomes a kind of continuous, distributed&#x20;
crowdfunding — except the crowd is everyone, and the funding is built&#x20;
in. The after-school program. The local farm. The neighborhood mediation
&#x20;team. The mutual aid network. The independent researcher. The artist&#x20;
whose work nobody else will pay for because no corporation can monetize&#x20;
it.

This is what philanthropy was always pretending to be and&#x20;
never was. Philanthropy is what happens when inequality becomes so&#x20;
extreme that society begs the winners to give some of the winnings back.
&#x20;Community Credit doesn’t beg. It gives The People civic power from the&#x20;
start.

This is democracy with a body.

Because here is something that should be obvious and somehow isn’t: laws are not the only things that govern us.

**Budgets
&#x20;govern us. Prices govern us. Debt governs us. Ownership governs us.&#x20;
Infrastructure governs us. The built world governs us.**

If
&#x20;the people only get to vote on legislation and not on the flows and&#x20;
sources of credit that build the actual world we inhabit, then democracy
&#x20;is ceremonial. A fragile ritual on top of an economic engine that&#x20;
nobody voted for.

Community Credit brings democracy into the engine.

## **The Objection That Sounds Smart**

Someone is going to say this is just Universal Basic Income with extra steps.

It isn’t.

UBI
&#x20;recognizes something true — that people need economic access to live,&#x20;
and the current system does not provide it fairly — and then leaves the&#x20;
deeper machinery intact. Give everyone money inside the same capitalist&#x20;
structure, and the gains are silently absorbed by the people who already
&#x20;own everything.

UBI gives you more chips in the same casino.

The differences with Creditism are structural, not cosmetic.

Money in capitalism is mostly created as **debt**, owed to private banks, with interest attached. Credit in Creditism is created as **recognition** — of your existence, of communal need, of your contribution.

Money in capitalism **accumulates**, becomes capital, compounds across generations into dynasties of unaccountable power. Credit in Creditism **deletes** when spent, like a vote.

Money in capitalism is **gatekept**
&#x20;by ownership positions: you need it from someone, and they extract for&#x20;
the privilege of giving it to you. Credit in Creditism flows **directly** to people and communities: as baseline access, as credit for contribution, and as civic capacity.

Money in capitalism encodes **scarcity by design**, even when material reality is one of abundance. Credit in Creditism encodes **what actually exists**: real production, real contribution, real ecological limits.

UBI
&#x20;gives people a floor in a building designed to keep raising the ceiling
&#x20;out of reach. Every time the floor goes up, the rent, the prices, and&#x20;
the debt go up faster. Creditism isn’t a higher floor. It’s a different&#x20;
building.

## **Not No Rules. No Rulers.**

Some people hear all this and reach instinctively for the chaos objection. *If everyone gets credit and there are no central banks and no taxation, surely everything falls apart.*

But democracy has never meant no structure. It has meant *legitimate* structure.

Rules
&#x20;are useful. Agreements are beautiful. Protocols help us coordinate at&#x20;
scale. Accounting helps us see clearly. Law can protect the vulnerable.&#x20;
Governance helps groups make decisions too large for face-to-face trust.

The problem is not rules. The problem is **rulers**.

The
&#x20;problem is when rules harden above the people they govern. When the&#x20;
game is mandatory and the players cannot revise it. When people are born
&#x20;into systems of debt and rent and employment and borders and&#x20;
surveillance and punishment, and then told this is freedom.

Real&#x20;
democracy means the players can change the rules. Not impulsively. Not&#x20;
destructively. Not without care. But actually. A system that cannot be&#x20;
revised by the people living inside it is not a democracy. It is a&#x20;
machine. And machines do not care who gets crushed in the gears.

Creditism
&#x20;is an attempt to make the economic rules revisable again — by the&#x20;
people they actually affect. To take the ledger back from banks and&#x20;
ownership positions. To say: if money is a technology, we can redesign&#x20;
it. If credit is a social agreement, we can reissue it differently. If&#x20;
value comes from life, life should sit at the center of the accounting.

Not capital.

**Life**.

## **What Democracy Points At**

There is something almost embarrassing about how simple this is once you see it.

Democracy
&#x20;was never supposed to stop at the ballot box. It was supposed to be a&#x20;
way of living together — a continuous practice of distributed choice. We
&#x20;just got handed a tiny version of it and were told that was the whole&#x20;
thing.

The first democracy gave people political voice.&#x20;
Incomplete, excluded, captured, corrupted — but still, a real&#x20;
breakthrough. It said kings are not inevitable. It said legitimacy comes
&#x20;from the people.

Our next democracy gives people economic voice.&#x20;
It says ownership is not inevitable. It says credit can come from The&#x20;
People. It says no one — not a king, not a bank, not a billionaire, not a
&#x20;corporation — should have the unilateral power to decide who gets to&#x20;
live.

This is what Personal Credit is doing. This is what&#x20;
Community Credit is doing. They are the missing technology of democracy —
&#x20;the part that was never built because, until very recently, it could&#x20;
not be built. (Part 4 went into why the trust infrastructure has only&#x20;
just become possible.)

Credit, properly designed, is not money. Nor merely the lubricant of commerce.

It
&#x20;is democracy in motion. A vote you can eat with. A vote you can build&#x20;
with. A vote you can care with. A vote you can use, every day, to bend&#x20;
the shared world toward Life.

## **The Game We Inherit, The Game We Could Play**

Every
&#x20;society is a game in the deepest sense. Not because it’s trivial, but&#x20;
because it’s made of rules, roles, boundaries, permissions, and shared&#x20;
beliefs about what matters.

The current game says the purpose is **accumulation**.
&#x20;Own more. Extract more. Win more. Compete harder. Grow forever. Until&#x20;
you can’t, because the world breaks under the strain — and then somehow&#x20;
it’s everyone’s fault except the rules’.

Creditism proposes a different game, one closer to what Carse called infinite. The purpose is not accumulation. The purpose is **continuation**
&#x20;— life, participation, creativity, care, regeneration, the ability to&#x20;
wake up without fear and contribute without coercion. The point is to&#x20;
keep the game going, and to bring more players in.

In that game, credit is not debt. It is trust.

Credit
&#x20;is not permission from capital. It is the people’s choice-making power,
&#x20;distributed widely, renewed regularly, deleting on use, never becoming a
&#x20;weapon.

Personal Credit lets individuals participate freely.&#x20;
Community Credit lets communities build the shared world democratically.
&#x20;Together, they form the beginning of an actual economic democracy — not
&#x20;the fake kind where consumers “vote with their dollars” after all the&#x20;
real decisions have already been captured, but the real kind, where the&#x20;
units of choice are issued widely, access is unconditional, collective&#x20;
needs do not wait on private profit, and the people affected by&#x20;
decisions hold power over those decisions.

This is what AYU is being built to test. Not in theory. Not in some distant future where the politicians finally listen.

Now. With whoever shows up.

The old ledger was written by whoever got there first.

The
&#x20;next one is going to be written by all of us — or by legacy power, in&#x20;
which case the capture continues, dressed up in newer software.

You have a vote in this. You always did.

The question is whether we use it now, while there is still time to write the next ledger together.

## **Join the Founding Generation**

There
&#x20;is an honest irony in what comes next: we are using donations — the&#x20;
tools of the old world — to build the accounting system of the next one.
&#x20;We name it plainly. The system we are building will eventually make&#x20;
philanthropy unnecessary. Until then, this is the bridge.

A few weeks ago, I typed a simple question into a search bar over morning coffee:

> *what does “time is money” mean?*

The answer came back with almost comic honesty:

> *True success often involves leveraging other people’s time to create free time for oneself.*

That’s the system, accidentally confessing itself through the ambient answers of a billion searches. *True success.*
&#x20;Other people’s time. Your free time. The architecture of an entire&#x20;
civilization, distilled into a sentence so casual it almost sounds&#x20;
wholesome.

It isn’t. It’s sick.

### A Letter From Nick Marconi

A
&#x20;friend of mine, Nick Marconi, sent me a letter a few weeks ago. He&#x20;
lives in Vermont. The letter started, as the best letters do, with the&#x20;
weather:

> *I’m enjoying the second real week of&#x20;
> spring here in Vermont. It always seems to take longer to arrive for me.
> &#x20;The leaves on my four Norway maple trees are almost fully grown.&#x20;
> Bright, but delicate — fresh electric green!*

Then, halfway through, between the maples and the sign-off, Nick put his finger on the thing.

> *Okay,
> &#x20;money. I’ve been giving money a lot of thought lately. It goes all the&#x20;
> way back to Ben Franklin, who is credited with the saying that “time is&#x20;
> money.” Let me emphasize this — time is money. If we reverse this&#x20;
> statement we get, “money is time.”*

Nick goes on, in the way you do when you’re thinking out loud to a friend you trust to follow:

> *What
> &#x20;money seems to do is give a person control of time itself as a special&#x20;
> kind of “asset” — not a strictly material or financial one. Of course,&#x20;
> this is an illusion, but, nevertheless a powerful one.&#x20;
> Current/conventional money comes from the conversion of “time” (via debt
> &#x20;creation plus interest, which is just a “rental fee” on money itself)&#x20;
> into a special form of “credit” to control human labor.*
>
> *Given
> &#x20;enough money, we wouldn’t need to work — instead, we gain “free” time —
> &#x20;by gaining access to the productive labor of others. So, the ultimate&#x20;
> value of money today is its ability to give us the illusion of time&#x20;
> control and all the consequences that implies as it plays itself out in&#x20;
> our lives, economically and politically — or in a word, socially —&#x20;
> throughout the world.*

And then, he signed off:

> *I hope you find these partially digested thoughts somewhat interesting. Wishing you well, Nick.*

I did. I do.

Nick’s
&#x20;letter is the kind of thinking most of us have been doing privately for
&#x20;years and rarely say out loud. Six essays in, I’d been circling this&#x20;
same point. Nick just said it outright.

I want to add one refinement:

Money isn’t time. **Money is enforceable claims on other people’s time.**

A billionaire doesn’t have more hours in their day than the rest of us. They have *other people’s hours*:
&#x20;the maid who cleans, the nanny who raises their children, the chef who&#x20;
cooks, the tutor who teaches, the driver who waits, the pilot who flies,
&#x20;and the security detail that watches. Each one is a person whose hours&#x20;
have been bought. A billionaire’s day is twenty-four hours like everyone
&#x20;else’s. What they have is their own twenty-four, plus the bought hours&#x20;
of an entire supporting cast.

### The Time-Capture Stack

[image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!CaPp!,f_auto,q_auto\:good,fl_progressive\:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F932c1e70-2167-4142-b0b8-d6dae77fe3e4_1672x941.png)

 ([image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!CaPp!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto\:good,fl_progressive\:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F932c1e70-2167-4142-b0b8-d6dae77fe3e4_1672x941.png))

The
&#x20;current system captures human time at three temporal positions:&#x20;
present, future, and past. Each capture has its own technology.&#x20;
Together, they form much of the architecture of modern wealth.

**Present time is captured through wages.**

You
&#x20;sell your hours. The value those hours produce doesn't come back to you
&#x20;whole. Profit is taken inside production by the owner. Taxes are&#x20;
withheld by the state. Rent flows to the landlord. Interest flows to the
&#x20;bank. When your wage arrives, it feels like the value of your hour, but
&#x20;it's only part of it. The rest became someone else's accumulation. Your
&#x20;hours, captured into someone else's claim.

**Future time is captured through debt.**

The
&#x20;mortgage. The student loan. The credit card. Your unborn hours,&#x20;
pre-spent, sold forward to a bank that created the money with a&#x20;
keystroke. You wake up at twenty-six already owing the next thirty years
&#x20;to people who manufactured the claim from nothing. Part 2 of this&#x20;
series walked through the mechanics. The point here is what it *does* to you: debt commits time you haven't lived yet to projects you didn't choose.

**Past time is captured through inherited wealth.**

The
&#x20;hours of dead generations, stored in titles, trusts, and dynastic&#x20;
ownership, still extracting from the living. A child born to one family&#x20;
starts at zero. A child born to another starts at a hundred million. The
&#x20;difference isn't merit. It's that some ancestors' captured hours kept&#x20;
compounding after they died, and now their grandchildren collect rent on
&#x20;the work of strangers who weren't even born when the original capture&#x20;
happened.

Stack the three together.

The wealthiest&#x20;
people in the world are not time-rich in their own lives. They cannot&#x20;
live longer than anyone else. Their days are still twenty-four hours.&#x20;
What they *are* is claim-rich on the time of others:
&#x20;past, present, and future. Their wealth is not, fundamentally, a pile&#x20;
of dollars. It's a portfolio of enforceable demands on human hours that&#x20;
other people are obligated to deliver.

That’s what wealth, in this system, is.

Enclosure
&#x20;of the commons is temporal capture: ownership positions generating&#x20;
ongoing claims on the labor needed to use what was enclosed. Debt-money&#x20;
is time made repayable. Capitalism is not only a system for allocating&#x20;
goods. It is a system for converting human time into transferable,&#x20;
enforceable, inheritable claims.

The colloquial phrase for what wealth provides is ‘*fuck-you money’*:
&#x20;enough to tell anyone in your life to go to hell and not face&#x20;
consequences. The phrase hides the machinery underneath. No one is free&#x20;
under capitalism. Not even the wealthy. They didn’t buy freedom...&#x20;
there’s none for sale here. Capitalism offers only two positions: ground
&#x20;down by the gears, or greasing them. Wealth is the price of the second&#x20;
one. The grinding doesn’t stop, it only moves to someone else. Their&#x20;
hours are their own because someone else’s aren’t. Not yachts or&#x20;
mansions. Stockpiled hours. They still play a game whose other players&#x20;
have force, violence, law, and rival capital. They are free only&#x20;
relative to the desperate. Everyone else trades theirs for survival.

Personal
&#x20;Credit isn't merely a baseline payment; it's the un-capturing of time.&#x20;
Your hours stop being owed before you've lived them. Five centuries of&#x20;
capture, reversed. The hours come back to the bodies that produce them.&#x20;
Creditism doesn't make everyone the buyer of hours. It dissolves the&#x20;
position itself. No one buys their way out by selling someone else in.&#x20;
The coercion economy ends, and time comes home.

### The Lie at the Heart of It

If the system is this extractive, why does it persist? Why don’t more people walk away from it?

The
&#x20;brutal answer isn’t that they’re trapped, though many are. It’s that&#x20;
most people, including many of the people being most exploited by it,&#x20;
accept the system because they accept the lie underneath it.

The lie has two faces.

The
&#x20;first: humans are fundamentally lazy. Without wages, debt, fear of&#x20;
homelessness, fear of hunger, they won't work. Civilization will grind&#x20;
to a halt. Someone has to be desperate, or no one will plant the wheat.

The
&#x20;second: humans are fundamentally selfish. Without competition and&#x20;
scarcity to channel their self-interest, they won’t cooperate. Without&#x20;
markets to convert private greed into public benefit, the social fabric&#x20;
falls apart.

Together, the two faces construct an animal that&#x20;
requires coercion to function — too lazy to produce without threat, too&#x20;
selfish to share without rivalry, redeemable only through systems that&#x20;
turn its broken nature into useful work.

This is the story holding
&#x20;capitalism in place. It rests on an account of human nature that almost
&#x20;everyone has half-internalized, including many of the people the story&#x20;
grinds down hardest.

The lie is not competition itself.&#x20;
Pressure is what produces growth in muscles, skills, and ideas.&#x20;
Creditism keeps the pressure. It keeps markets and prices doing the one&#x20;
honest thing prices do: signaling what’s *genuinely*
&#x20;scarce — not diamonds locked in a vault to stay “rare,” but the real&#x20;
limits, this lithium, this acre, an expert’s hour — so finite things&#x20;
flow to where they matter most. Creditism keeps the reward for excellent
&#x20;work.

What it removes is the rule that nobody admits is in
&#x20;the game. Right now we’re playing musical chairs, and not the&#x20;
children’s kind, where losing just means you sit out and watch. In this&#x20;
version, the player left standing loses their business, then their home.
&#x20;A cafe that loses customers to another cafe down the street doesn’t&#x20;
only lose customers; one bad year and a whole family is out of the only&#x20;
town they’ve ever known. We’ve fused two games into one: the game of&#x20;
running a cafe, and the game of being alive. Lose the first, lose the&#x20;
second.

Creditism pulls apart this con-fusion. It keeps the&#x20;
scramble for the better chairs — contracts, prizes, recognition for work
&#x20;done well. Those are really scarce, and competing for them is the&#x20;
pressure that makes us better. But bolt one chair to the floor for every
&#x20;body in the room, and never take it away. Now you can lose your cafe&#x20;
and keep your home, your health, your footing, and your next try.

None of this means no one loses. The cafe can still close; the venture can fail; competition means sometimes it will and it’s *supposed* to. The game has an ending built into it. What ends is the cafe, not the life behind it.

### What We Actually Keep

Creditism
&#x20;is a better capitalism and a better communism. Which is to say we owe&#x20;
both gratitude for their friction and fusion. Capitalism built a great&#x20;
deal we don’t want to lose. Walk into a good bookstore with full shelves
&#x20;heavier with knowledge than one person could absorb in a hundred&#x20;
lifetimes. The drive that filled them is real. It helped give us&#x20;
medicine, mobile computers, and more food per acre than any farmer in&#x20;
history could have dreamed. That drive is evolutionarily essential.

The abundance is real *and*
&#x20;the extraction is real. Capitalism made extraordinary things, but it&#x20;
made them by fusing two things that no longer have to be joined: the&#x20;
work of building, and the threat of ruin if you failed at it. It would&#x20;
be a mistake to continue believing they can’t come apart, and that to&#x20;
keep the bookstore, someone always has to be left without a chair when&#x20;
the music stops. If they come apart, the shelves don’t need anyone to be
&#x20;desperate. The presses don’t run on fear. Creditism keeps everything&#x20;
actually built in capitalism, but it ends the one thing it built that no
&#x20;one ever wanted: the part where you pay with your life for a seat.

So. Let’s actually look at it.

### What People Really Do When the Threat Stops

The
&#x20;evidence on what humans do when survival is decoupled from labor is&#x20;
extensive, decades old, and more nuanced than the most enthusiastic&#x20;
versions of either side suggest.

In the **Mincome experiment**
&#x20;in Manitoba in the 1970s, the town of Dauphin became a “saturation&#x20;
site”: every family meeting the income guidelines was eligible for a&#x20;
guaranteed income supplement for four years. Working hours dropped&#x20;
modestly. Where they did, it was overwhelmingly two groups: new mothers&#x20;
staying home longer with infants, and teenagers staying in school&#x20;
instead of dropping out to support their families. The system that&#x20;
funded the experiment classified those reductions as “lost&#x20;
productivity.” Most people would call them better outcomes.

The **Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend**
&#x20;has paid every resident of Alaska an unconditional annual check since&#x20;
1982\. The most rigorous study of its labor market effects found no&#x20;
significant decrease in overall employment, with a modest increase in&#x20;
part-time work. Forty years of unconditional cash, no productivity&#x20;
apocalypse.

**Lottery winner studies** are more complicated than the popular telling. A large Swedish study found that winners *did*
&#x20;reduce their labor supply, with effects persisting for at least a&#x20;
decade. They worked less. But that is not the same as doing nothing. The
&#x20;research does not show that money makes people useless. It shows that&#x20;
survival pressure makes people work more than they would otherwise&#x20;
choose, often at things they might otherwise refuse.

That’s
&#x20;the thing. The question was never whether unconditional support changes
&#x20;labor patterns. It does. The question is whether what comes after looks
&#x20;like collapse. The evidence on that is consistent: it doesn’t.

**Retirees**
&#x20;are among our most active volunteers, caregivers, mentors, and creators
&#x20;— people the official economy has classified as “non-productive” who,&#x20;
on being released from wage labor, redirect their hours toward exactly&#x20;
the kind of work civilization actually needs.

**Open source software:**
&#x20;much of the digital infrastructure the global economy runs on, built&#x20;
largely by people who could be billing hours elsewhere, working without&#x20;
wages because the work was meaningful and they wanted it to exist.

**Parents, especially mothers:**
&#x20;the largest unpaid workforce in human history, doing the actual daily&#x20;
labor of reproducing civilization, every single day, for free, often&#x20;
while also holding down wage labor on the side.

Every **artist, scientist, scholar, and inventor**
&#x20;who ever did the work for years before anyone was willing to pay for&#x20;
it. Most of the things you love were made by people working without&#x20;
compensation, often against the interests of the people who later&#x20;
profited from them.

When survival pressure is reduced,&#x20;
people do not simply drop out. Some reduce paid hours. Some go to&#x20;
school, care for children, recover, create, build, or choose different&#x20;
work. The will to contribute doesn’t vanish. What changes is the kind of
&#x20;work people are willing to give their lives to. We wonder how you’ll&#x20;
surprise us when the threat stops

### Bullshit Jobs and Displaced Meaning

The
&#x20;anthropologist David Graeber spent years documenting what he called&#x20;
“bullshit jobs”: work that the people performing it privately admit&#x20;
makes no real contribution to the world. Compliance theater.&#x20;
Administrative bloat. Manufactured intermediation. Corporate roles whose
&#x20;purpose seems to be justifying the existence of other corporate roles.

The
&#x20;empirical picture is contested but real. A YouGov poll in the UK found&#x20;
that 37% of workers said their job did not make a meaningful&#x20;
contribution to the world. Academic studies vary: one large European&#x20;
analysis found lower rates and argued the phenomenon is better described
&#x20;as alienation than uselessness, while a 2023 U.S. study found stronger&#x20;
evidence for Graeber’s thesis. The exact number is debated. The pattern —
&#x20;that a substantial fraction of modern paid work is experienced by the&#x20;
people doing it as pointless — is not.

Coercion didn’t *produce* productivity. It largely *displaced* it.

Forced
&#x20;labor performed under threat of destitution doesn’t add value to&#x20;
civilization in proportion to the hours it consumes. It redirects human&#x20;
attention away from what matters and toward whatever happens to pay. The
&#x20;hours get spent. The meaning leaks out. The output is mostly&#x20;
throughput: paper that justifies more paper, intermediation that brokers
&#x20;more intermediation, optimization that optimizes nothing real.

Remove
&#x20;the coercion, and what disappears isn’t productive work. It’s the&#x20;
bullshit. People move, often quickly, toward work that means something:&#x20;
care, craft, creation, restoration, teaching, building, repair. The work
&#x20;that was always there. The work that capitalism couldn’t see, because&#x20;
it didn’t generate a return.

There’s a second waste underneath&#x20;
this, equally invisible because the official numbers never count it.&#x20;
Bullshit jobs are the hours that get spent on nothing. Unused labor is&#x20;
the hours that never get spent at all.

Look at what doesn’t exist&#x20;
because no profit pathway exists for it. The teacher who would teach but
&#x20;couldn’t afford the tuition. The healer who would heal but the clinic&#x20;
wasn’t profitable. The builder who would build but no one would lend&#x20;
them the tools. The researcher whose question didn’t promise a return.&#x20;
The artist, the mender, the elder who would mentor: all of them&#x20;
existing, capable, willing, and excluded from contribution because their
&#x20;work didn’t fit a profit-making position.

The capacity is there.&#x20;
It always was. What blocks it is an economy whose only logic for&#x20;
creating work is whether someone can profit from it. If extraction isn’t
&#x20;possible, the work doesn’t happen, no matter how badly it’s needed.&#x20;
Human potential is the input. The profit-making position is the gate.&#x20;
Most of the potential never gets through.

The capitalist economy&#x20;
doesn’t have a free-rider problem. It has a structural under-employment&#x20;
problem dressed up as a labor market. The cost is paid by every&#x20;
community that lacked the teachers, healers, builders, and elders who&#x20;
never got the chance to step into the roles they were ready for.

Buckminster Fuller saw all of this half a century ago. *“We keep inventing jobs,”* he wrote, *“because
&#x20;of this false idea that everybody has to be employed at some kind of&#x20;
drudgery because, according to Malthusian-Darwinian theory, he must&#x20;
justify his right to exist.”* He understood that technological
&#x20;capacity had already outrun the moral architecture justifying it. We&#x20;
could already provide for everyone. We kept inventing reasons we&#x20;
couldn’t. *“The true business of people,”* he added, *“should
&#x20;be to go back to school and think about whatever it was they were&#x20;
thinking about before somebody came along and told them they had to earn
&#x20;a living.”*

He was right then. He’s more right now. The&#x20;
freedom promised by automation didn’t return as time to the people whose
&#x20;labor was replaced. It returned as profit to someone else.

This&#x20;
is what the lottery winners and the retirees and the new parents and the
&#x20;open-source developers have been showing us every day. They are&#x20;
experiment. The data is in front of us.

The species we’d discover,
&#x20;freed from chronic survival threat, is not a lazy one. It’s a builder.&#x20;
It just refuses to build the wrong things.

### What Time-Freedom Actually Feels Like

None
&#x20;of what we’re describing is leisure in the consumerist sense. It isn’t&#x20;
vacation. It isn’t a reward earned at the end of a long working life. It
&#x20;needs a name: **unowned hours**. Hours that are yours because they weren’t owed before you’d lived them.

Most
&#x20;of us have to strain to picture it. Waking up without other people’s&#x20;
claims already sitting on our necks. The endless inboxes. The mortgage&#x20;
sucking silently in the background. The bills in the drawer. The dread&#x20;
of the next call from the landlord.

That low constant hum of&#x20;
pre-owed time is so universal we mistake it for simply being alive. It&#x20;
isn’t. It’s being inside the machine.

That feeling... the absence of the hum.

That’s what’s been stolen.

### The Free Rider, Dissolved

Every time someone proposes decoupling survival from labor, the same objection arrives like a reflex: *what about the free riders?* The people who would take the baseline and contribute nothing? The freeloaders? The slackers?

The category dissolves under inspection.

The
&#x20;free rider is a coherent moral concept mostly inside a coercive system.
&#x20;It requires three conditions, all of which Creditism removes:

**First, it requires zero-sum distribution.**
&#x20;Under capitalism, every dollar one person takes is a dollar another&#x20;
doesn’t have. Social programs feel extractive because the math is&#x20;
extractive. Creditism changes the arithmetic. Baseline Personal Credit&#x20;
is issued because you exist as a participant — calibrated to real&#x20;
productive capacity and the shared inheritance held in the commons, not&#x20;
skimmed from active workers. Earned Credit comes through contribution:&#x20;
the baker baking, the teacher teaching, the builder building, the&#x20;
caregiver caring. The retired teacher next door isn’t taking from the&#x20;
active welder. They’re both participating in a flow where contribution&#x20;
is recognized and access is distributed. The arithmetic of resentment&#x20;
dissolves.

**Second, it requires coerced participation as the default.** The “free rider” only stings when most people are riding because they have to. The resentment runs on the implicit comparison: *I’m forced to work, and they aren’t*.
&#x20;Once participation is genuinely voluntary, the category becomes “person
&#x20;who chose differently this season.” Some seasons you build. Some you&#x20;
rest. Some you raise children. Some you grieve. Some you study. Some you
&#x20;travel. There is no boundary left to police.

**Third, it requires the invisibility of unpaid labor.**
&#x20;Under capitalism, the “free rider” charge has often landed on people&#x20;
doing massive amounts of uncompensated work: caregivers, mothers,&#x20;
elders, artists, organizers, the disabled. Capitalism couldn’t see their
&#x20;contribution because it didn’t pay them, so it called them parasites.&#x20;
Personal Credit is, in part, the recognition that most of what holds&#x20;
civilization together was never on the wage ledger to begin with.

Underneath all three sits the structural answer: **inputs and outputs stay coupled at the community level.**
&#x20;A community whose members collectively opt out has less to share, less&#x20;
to celebrate, less to build with. The feedback is built into reality,&#x20;
not policed by bureaucrats. Communities that show up flourish.&#x20;
Communities that don’t, just get by. No threat is required. The world&#x20;
itself signals that participation matters, because participation is what
&#x20;makes a world.

What about the genuinely hard cases?&#x20;
Addiction. Severe antisocial behavior. Deep despair. People who, even in
&#x20;a fair system, will not contribute and will draw on common resources?

They
&#x20;exist. They’ve always existed. The threat of starvation never solved&#x20;
them: it made them worse. People die of overdoses while their&#x20;
communities tell themselves the system is teaching personal&#x20;
responsibility. People with severe mental illness sleep on sidewalks in&#x20;
countries that could trivially house them. The current system isn’t&#x20;
curing these conditions. It is hiding behind them, using them as the&#x20;
moral excuse for keeping everyone else terrified.

Creditism&#x20;
doesn’t claim to fix the hard cases. It claims to stop using them as the
&#x20;justification for a system that punishes the other ninety-nine percent&#x20;
of us. They still have baseline access, and their community still has&#x20;
responsibility to respond. The moral excuse goes. The responsibility to&#x20;
try doesn’t.

### What Happens to Ambition

You’ll hear this from the comfortable: *“If
&#x20;no one has to work, who builds anything great? Who founds the&#x20;
companies? Who pushes the frontiers? Don’t we need ambition? Don’t we&#x20;
need a competitive edge?”*

The answer: the same people who would have built it anyway.

The
&#x20;drive to create, to master, to lead, and to make things that last —&#x20;
that is real. So is the desire to test yourself against others. It runs&#x20;
deep in us. It does not, however, require a captive workforce as its&#x20;
stage.

Look at the actual builders of consequential things. Most&#x20;
of them were obsessed long before they were paid. Most of them, asked&#x20;
privately, would say money was the least interesting thing about the&#x20;
work. The romance of the founder mythology — the lone genius motivated&#x20;
by the prospect of becoming a billionaire — has always been retroactive.
&#x20;It’s a story the system tells about itself to justify the extraction&#x20;
that followed the work, not a description of what motivated the work in&#x20;
the first place.

The people who built the internet were not,
&#x20;mostly, doing it for money. The people who developed the science&#x20;
underlying every modern medical treatment were, by and large, not doing&#x20;
it for money. The people who write the great novels, build the great&#x20;
buildings, raise children well, run strong communities, push cultural&#x20;
boundaries — almost none of them are doing it primarily for money. Many&#x20;
are doing it *despite* the money, working second jobs to fund the work that matters.

In
&#x20;Creditism, contribution is still credited. The baker, the teacher, the&#x20;
builder, the healer — all of them get recorded for what they do. But the
&#x20;credit isn’t what conjures the doing. It records what happened; it&#x20;
doesn’t manufacture the want. People show up because the work matters to
&#x20;them, and then the system acknowledges that they showed up.

Ambition
&#x20;doesn’t die. It redirects. People still want to build cathedrals, run&#x20;
things, master crafts, push frontiers, and leave something behind. They&#x20;
just stop wanting — *needing* — to own other people’s hours to do it.

What dies isn’t ambition. It’s a particular form: **the ambition to convert other humans into instruments**.
&#x20;The drive to acquire control over other people’s time as a measure of&#x20;
one’s own success. The auto-suggested vision of “true success” as&#x20;
leveraging other people’s hours.

That ambition is the antithesis of liberty and it deserves to die.

What
&#x20;replaces it is older, and better, and has been waiting for us all&#x20;
along. The ambition to make a thing the world didn’t have. To teach a&#x20;
child what no one taught you. To repair what was broken, restore what&#x20;
was lost, learn what you don’t know yet, master a craft, lead a&#x20;
community, or build something that outlives you.

That ambition doesn’t need anyone to be desperate. It works fine in a world where everyone has a home.

### The Deeper Claim

We
&#x20;were told humans needed coercion because they were lazy. Most of us&#x20;
still hold some version of this belief, even those of us who hate the&#x20;
system, even those who can feel in their own lives that the story&#x20;
doesn’t quite fit.

The truth is the inverse.

The *system*
&#x20;needs coercion because it cannot survive without it. The story about&#x20;
human nature was less a description than a justification: an account&#x20;
constructed after the fact to explain why a particular group of humans&#x20;
needed to be kept in a state of perpetual economic threat.

Reduce
&#x20;the coercion and a great deal of what looked like laziness reveals&#x20;
itself as something else: refusal to work for the wrong people on the&#x20;
wrong things. The system mistook resistance for sloth and called it a&#x20;
moral failing. It wasn’t. It was a clue.

People are not, on average, lazy.

They
&#x20;are exhausted, indebted, surveilled, anxious, and pointed at work that&#x20;
makes no sense to them. Address that, and what you find underneath is&#x20;
the same human inheritance that built every cathedral, every symphony,&#x20;
every open-source repository, every garden, every meal made with care&#x20;
for someone else, every act of teaching, every moment of&#x20;
grandmother-to-granddaughter knowledge ever passed down on this planet.

That species is still here. It has been here the whole time.

It has just been renting its hours back, week after week, to the people who own the deed to its life.

A
&#x20;life-affirming economy begins when no human being’s survival depends on
&#x20;surrendering their future hours to someone else’s accumulated claim.

### Closing

Nick’s
&#x20;letter ended the way letters between friends end: wishing me well,&#x20;
hoping his “partially digested thoughts” might be somewhat interesting,&#x20;
and looking forward to hearing back.

They were. They are. He had&#x20;
said what most of us have been thinking over privately for years and&#x20;
rarely been able to say out loud.

Money was never just money.

It was claims on your hours.

The
&#x20;economy that comes next does not begin with revenge. It begins by&#x20;
ending the conversion of human time into something other humans can own.

Your hours come home.

# Creditism, Described Simply

### Today's game, and the new one.

[Common Planet Foundation](https://substack.com/@commonplanet) and [Remzi Bajrami](https://substack.com/@remzib)

Aug 27, 2026

Audio version read by the author: 

Let me describe the game we are all playing, as simply as I can.

The
&#x20;architect, inventor and futurist Buckminster Fuller liked to say that&#x20;
we are all riding a spaceship. He meant it literally. Earth is a vessel&#x20;
moving through space at sixty-seven thousand miles an hour, carrying&#x20;
eight billion crew members, with no port to stop at and no resupply&#x20;
coming. Everything we will ever have is already aboard.

Common
&#x20;Planet is a reader-supported publication. To receive new posts and&#x20;
support my work, consider becoming a free or paid subscriber.

Fuller
&#x20;told a story about this ship. The ship’s admirals divided it up. The&#x20;
ones with staterooms above the fuel tanks declared they owned the fuel.&#x20;
The ones beside the kitchen declared they owned the food. One admiral&#x20;
bunked next to a lifeboat and claimed the lifeboat. Then they invented a
&#x20;game among themselves called balance of trade, and soon most of them&#x20;
were losing. The admirals on one side began quietly scheming to tilt the
&#x20;ship and drown the admirals on the other side, who were scheming the&#x20;
same thing back.

And while all of this went on, nobody was&#x20;
steering. Nobody was watching the fuel. Nobody was checking the course.&#x20;
The ship drifted until the food and fuel ran out, too far from anywhere&#x20;
to resupply.

Finis, Fuller wrote.

A silly story, until you&#x20;
look out the window. On the real ship, a few players claim the fuel, the
&#x20;food, and the land the ship is made of, while billions scramble below&#x20;
deck. And the actual steering, the climate, the oceans, the soil we grow
&#x20;everything in, is mostly nobody’s job.

We are riding a real ship:
&#x20;one hull, finite stores, no port, no replacement. And aboard that ship,
&#x20;we are playing a game: rules that decide who owns what, who owes what,&#x20;
and who collects. The two are not the same thing, and the difference is&#x20;
our way out. You can’t change the game unless you change its rule set,&#x20;
and rules, unlike planets, are ours to write.

> **The game can be redesigned. The ship cannot be replaced.**

I
&#x20;call today’s game Game A, and I promise it is simpler than they told&#x20;
you. Game A runs on a three-beat loop: capture first, charge later,&#x20;
compound forever. Capture a piece of the shared world, the land, the&#x20;
water, the machines, the ideas, and write the claim into law. Charge&#x20;
everyone else for access to it. Then use the winnings to capture more.&#x20;
The players are people, Corporations and Nations, all running the same&#x20;
loop, and every player is ranked by a single score called net worth, the
&#x20;total value of everything they own.

So why do the players act&#x20;
this way? Are they evil? Mostly, no. They are winning. Game A rewards&#x20;
capturing, charging, and compounding, and it pays nothing for steering.&#x20;
The game and its rules shape our behavior more than our morals ever&#x20;
will. The failure point is not human nature. It is incentives, and we&#x20;
have ours backwards: this game pays most for the moves that hurt the&#x20;
ship, and least for the moves that keep it alive.

The game keeps&#x20;
score in points. That is what money is: points that decide who gets&#x20;
access to what everyone aboard produces together. People grow the food,&#x20;
build the cabins, make the medicine, fix the engines. Points decide who&#x20;
may use it all.

Points could be a fair way to run a ship. So look at how Game A was set up.

The
&#x20;first rule was finders keepers. Before most players ever arrived, the&#x20;
board had been divided: the land, the fuel, the food supply, the&#x20;
machines, claimed, and the claims written into law. Most of the crew&#x20;
never claimed anything; they were born onto squares already owned, with&#x20;
nothing to trade but their time. Losers weepers.

Then came the&#x20;
points. They told you money doesn’t grow on trees, and they were right:&#x20;
it is easier than that. There is a faucet in the sky, and in Game A two&#x20;
hands are on it. Governments that issue their own money spend new points
&#x20;into the world, and banks pour out more as loans, owed back with extra.
&#x20;Either way, the points land on a board already divided, and from the&#x20;
first roll, whatever you need sits on a square somebody already owns.

[image](https://common-planet.org/creditism)

 ([image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!85pl!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto\:good,fl_progressive\:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5dffaa2b-e901-4d62-ab1e-1df82eadb182_1536x1024.png))

Here
&#x20;is the loop in motion. Landing on an owned square costs you: rent for&#x20;
the cabins, profit on the food, interest on the loans. Points wash in&#x20;
every direction, wages out, prices back, but the tide pulls one way,&#x20;
toward the squares. And the squares themselves rise in price while their
&#x20;owners sleep. The more of the board you own, the more points you&#x20;
collect; the more points you collect, the more of the board you can buy.
&#x20;The winners do not just get richer. They buy the board, then charge&#x20;
everyone else for landing on it.

You have played this game on a&#x20;
rainy afternoon. Monopoly was designed by Lizzie Magie as a warning, and
&#x20;every match ends the way Fuller’s story does: one player owns the&#x20;
board, everyone else is broke, and the game is over. A big enough pile&#x20;
of points stops being spending money and becomes command over the&#x20;
places, tools, and work everyone else needs. But the points are only the
&#x20;money. The real score is control.

One more thing about our ship.&#x20;
Fuller loved airplane cockpits: walls of dials reporting the true&#x20;
condition of every part, the fuel, the engines, the weather, the course,
&#x20;so the crew can catch problems and fix them before anyone gets hurt.&#x20;
That is what instruments are for.

Our ship finally has&#x20;
instruments. Satellites watch the weather. Networks connect every deck.&#x20;
Computers can track what we have, what we need, and who knows how to fix
&#x20;what. And where are they pointed? At the players. They measure what&#x20;
scares us, what hooks us, what we will click on and buy. The finest&#x20;
sensors ever built are aimed at the crew, to sell to the crew, and what&#x20;
they do learn about the ship never makes it into the score.

It&#x20;
does not have to be this way. One of my favorite authors, the late David
&#x20;Graeber, liked to remind us that we made this world, and we could just&#x20;
as easily make it differently.

So here is the new game. Creditism&#x20;
is a new rulebook for the same ship, and its first principle fits in&#x20;
three words: people are valuable. Game A ran on capture, charge,&#x20;
compound. Creditism breaks the loop at every link: the commons can no&#x20;
longer be captured, survival can no longer be tolled, and what you spend
&#x20;can no longer compound into command over others. And the unit of the&#x20;
new game is not points. It is Credit.

Everyone aboard gets Credit&#x20;
for being aboard. Nobody alive built this ship; we were all born&#x20;
mid-voyage, heirs to it together. So every person receives Personal&#x20;
Credit, Credit of your own for your needs and wants, while a home,&#x20;
healthcare and learning are secured for every crew member as part of&#x20;
being crew. Why does everyone get Credit just for existing? Because&#x20;
every person is valuable, that’s why. No owner can ever again use your&#x20;
survival as a leash.

You collect more Credit every time you learn,
&#x20;play or work. Grow food, fix engines, teach the young crew, care for&#x20;
the sick, invent what is missing. The scoreboard finally counts the work
&#x20;that keeps us alive.

[image](https://common-planet.org/creditism)

 ([image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!MWFy!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto\:good,fl_progressive\:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F442fc62b-ad0f-4355-a7e8-64ae6fc275de_1536x1024.png))

Credit
&#x20;deletes when you use it. In this game the faucet in the sky points at&#x20;
people: Credit flows to you, and when you spend it, the purchase&#x20;
completes because the Credit deletes. Like a vote: your choice counts,&#x20;
and the ballot never becomes the winner’s property. The maker’s work is&#x20;
recognized with fresh Credit of their own, and nothing piles into a&#x20;
mountain that becomes command over other players. The game stops making&#x20;
admirals. Credit is a tool to incentivize people. Debt was a tool to&#x20;
control them.

[image](https://common-planet.org/creditism)

 ([image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!xT5d!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto\:good,fl_progressive\:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd3779af4-9e95-410a-a722-40535929b2ce_1536x1024.png))

The
&#x20;ship’s stores belong to the voyage. Your home stays private and your&#x20;
things stay yours, same as now. But the fuel tanks, the kitchen, the&#x20;
water, the engine room, everything every life depends on, is held by the
&#x20;crew in common and kept by stewards the crew chooses and can question.

Every
&#x20;deck gets Community Credit too: shared Credit for shared things. The&#x20;
crew decides together what to build next, and putting your Credit behind
&#x20;a project is your vote, counted the day you cast it.

Then we turn
&#x20;the instruments around. Point them at the ship: the soil, the water,&#x20;
the needs, the skills, the work waiting to be done. Let the crew see the
&#x20;true condition of the vessel the way pilots see their plane, and let&#x20;
what the instruments find become part of the score. Watching the ship,&#x20;
instead of watching the players.

Same ship. Same crew. Same&#x20;
stores; Fuller’s bet was that the ship has carried enough all along, and
&#x20;that what we lacked was know-how. What changes is the game. Game A pays
&#x20;players to capture, charge, and compound, and pays no one to steer. The
&#x20;new game pays the crew to keep the ship alive. It’s that simple.

Fuller’s
&#x20;story ended in the dark, out of food and fuel. Monopoly ends with one&#x20;
winner and a dead board. Ours doesn’t have to end at all. There are no&#x20;
lifeboats off a planet, and no second board in the box. The ship cannot&#x20;
be replaced. The game can. So we change the game and run the ship:&#x20;
together, on purpose, with the score finally kept for life.

***That
