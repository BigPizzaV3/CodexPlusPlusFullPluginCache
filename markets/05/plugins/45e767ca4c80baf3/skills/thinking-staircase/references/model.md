# Thinking Staircase Model

## Status of the model

The Thinking Staircase is an applied synthesis, not a validated seven-stage developmental theory. It combines ideas associated with dual-process cognition, observation and pattern recognition, logical reasoning, critical thinking, metacognition, systems thinking, and executive control.

Use it to navigate thinking and conversation. Do not use it as a clinical assessment, intelligence measure, or fixed personality label.

## The seven levels

### Level 1 — Reaction

- **Focus:** stimulus, emotion, habit, trained intuition
- **Question:** What must happen now?
- **Strength:** speed; essential in genuine emergencies and practiced responses
- **Failure mode:** treats emotion or familiarity as fact; blame and certainty arrive before inspection
- **Example:** "Young people today simply lack perseverance."

Distinguish two forms:

- **Adaptive reaction:** seconds-sensitive, purpose-aligned, trained action followed by later review
- **Emotional collapse:** anger, anxiety, fatigue, or threatened identity bypasses premise checking and produces attack, blame, or unsupported certainty

### Level 2 — Observation

- **Focus:** events, changes, repetitions, surface patterns
- **Question:** What exactly is happening?
- **Strength:** establishes a shared factual starting point
- **Failure mode:** mistakes noticing for explaining; stops at "that is the trend"
- **Example:** "Employees in their third year appear to be leaving more often."

### Level 3 — Causality

- **Focus:** cause-and-effect sequence, explanation, solution
- **Question:** Why is it happening, and what follows?
- **Strength:** supports reasoning, comparison, planning, and concrete proposals
- **Failure mode:** a valid chain can still begin from a false premise; overuses one linear cause
- **Example:** "Responsibility rises in year three while compensation changes little, so earlier raises may reduce departures."

### Level 4 — Validation

- **Focus:** premises, evidence, source, sample, comparison, counterevidence
- **Question:** Is the question and its evidence sound in the first place?
- **Strength:** inspects the playing field instead of merely optimizing within it
- **Failure mode:** skepticism becomes an identity; nothing can ever be decided
- **Example:** "Did departing employees report this reason, or is it the remaining managers' inference? How does the rate compare with peers?"

Critical thinking means inspecting premises so a decision can advance. It does not mean reflexively opposing a claim.

### Level 5 — Self-check

- **Focus:** one's own cognition, experience, interest, identity, and emotional state
- **Question:** What do I want to believe, and why?
- **Strength:** detects bias that knowledge or intelligence alone does not reliably remove
- **Failure mode:** becomes self-negation, indecision, or unsupported psychoanalysis
- **Example:** "Am I interpreting this as a compensation issue because my own early career was shaped by low pay?"

Phrase a Level 5 inference as a testable question or hypothesis unless the user has explicitly supplied the motive.

### Level 6 — Structure

- **Focus:** relationships, incentives, feedback loops, constraints, delays, recurring conditions
- **Question:** What system repeatedly generates this event?
- **Strength:** reveals recurrence, side effects, time delays, and partial optimization
- **Failure mode:** expands the issue until action disappears; treats a person's pain as merely system output
- **Example:** "The gap between recruiting promises and reality, managers' lack of teaching capacity, and a system that does not reward development may repeatedly generate departures."

A Level 6 analysis is incomplete until it can descend to observable evidence and executable action.

### Level 7 — Strategic operation

- **Focus:** purpose, timing, reversibility, constraints, selection and sequencing of levels
- **Question:** Which level should be used now, and when should we decide or act?
- **Strength:** selects among reaction, observation, causality, validation, self-check, and structure instead of living permanently at the top
- **Failure mode:** uses complexity to delay action or present ordinary indecision as sophistication
- **Example:** "Begin reversible interviews and workload checks now; defer an irreversible policy change until the employee evidence arrives."

Level 7 is the controller, not merely the highest abstraction. Its output may deliberately return to Level 1 for urgent action, Level 2 for fact gathering, or Level 3 for an executable plan.

## Core propositions

1. A higher level is not inherently superior.
2. The appropriate level depends on urgency, purpose, evidence, audience, and reversibility.
3. A person's level changes by domain, role, fatigue, emotion, and time pressure.
4. Practical strength depends on ordinary range, stress-floor, and recovery speed.
5. Structural insight must be translated downward to reach action and other people.
6. Conversation conflict often reflects different scopes or time horizons, not one side being simply wrong.

## Standard prompts

Use these as the complete audit:

1. Reaction — What did I immediately feel or want to conclude?
2. Observation — What facts and patterns are actually confirmed?
3. Causality — What mechanism could connect cause and outcome?
4. Validation — Are the premise, evidence, comparison, and counterevidence adequate?
5. Self-check — What might I prefer to believe, and what experience or interest could be narrowing me?
6. Structure — What relationship, incentive, constraint, loop, or delay repeatedly produces this?
7. Operation — Which level is useful now; what should be decided, tested, delayed, communicated, or done?

## Short training moves

| Move | Practice |
|---|---|
| 1 → 2 | Insert five seconds and name one observable fact. |
| 2 → 3 | Ask “why” up to three times and compare causal candidates. |
| 3 → 4 | Ask one “in the first place” question about the premise or evidence. |
| 4 → 5 | Name the emotion, interest, prior success, or identity pressure affecting judgment. |
| 5 → 6 | Ask “what larger system is this part of?” |
| 6 → 7 | Separate timing, reversibility, containment, and structural correction. |
| 7 → lower | Translate the structure into facts, causal language, choice, owner, and next action. |

## Conceptual sources

- Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011): fast automatic and slower deliberative cognition
- John H. Flavell, “Metacognition and Cognitive Monitoring” (1979): metacognitive knowledge and monitoring
- Peter M. Senge, *The Fifth Discipline* (1990): interrelationships, feedback, and systems thinking
- Adele Diamond, “Executive Functions” (2013): inhibition, working memory, and cognitive flexibility
- Keith E. Stanovich, Richard F. West, and Maggie E. Toplak, “Myside Bias, Rational Thinking, and Intelligence” (2013): relative independence of myside bias and intelligence
