# Editorial Infographic Patterns

Use this reference for single-page, information-dense session infographics. Treat the patterns as compositional guidance, not a quota or fixed template.

## Select architecture by session type

Choose the architecture that best represents the source; do not force every session into the same template.

- **Leadership or keynote:** hero title and supported speaker details; core message; key figures; leadership principles; frameworks or pillars; journey or timeline; examples; lessons; future direction; takeaway.
- **Government or policy address:** current situation; challenges; strategic priorities; evidence; policies or initiatives; stakeholder responsibilities; implementation direction; expected impact; call to action.
- **Technical presentation:** problem; evidence; method; technical solution; results; applications; risks or limitations; recommendations.
- **Panel discussion:** central question; speaker perspectives; shared views; contrasting positions; principal themes; consensus; unresolved questions; collective takeaways.
- **Roundtable:** discussion theme; stakeholder perspectives; recurring concerns; proposed solutions; convergence; outstanding issues; recommended actions.

For a detailed keynote, the useful default flow is header, core message, key facts, main framework, journey or process, examples, initiatives or technology, lessons, future direction, and takeaway footer. Change it whenever another sequence better preserves the session.

## Page architecture

### Header

Include the session label, factual title, optional source-supported subtitle, and speaker or event context when useful. Use a supplied portrait only when authorized.

### Framing banner

Feature the session's purpose, central proposition, or defining context in a visually prominent band. Orient the reader rather than repeating the title.

### Numbered theme modules

Group related source material into clearly ordered sections. A module may contain a concise heading, short explanatory prose or bullets, a supported example, and a functional icon or authorized image. Do not force every module into the same internal structure or manufacture enough themes to fill a layout.

### Closing synthesis

End with stated commitments, outcomes, next steps, or a faithful summary of what success means. Keep aspirations distinct from achieved results.

### Pull quotation

Use quotation marks only for exact, verified transcript wording. Otherwise present the idea as an unquoted synthesis.

Choose quotations only when they express the speaker's central philosophy, a major conclusion, or a strong human message. Use them sparingly. If transcription quality prevents confidence in the wording, paraphrase without quotation marks.

### Statistics

Give reliable and consequential figures visual prominence while retaining their units, time period, comparator, qualification, and relationship to the speaker's claim. Never amplify a statistic beyond what the source states.

## Visual hierarchy

- Make the title the strongest typographic element.
- Use a distinct banner for the central proposition.
- Number major themes consistently when sequence aids navigation.
- Keep body copy subordinate to headings.
- Use spacing, borders, and background tints to define modules.
- Prefer one dominant brand color, one accent, and neutrals unless the supplied brand system requires otherwise.
- Use icons semantically; do not imply unsupported facts through decorative symbols.
- Preserve generous margins despite high information density.

Design three reading levels:

1. **Immediate message:** what the session was about, who spoke, and the central message.
2. **Major insights:** approximately four to seven supported themes, frameworks, or findings.
3. **Supporting evidence:** figures, examples, concise explanations, milestones, case studies, initiatives, and short verified quotations.

## Content density

Prefer concise, faithful paraphrases over transcript excerpts. If content cannot remain readable at the requested dimensions:

1. remove repetition;
2. shorten secondary explanations;
3. move optional detail into captions or supporting material;
4. enlarge the page when the delivery format permits; or
5. convert the design into a carousel.

Never solve overcrowding with illegibly small type or by deleting material qualifications.

## Production method

Compose text-dense infographics with a deterministic layout system such as Canva, SVG, HTML/CSS, presentation software, or another tool that keeps typography controllable. Use image generation for supporting illustrations, backgrounds, or authorized visual assets—not for rendering the complete text-heavy page in one pass.

Keep visible text editable where the production tool permits. Insert text through the layout system so names, quotations, numbers, and wording can be proofread exactly.

Before production, construct a detailed prompt or design specification covering the infographic purpose, title, supported speaker details, hierarchy, exact sections, verified figures, required labels, style, color direction, layout, accuracy constraints, and text-density limits.

## Final quality control

Check every name, role, organization, date, number, acronym, program name, and quotation against the source. Confirm that the central message reads quickly, major insights are visually distinct, text remains concise and legible, material nuances survive condensation, and no unsupported content has entered through copy, icons, imagery, or layout labels.

## Asset provenance

- Use portraits, logos, venue images, and organizational graphics only when supplied or authorized.
- Do not generate a real speaker's likeness to replace an unavailable portrait.
- Do not use representative imagery in a way that implies it documents the actual event, workplace, incident, or participant.
- Label representative imagery when a reasonable reader could mistake it for documentary evidence.
