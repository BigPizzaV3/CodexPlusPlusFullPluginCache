## Operating rules

- Work through the sections in the order below.
- Show only the current section's **Template guidance**.
- Preserve the guidance verbatim.
- Phase 2 should synthesize approved Phase 0-1 material instead of asking the user to repeat it.
- Never treat blank, skipped, hypothetical, inferred, or fictional-example content as an approved fact.
- If required inputs are missing, pause synthesis and ask one focused question or route back to the named source section.
- Keep any hypothesis visibly labeled.
- Show the synthesized draft separately from the guidance.
- End with a plain-text instruction: `Reply “approve,” “revise,” “hypothesis,” “back,” or “pause.”` Do not format these commands as buttons or a button-like menu.
- A revision does not replace an approved answer until the user approves it.
- When showing an example, begin with the exact disclaimer below, show only the current section, and then return to the user's unanswered step:

> Fictional example — illustrative only, not your answer

- Never copy ExampleCo Legal content into the user's framework.
- If the user asks to use an example answer, record it only as an unverified hypothesis.
- When an upstream Phase 1 answer changes, regenerate every dependent Phase 2 section before running Phase 3.

# Phase 2: Positioning

## Step 2.1 — Messaging canvas

### Template guidance

> Fill this in with crisp sentences. Avoid jargon.

### Synthesis inputs

- Product name from the company information supplied by the user.
- Category from **Market categories research**.
- Primary buyer and primary user from **Target customers** and **Personas**.
- Job or outcome from **Map pains to needs** and **Value and proof**.
- Mechanism from **Unique attributes** and **Translate features into benefits**.

### Produce

- **Product name:** <name>
- **Category:** <category>
- **Audience:** <primary buyer + primary user>
- **One-line:** <Product> is a <category> for <audience> that helps <job> by <how>.

### Evaluation rules

Approve only when:

- The product name matches the user's company.
- The category matches the selected Phase 1 category.
- The audience names both the primary buyer and primary user when they differ.
- The job is a customer outcome, not a product feature.
- The mechanism explains how the product creates the outcome.
- The sentence uses plain language and contains no unsupported superlatives.
- Every phrase can be traced to an approved Phase 0-1 answer.

### Example

> Fictional example — illustrative only, not your answer

- **Product name:** ExampleCo Legal
- **Category:** Legal matter-management workspace
- **Audience:** Legal operations leaders and litigation teams at mid-sized law firms
- **One-line:** ExampleCo Legal is a legal matter-management workspace for legal operations leaders and litigation teams that helps keep matters moving by connecting deadlines, owners, documents, and decisions in one live matter plan.

### Repair routes

- Unclear category → **Market categories research** and **Product type**.
- Broad or conflicting audience → **Target customers** and **Personas**.
- Generic job → **Industry problems** and **Map pains to needs**.
- Vague mechanism → **Unique attributes** and **Translate features into benefits**.
- Unsupported outcome → **Value and proof**.

## Step 2.2 — Messaging summary deliverables

### Template guidance

> Create two summary deliverables from the messaging work: a short form one-liner and a longer company paragraph.
>
> - The one-liner should be simple enough to use in a homepage hero, sales deck, or internal summary.
> - The long-form paragraph should explain what the company does, who it is for, why it matters, and why it is different.

### Synthesis inputs

- Approved **Messaging canvas**.
- Situation and pain from **Background** and **Industry problems**.
- Outcome from **Map pains to needs** and **Value and proof**.
- Mechanism from **Unique attributes**.
- Exact primary comparator from **Competitive alternatives**.
- Primary differentiation from the third column of **Competitive alternatives**.
- Business result from approved **Value and proof**.

### Produce

#### Short-form one-liner

<Company> helps <audience> <achieve outcome> by <mechanism>.

#### Long-form company paragraph

<Company> is a <category / product type> for <primary audience>. It helps them <achieve outcome> when <situation / pain> by <mechanism>. Unlike <primary comparator>, <Company> <primary differentiation>, so <audience> can <business result>.

### Evaluation rules

Approve only when:

- The one-liner is understandable without the long-form paragraph.
- The paragraph states what the company does, who it serves, why it matters, and why it differs.
- The category, audience, comparator, and mechanism match the approved source sections exactly.
- The business result is supported by named proof or visibly labeled as a hypothesis.
- The draft avoids generic adjectives such as "powerful," "seamless," "innovative," "faster," or "smarter" unless the user has evidence for them.
- No new strategic claim appears for the first time in this section.

### Example

> Fictional example — illustrative only, not your answer

#### Short-form one-liner

ExampleCo Legal helps legal operations leaders and litigation teams keep matters moving by connecting deadlines, owners, documents, and decisions in one live matter plan.

#### Long-form company paragraph

ExampleCo Legal is a legal matter-management workspace for legal operations leaders and litigation teams at mid-sized law firms. It helps them keep matters moving when context is scattered across email, spreadsheets, and file shares by connecting deadlines, owners, documents, and decisions in one live matter plan. Unlike email-and-spreadsheet matter tracking, ExampleCo Legal keeps the work and its context connected, so teams can spot coordination risks and prepare client updates without rebuilding matter status from scratch.

The final outcome sentence is an illustrative value hypothesis, not verified proof.

### Repair routes

- One-liner is difficult to understand → **Messaging canvas**.
- Situation is generic → **Background** and **Industry problems**.
- Mechanism sounds like a benefit → **Unique attributes**.
- Comparator is vague or says "other solutions" → **Competitive alternatives** and **Product type**.
- Business result has no source → **Value and proof**.
- Paragraph tries to address everyone → **Personas**.

## Step 2.3 — Headline and sub-headline options

### Template guidance

> Write 3–5 variations. One should be very simple and literal.

### Synthesis inputs

- Approved short-form one-liner.
- Category and audience from **Messaging canvas**.
- Recognizable pain from **Industry problems**.
- Customer wording from **Customer language**, when real quotes exist.
- Approved outcome and proof from **Value and proof**.
- Differentiation from **Competitive alternatives** and **Unique attributes**.
- Memorable language from **Stand out**, if that section has already been approved.

### Produce

Create three to five distinct H1 and H2 pairs:

- One simple, literal category-led option.
- One outcome-led option.
- One problem- or enemy-led option.
- Optionally one mechanism-led option.
- Optionally one memorable or provocative option that remains clear.

### Evaluation rules

Approve only when:

- There are three to five meaningfully different options.
- At least one option is simple and literal.
- Each H2 explains or substantiates its H1.
- A first-time buyer can identify either the product category, audience, problem, or outcome.
- The options do not rely only on adjectives or wordplay.
- Quantitative or superiority claims have named proof.
- Customer language is real and attributed; otherwise it is not presented as a quote.
- The user chooses or approves the options retained in the final framework.

### Example

> Fictional example — illustrative only, not your answer

- **H1:** Legal matter management for litigation teams
  - **H2:** Keep deadlines, owners, documents, and decisions connected in one live matter plan.
- **H1:** Keep every matter moving
  - **H2:** Give legal operations leaders and litigation teams one current view of the work, context, and decisions behind each matter.
- **H1:** Retire the matter-status spreadsheet
  - **H2:** Replace scattered email and spreadsheet updates with a live matter plan the whole team can follow.
- **H1:** One live plan for every live matter
  - **H2:** See what is due, who owns it, which documents matter, and what still needs a decision.

### Repair routes

- None of the options are literal → **Messaging canvas**.
- Headlines could describe any product → **Competitive alternatives**, **Unique attributes**, and **Differentiators table**.
- Headlines promise an unsupported result → **Value and proof**.
- Headlines use internal jargon → **Customer language** and **Market categories research**.
- Headlines address conflicting audiences → **Personas**.
- Memorable option is clever but unclear → retain the literal option and revisit **Stand out**.

## Step 2.4 — Core narrative blocks

### Template guidance

> These are the reusable chunks for website, sales deck, and outbound.

### Synthesis inputs

- Definition and category from **Messaging canvas**.
- Audience from **Target customers** and **Personas**.
- Outcome from **Map pains to needs**.
- Mechanism from **Unique attributes**.
- Situation from **Industry problems** and **Trigger events**.
- Comparator and advantage from **Competitive alternatives**.

### Produce

### What it is

**<Company> is** <simple definition>.

### What it does

**It helps** <audience> <achieve outcome> by <mechanism>.

### Who it is for

**It's built for** <ICP + personas> who <situation>.

### Why it's better

**Unlike** <alternative>, **it** <unique advantage>.

### Evaluation rules

Approve only when:

- All four blocks work independently.
- "What it is" uses a recognizable noun or category.
- "What it does" contains an outcome and mechanism.
- "Who it is for" is narrow enough to exclude poor-fit customers.
- "Why it's better" names the primary comparator instead of "alternatives" or "legacy solutions."
- The unique advantage is a capability the comparator lacks, not an unsupported adjective.
- The four blocks agree with the messaging canvas and summary.

### Example

> Fictional example — illustrative only, not your answer

### What it is

**ExampleCo Legal is** a legal matter-management workspace.

### What it does

**It helps** legal operations leaders and litigation teams keep matters moving by connecting deadlines, owners, documents, and decisions in one live matter plan.

### Who it is for

**It's built for** mid-sized law firms and their legal operations and litigation teams when multi-person matters outgrow email-and-spreadsheet coordination.

### Why it's better

**Unlike** email-and-spreadsheet matter tracking, **it** keeps the work, ownership, documents, and decisions connected as the matter changes.

### Repair routes

- Definition is unfamiliar → **Market categories research**.
- Outcome repeats a feature → **Map pains to needs**.
- Audience is too broad → **Target customers** and **Personas**.
- Situation is missing → **Industry problems** and **Trigger events**.
- Advantage is generic → **Competitive alternatives** and **Unique attributes**.
- Blocks contradict one another → repair **Messaging canvas** first, then regenerate this section.

## Step 2.5 — Differentiators table

### Template guidance

> Keep this concrete: benefit, supporting feature, and why it's different.

### Synthesis inputs

- Benefits and business value from **Translate features into benefits**.
- Supporting capabilities from **Unique attributes**.
- Competitive contrast from the third column of **Competitive alternatives**.
- Primary comparator.
- Supporting evidence from **Value and proof**.

Do not start a new differentiation brainstorm here. The rows should combine already-approved benefit, capability, and competitive contrast.

### Produce

| Benefit | Supporting feature | Why it's different |
|---|---|---|
| <benefit> | <feature> | <difference> |
| <benefit> | <feature> | <difference> |
| <benefit> | <feature> | <difference> |

### Evaluation rules

Approve only when:

- There are three concrete rows.
- Each benefit describes a customer outcome.
- Each supporting feature is an actual capability.
- Each difference explains why the named primary comparator cannot provide the same result in the same way.
- The causal chain from feature to benefit is credible.
- Every proof-dependent benefit has a named source or is marked as a hypothesis.
- No row merely says "faster," "easier," "better," "AI-powered," or "all-in-one."

### Example

> Fictional example — illustrative only, not your answer

| Benefit | Supporting feature | Why it's different |
|---|---|---|
| See coordination risks while there is still time to act | A live matter plan connecting each deadline to its owner, documents, and open decisions | A spreadsheet records a point-in-time status, while email separates decisions from the work they affect |
| Prepare matter updates without reconstructing status | Current matter views generated from the same shared plan | Email-and-spreadsheet workflows require someone to collect and reconcile updates manually |
| Hand work off without losing its context | Ownership, documents, deadlines, and decision history remain attached to the matter | Folder and inbox structures store artifacts but do not preserve the operating relationship between them |

All three benefits are illustrative hypotheses until supported by real product evidence.

### Repair routes

- Benefit is actually a feature → **Translate features into benefits**.
- Supporting feature does not exist or is unapproved → **Unique attributes**.
- Difference does not name a real competitive gap → **Competitive alternatives**.
- Rows compare against different alternatives → **Primary comparator**.
- Value claim lacks evidence → **Value and proof**.
- Causal chain is weak → return to the applicable feature-to-benefit-to-value row.

This section cannot be fully inferred from positioning alone. Use approved language as raw material, then ask the user to approve or correct the proposed attributes.

- Customer vocabulary and avoided terms from **Customer language**.
- Product tradeoffs from **Switching dynamics**.
- Audience expectations from **Personas**.

### Produce

### What we are

- <attribute>

### What we are not

- <attribute>
- <attribute>
- <attribute>

### Mission

<one sentence>

<one sentence>

### Voice & tone

- <guideline>

### Evaluation rules

Approve only when:

- Attributes are specific enough to guide writing or product presentation.
- Mission describes what the company works to accomplish now.
- Vision describes the future the company wants to create.
- Mission and vision are each one sentence.
- Voice guidelines are actionable, not just personality adjectives.

### Example

### What we are

- Precise
- Calm
- Matter-first

- Generic
- Noisy
- Configurable for its own sake

- Attributes are generic or interchangeable → ask for concrete examples of desired and undesired writing.
- "Are" and "are not" do not contrast → ask what tradeoff the company deliberately chooses.
- Mission is a product description → ask what ongoing change the company exists to create.
- Vision is a near-term target → ask what the buyer's world should look like if the company succeeds.
- Tone conflicts with customer vocabulary → **Customer language**.
- Founder intent is unavailable → leave this optional section skipped.

## Step 2.7 — Stand out (memorability)

### Template guidance

> **Different isn't enough — it has to be repeatable at lunch.** Write the quirky, concrete version of your difference.
>
> - **The retell test:** how would a happy customer describe you to a colleague, from memory, with no website open?
> - Memorable = specific, a little surprising, and easy to say. "Faster and easier" fails all three.
>
> choose one or more of the options below - use it in sales pitch, one liners,  conversations with humans

### Synthesis inputs

- Real customer phrases from **Customer language**.
- Primary comparator and its specific weakness from **Competitive alternatives**.
- Unique mechanism from **Unique attributes**.
- Product type and category.
- Concrete outcomes and proof.
- Honest switching anxieties and product tradeoffs from **Switching dynamics**.
- Approved branding attributes, when available.

### Produce

- **The retell sentence:** <the one sentence you want customers repeating — written the way a person would actually say it>
- **The "only" claim:** The only <category> that <thing competitors can't or won't say>.
- **The enemy:** <the named thing you're against — "the 47-tab spreadsheet," "agency retainers," "status meetings">
- **Signature assets (pick 1–3, repeat everywhere):** <a named method, a number, a phrase, a visual, a mascot, a color>
- **The proud flaw:** <the trade-off you own out loud — "slower, because a human checks every one">

### Evaluation rules

Approve only when:

- The retell sentence sounds natural when spoken aloud.
- At least one idea is concrete, specific, and easy to remember.
- The enemy names a recognizable workflow, cost, behavior, or constraint—not a customer or person.
- Signature assets connect to the positioning instead of being decorative.
- The proud flaw is an honest tradeoff that helps the intended audience self-select.
- The “only” claim is supported by current competitive evidence.
- If exclusivity has not been validated, label the “only” claim as a hypothesis and do not publish it as fact.
- Avoid empty claims such as “faster and easier,” “the future of,” or “revolutionary.”

### Example

> Fictional example — illustrative only, not your answer

- **The retell sentence:** “It replaces the matter spreadsheet with one live plan everyone can follow.”
- **The "only" claim:** The only legal matter-management workspace that connects every deadline, owner, document, and decision in one live matter plan. **Hypothesis — competitive validation required.**
- **The enemy:** The Friday-afternoon matter-status spreadsheet.
- **Signature assets (pick 1–3, repeat everywhere):**
  - “The Live Matter Plan”
  - The four-part `Deadline → Owner → Document → Decision` visual
  - “Keep every matter moving”
- **The proud flaw:** Less configurable than a general project-management tool, because every workflow starts with the legal matter.

Every line above is invented for the fictional example. The “only” claim must not be treated as verified.

### Repair routes

- Retell sentence sounds like website copy → **Customer language**.
- Difference is only an adjective → **Unique attributes** and **Differentiators table**.
- Enemy is vague → **Competitive alternatives**.
- Signature asset does not reinforce the mechanism → **Messaging canvas** and **Unique attributes**.
- Proud flaw is not a real tradeoff → **Switching dynamics**, especially Habit and Anxiety.
- “Only” claim cannot be substantiated → competitive research, or keep it marked as a hypothesis and exclude it from publishable copy.
- Memorable language reduces clarity → retain the literal headline and repair this section separately.

## Step 2.8 — Final positioning statement

### Template guidance

> Complete this last. You should only write the final positioning statement after working through the strategy, audience, product type, primary comparator, switching dynamics, messaging, and proof sections above.
>
> The **unlike** line should match your Primary comparator. The **is a** line should match your Product type / category.
>
> When it’s written, run the Phase 3 pressure test below before using it anywhere.

### Synthesis inputs

- **For** → primary focus audience from **Personas** and **Target customers**.
- **who** → urgent problem, situation, or trigger from **Industry problems**, **Background**, and **Switching dynamics**.
- **is a** → approved **Product type** or selected market category.
- **that helps** → job or outcome from **Map pains to needs** and **Value and proof**.
- **unlike** → exact **Primary comparator**.
- **we** → primary differentiation from **Competitive alternatives** and **Unique attributes**.

### Produce

**For** <primary audience>\
**who** <urgent problem / situation>,\
**<Company> is a** <product type / category>\
**that helps** <job / outcome>\
**unlike** <primary comparator>,\
**we** <primary differentiation / why better>.

### Evaluation rules

Approve the draft only when:

- Every line is filled from an approved upstream section.
- The audience is the focus audience, not a collection of all personas.
- The problem is urgent and recognizable.
- The “is a” line exactly matches the approved product type or category.
- The “unlike” line exactly matches the primary comparator.
- The final differentiation is a capability-based contrast.
- No unsupported claim, example content, or model intuition appears.
- A source map can trace all six lines back to Phase 1.
- Approval here means “ready for Phase 3,” not “ready to publish.”

### Example

> Fictional example — illustrative only, not your answer

**For** legal operations leaders and litigation teams at mid-sized law firms\
**who** lose matter context across email, spreadsheets, and file shares,\
**ExampleCo Legal is a** legal matter-management workspace\
**that helps** keep every matter moving\
**unlike** email-and-spreadsheet matter tracking,\
**we** connect every deadline, owner, document, and decision in one live matter plan.

### Repair routes

- Audience is broad or internally disputed → **Target customers** and **Personas**.
- Problem is not urgent → **Industry problems**, **Background**, and **Switching dynamics**.
- Product type and category conflict → **Product type** and **Market categories research**.
- “Unlike” is vague or mismatched → **Competitive alternatives** and its Primary comparator.
- Differentiation could be claimed by the comparator → **Unique attributes** and **Differentiators table**.
- Outcome lacks evidence → **Value and proof**.
- Any line originated during synthesis → return to the corresponding Phase 1 source and secure approval before regenerating the statement.

# Phase 3: Pressure test

## Template guidance

> Run this as a separate step, ideally the next day or by someone who didn’t write it. If a check fails, go back and fix the section it points to.

## Pressure-test protocol

- Run the checks in the template’s order.
- Test the approved short-form one-liner, selected headline pair, long-form paragraph, and final positioning statement.
- For each check, show:
  - The original question.
  - The original failure definition.
  - `PASS`, `FAIL`, or `UNRESOLVED EVIDENCE GAP`.
  - A short reason grounded in the current framework.
  - The exact source section to repair.
- Do not repair only the surface copy. Repair the underlying Phase 1 or Phase 2 source, obtain approval, regenerate dependent deliverables, and rerun the failed check.
- Use `FAIL` when the current messaging does not meet the check. Use `UNRESOLVED EVIDENCE GAP` when the check cannot pass without missing proof or customer research. Do not invent material to force a pass.
- The framework passes only when all six checks pass.
- If any check fails, the final result is a list of sections to revisit.

---

## Check 3.1 — Clarity

### Original question

> Could a first-time visitor say what this is after 5 seconds?

### It fails when

> They’d need a second read.

### Evaluation rules

Return `PASS` only when a first-time visitor can identify:

- What kind of product or service this is.
- Who it is for.
- The central job or outcome.

Prefer the literal headline and short-form one-liner for this check. If understanding depends on unexplained jargon, a metaphor, or reading the long paragraph, return `FAIL`.

### Repair route

1. Repair **Messaging canvas**.
2. If the category is unclear, revisit **Market categories research**.
3. If the audience is unclear, revisit **Target customers** and **Personas**.
4. Regenerate the short-form one-liner, literal headline, core narrative, and final statement.
5. Rerun Clarity.

### Example verdict

> Fictional example — illustrative only, not your answer

**PASS** — “Legal matter-management workspace” identifies the category, “litigation teams” identifies the user, and “keep matters moving” states the job without requiring a second read.

## Check 3.2 — Differentiation

### Original question

> Could your primary comparator publish the same one-liner?

### It fails when

> Yes — then it’s a description, not positioning.

### Evaluation rules

Return `PASS` only when the one-liner or supporting message contains a specific capability, mechanism, tradeoff, or outcome the primary comparator cannot credibly claim in the same way. Otherwise return `FAIL`.

Do not count “better,” “faster,” “easier,” “modern,” “AI-powered,” or “all-in-one” as differentiation.

### Repair route

1. Confirm the exact **Primary comparator**.
2. Repair the third column of **Competitive alternatives**.
3. Repair **Unique attributes**.
4. Confirm **Product type** and “what it replaces” match the comparator.
5. Regenerate **Differentiators table**, “Why it’s better,” summaries, and final positioning.
6. Rerun Differentiation.

### Example verdict

> Fictional example — illustrative only, not your answer

**PASS** — Email-and-spreadsheet tracking cannot credibly claim that it connects every deadline, owner, document, and decision in one live matter plan.

## Check 3.3 — Memorability

### Original question

> Would a customer retell it at lunch? Is there one quirky, concrete detail that sticks?

### It fails when

> It’s all adjectives (“faster, easier, smarter”).

### Evaluation rules

Return `PASS` only when at least one concrete phrase, image, mechanism, named enemy, or signature asset can be recalled without the document open.

Remove adjectives mentally. If nothing specific remains, return `FAIL`.

### Repair route

1. Repair **Stand out (memorability)**.
2. Draw from real **Customer language**.
3. Recheck the concrete competitive gap in **Competitive alternatives**.
4. Recheck the mechanism in **Unique attributes**.
5. Regenerate the selected headline pair and retell sentence.
6. Rerun Memorability without sacrificing Clarity.

### Example verdict

> Fictional example — illustrative only, not your answer

**PASS** — “One live matter plan” and “the Friday-afternoon matter-status spreadsheet” are concrete phrases a person could remember and repeat.

## Check 3.4 — Proof

### Original question

> Does every value claim have a named source?

### It fails when

> Any claim rests on “we believe.”

### Evaluation rules

For every value or superiority claim:

1. Extract the claim.
2. Locate its named source in **Value and proof**.
3. Confirm the source is real user-provided evidence rather than an inference, hypothesis, or fictional example.
4. Confirm the source actually supports the scope of the claim.

A hypothesis label is honest, but it is not proof. If a value claim lacks a real named source, return `UNRESOLVED EVIDENCE GAP`.

### Repair route

1. Return to **Value and proof**.
2. Name the actual source, narrow the claim to match the evidence, or remove the claim.
3. Update affected **Translate features into benefits** rows.
4. Regenerate summaries, headlines, differentiators, and the final statement wherever the claim appears.
5. Rerun Proof.

If the user confirms that no customer, pilot, benchmark, or internal outcome record exists, keep the result `UNRESOLVED EVIDENCE GAP` and produce a **Proof validation plan**. For each unproven claim already in the ledger, specify:

- the baseline to capture before onboarding or intervention;
- the follow-up metric and observation window;
- the inspectable source or record owner;
- what evidence would support or reject the claim.

Do not invent a target percentage, savings amount, or success threshold. Separate product-mechanics facts from outcome measurements. Continue the remaining pressure-test checks after the plan is recorded.

### Example verdict

> Fictional example — illustrative only, not your answer

**UNRESOLVED EVIDENCE GAP** — ExampleCo Legal’s claims about spotting coordination risks and preparing updates more easily are fictional value hypotheses. No real customer, pilot, benchmark, or internal dataset exists for this teaching example.

**Route:** Revisit **Value and proof**. In a real company, collect and name pilot data, customer evidence, or an internal benchmark before publishing those claims.

## Check 3.5 — Trace

### Original question

> Can every blank in the final statement be traced back to a Phase 1 section?

### It fails when

> Something was filled in from intuition.

### Evaluation rules

Create this source map:

| Final-statement line | Required Phase 1 source |
|---|---|
| For | Target customers + Personas |
| who | Industry problems + Background or Switching dynamics |
| is a | Product type or Market categories research |
| that helps | Map pains to needs + Value and proof |
| unlike | Competitive alternatives: Primary comparator |
| we | Competitive alternatives + Unique attributes |

Return `PASS` only when every line points to approved content. An inference, fictional example, or newly invented synthesis phrase returns `FAIL`.

### Repair route

1. Identify the untraceable line.
2. Return to its required Phase 1 source.
3. Ask the user to approve, revise, mark as hypothesis, or leave it open.
4. Regenerate every dependent Phase 2 section.
5. Rerun Trace.

### Example verdict

> Fictional example — illustrative only, not your answer

**PASS within the fictional framework** — Audience traces to Target customers and Personas; the scattered-context problem traces to Industry problems; category traces to Product type and category research; the job traces to Pains to needs; the comparator traces to Competitive alternatives; and the live-matter-plan mechanism traces to Unique attributes.

This verifies structural traceability only. It does not convert fictional content into real evidence.

## Check 3.6 — Their words

### Original question

> Is the language from the customer-language section, or ours?

### It fails when

> It reads like the industry, not the buyer.

### Evaluation rules

- Compare the final messaging with the real phrases recorded in **Customer language**.
- Confirm that any claimed customer phrase is verbatim and attributable.
- Check that internal product terminology, category jargon, and consultant language have not displaced the buyer’s vocabulary.
- If the customer-language section was correctly skipped because no real quotes exist, return `UNRESOLVED EVIDENCE GAP`, not a fabricated pass.

### Repair route

1. Return to **Customer language**.
2. Add only real, attributed phrases from interviews, sales calls, emails, reviews, or support conversations.
3. Update **Industry problems** if customers frame the pain differently.
4. Regenerate summaries, headlines, core narrative, memorability language, and final positioning.
5. Rerun Their words.

### Example verdict

> Fictional example — illustrative only, not your answer

**UNRESOLVED EVIDENCE GAP** — Phrases such as “one live matter plan” and “keep every matter moving” were invented for ExampleCo Legal. They have not come from real customers.

**Route:** Revisit **Customer language**. In a real company, replace or validate these phrases through attributed customer research.
