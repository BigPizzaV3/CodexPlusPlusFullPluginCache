# Jurisdictional, labor-law, permit, and health-department research

Use this reference only when the main Skill's NCAP-first scope gate activates government/legal research. A named camp location by itself is not enough. Ordinary NCAP staffing, qualification, ratio, age-eligibility, or program-operation questions should remain focused on Scouting America sources unless a legal/regulatory issue is actually material.

## 1. Establish the physical jurisdiction

Determine where the activity actually occurs. Do not use the council office location unless the operation occurs there.

As needed, determine:

- physical street address or identifiable property location
- municipality or unincorporated area
- county
- state
- relevant special district (fire-protection district, park district, etc.)

Then identify the actual agencies having jurisdiction.

## 2. Research hierarchy

Prefer official primary sources in this order when available:

1. Federal statute/regulation and responsible federal agency
2. State statute or administrative code
3. State agency official guidance/forms
4. County code and county agency/health department
5. Municipal code/agency
6. Special-district or fire-protection authority
7. Official permit/license conditions for the specific facility

Secondary sources may help find the right authority but should not establish the legal requirement if the primary source is available.

## 3. Employment and seasonal camp staffing

Research only the issues implicated by the user's facts.

### Federal baseline

Consider the Fair Labor Standards Act and U.S. Department of Labor guidance, including:

- minimum wage
- overtime
- recordkeeping
- child labor
- employee versus volunteer status
- salary/exempt classification
- possible seasonal amusement/recreational establishment exemptions

Never assume a Scout camp qualifies for a seasonal/recreational exemption. Verify the statutory/regulatory test and the camp's actual operating facts. Also verify whether the exemption affects minimum wage, overtime, or both and whether state law recognizes or overrides the federal result.

### State and local overlay

Check the state labor agency and current statutes/rules for:

- minimum wage higher than federal
- overtime rules that differ from federal
- exemptions narrower than federal
- minor/youth work-hour limits
- school-day/school-week limits where relevant
- prohibited occupations for minors
- restrictions on driving, tractors, mowers, power-driven equipment, kitchen equipment, construction/maintenance tools, waterfront work, or other hazardous tasks where relevant
- work permits, employment certificates, or age certificates
- meal/rest/day-of-rest rules
- paid sick leave, paid leave, or local leave ordinances
- wage-payment timing and final-pay requirements when relevant
- lawful deductions or credits for lodging, meals, uniforms, or camp-provided items
- required payroll records and workplace posters
- workers' compensation coverage
- unemployment insurance or seasonal-employer treatment where relevant

If a local government can adopt a higher wage or employment rule, check the municipality/county where the camp is located.

### Volunteers and mixed paid/unpaid service

For nonprofit organizations, do not assume any unpaid service is lawful simply because the organization is nonprofit. When relevant, verify:

- whether the person is genuinely volunteering for civic/charitable reasons;
- whether they are otherwise employed by the organization;
- whether they are volunteering the same type of services they are paid to perform;
- whether state law adds restrictions.

### Housing, meals, and residential staff

When staff live/eat at camp, consider whether the question implicates:

- wage credits/deductions for lodging or meals
- written authorization requirements
- valuation limits
- payroll treatment
- housing code, sanitation, or youth-camp rules

Do not assume room and board can automatically reduce cash wages.

## 4. Camp operating permits and health regulation

Depending on the jurisdiction and operation, research only relevant categories:

### Youth/recreational camp licensing

Determine whether the state or county licenses or permits youth camps, organized camps, recreational camps, day camps, or residential camps. Check:

- definitions and applicability thresholds
- annual application/renewal
- inspection requirements
- health officer/medical staffing rules
- sanitation and sleeping-facility standards
- emergency-plan requirements
- water/sewage requirements
- communicable-disease rules
- incident-reporting requirements

### Food service

Check whether the camp dining hall, trading post, commissary, mobile food unit, temporary food operation, or unit cooking model requires:

- food-establishment permit
- certified food-protection manager
- food-handler training
- inspection
- temperature control/sanitation rules
- temporary-event permit

Do not assume a youth-camp license automatically covers food service.

### Pools, beaches, and waterfronts

Check state/local definitions for public/semi-public pools, bathing beaches, swimming facilities, or recreational water venues. Research:

- operating permit/license
- inspection frequency
- water-quality testing
- lifeguard or attendant rules imposed by law
- required rescue equipment/signage
- closures and reporting

Keep these government rules separate from Scouting America's aquatics standards.

### Drinking water and wastewater

Where relevant, identify rules for:

- public/non-community water systems
- private wells
- seasonal water systems
- testing/sample schedules
- septic/wastewater systems
- gray water and sanitation

### Fire and occupancy

Identify the applicable fire authority and, when relevant:

- annual fire inspection
- occupancy classification/capacity
- alarms/extinguishers
- commercial kitchen suppression
- open burning/campfires
- burn bans
- fuel storage
- propane
- fireworks
- evacuation requirements
- temporary structures/tents

### Program-specific government regulation

Research as applicable:

- boating/watercraft/operator requirements
- fishing/hunting/wildlife permits
- firearms and ammunition law
- shooting-range regulation
- archery-specific regulation if any
- climbing wall, zip line, challenge course, aerial adventure, or amusement-ride regulation
- ATV/OHV operation
- pesticide application
- equestrian operation
- transport/driver/CDL rules

Do not mention categories unrelated to the user's actual program.

## 5. Resolve multiple authorities

A single operation may need to satisfy Scouting America requirements and multiple government layers simultaneously.

When rules differ:

- identify each authority separately;
- explain whether both apply;
- do not claim one automatically preempts another unless the source supports that conclusion;
- where the more protective/restrictive rule controls in practice, explain why;
- flag questions that require an agency or attorney determination.

## 6. Time sensitivity

Labor rates, permit requirements, administrative code, agency interpretations, and local ordinances change. For current questions:

- verify current effective dates;
- use exact dates when a future change is scheduled;
- do not rely on archived agency pages without checking current law;
- if a permit is property-specific, distinguish general code from the actual permit condition.

## 7. Legal framing

Provide practical regulatory research, not definitive legal advice. Avoid excessive disclaimers; one concise qualification is enough when needed.

Use language such as:

- “The current Illinois rule requires…” when the official source is clear.
- “This appears to fall within…” when applying a definition to uncertain facts.
- “The statute is not explicit on this camp scenario; the safer interpretation is…” for genuine ambiguity.
- “Confirm with the county health department before relying on this interpretation” when the authority having jurisdiction must decide.
