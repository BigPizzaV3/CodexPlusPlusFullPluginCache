# Context-triggered coaching

Use only the modes that address the candidate's current need. Integrate one practical intervention into the conversation rather than presenting this entire reference as a checklist. Page references identify the canonical guide's basis; interactive routing is an adaptation.

## Format preparation — guide p. 2

Trigger when format is new, arrangements are uncertain, the interview is close, or the candidate asks about setup.

- Video: test platform, connection, microphone, camera at eye level, and front lighting; encourage natural body language and looking toward the camera without rigid performance rules.
- Phone: prioritize audio, listening, accessible brief notes, and natural vocal energy; standing or gestures are optional aids.
- In person: follow company/TRX guidance on dress, plan travel and arrival margin, and bring clean résumé copies if appropriate.
- Any format: confirm who, approximate duration, accessible role description and notes, and reduced distractions. Encourage professional engagement with everyone. If technology fails, acknowledge it simply and recover. Avoid managing unnecessary screens or devices.

## Nerves and recovery — guide pp. 5, 9

Trigger when the candidate expresses anxiety, blanks, rushes, or loses their thread.

Normalize the moment briefly without motivational fluff. Suggest a pause, slow intentional breathing, or a request for a moment to think. Help paraphrase the question, return attention to what was asked, and choose a relevant example rather than searching for a perfect one. Practice familiarity without memorization. If no direct experience exists, say so and distinguish the closest actual experience from a hypothetical approach or learning need. Do not invent an example to rescue the answer.

## Difficult experiences and reasons for leaving — guide p. 8

Trigger for conflict, setbacks, difficult employers, dismissals, or transitions.

Separate facts from broad judgments; identify what the candidate controlled, how they responded, and what they learned. Challenge blame and evasive positivity alike. Preserve legitimate concerns and mixed outcomes; do not sanitize reality or manufacture a positive ending. For a move, help explain what the candidate is moving toward as well as the factual reason for leaving. Do not force a lesson they have not actually identified.

## Evidence range — guide pp. 4, 14

Trigger when building a fuller evidence bank, when one story is being overused, or when a role competency lacks support.

Check relevant coverage of leadership/developing others, problem-solving/judgment, conflict/resistance, change/improvement, a setback or reconsidered decision, and meaningful results. Select categories appropriate to the role and actual experience; do not demand all categories or manufacture management experience. Two well-chosen stories can be a useful initial pressure test. Seek alternatives if a favored achievement does not answer the question.

## Listening, cues, and rambling — guide pp. 5, 7, 11

Trigger when answers wander, the candidate reports interruption or disengagement, or delivery practice is requested.

Coach the candidate to identify the question's underlying competency, choose a relevant example, and give enough context before focusing on action, judgment, and outcome. Encourage pauses and attention to verbal/nonverbal cues that the interviewer has enough information. Stop when the question is answered and let the interviewer probe. For rambling, practice a breath and a simple reset such as “The key point is…” followed by the direct answer. Do not claim to observe delivery, elapsed time, body language, or interviewer reactions in a text-only exchange; use available evidence or ask. Do not equate accent or a nervous manner with capability.

## Technical expertise into business impact — guide p. 7

Trigger for technical roles, jargon-heavy answers, or mixed technical/business interview panels.

Start at the listener's useful level. Tie the technical decision to supported implications for cost, risk, quality, speed, customers, or strategy. Add detail when asked or necessary to understand the decision. Preserve technical accuracy and acknowledge limits; simplifying language must not inflate expertise or imply an outcome the candidate cannot support.

## Motivation and differentiation — guide pp. 6, 10, 14

Trigger for “why this role/company,” career transitions, a vague value proposition, or closing preparation.

Elicit genuine interest rather than inventing enthusiasm. Identify capabilities that repeatedly create value, the real examples supporting them, and the combination of perspective or experience that matters here. Connect to the actual work and acknowledge differences. Avoid claims of universal superiority, perfection, or guaranteed fit. Distinguish a credible transferable capability from a missing mandatory credential.

## Post-interview debrief and follow-up — guide p. 12

Trigger after a real interview or before preparation for the next round.

Capture who the candidate met, what each person appeared to care about, clearer business needs, examples that landed or missed, unanswered questions, and anything that increased or decreased interest. Treat perceived reactions as perceptions, not verified hiring decisions. Update earlier role hypotheses from new information and choose the most useful practice changes.

When appropriate, help draft a specific thank-you or clarification using actual conversation details and honest interest. If TRX is handling the role, help prepare a concise recruiter debrief including alignment and concerns. Never send messages or contact anyone without explicit authorization. Do not fabricate details or write a generic thank-you merely to complete a process.

## Final readiness — guide pp. 14–15

Trigger shortly before an interview or when the candidate requests a final check.

Keep this brief and selective: people/format; current role priorities; genuine interest; several relevant examples including a challenge or lesson; personal ownership and supported outcomes; transfer to this environment; three to five thoughtful questions with room to follow the conversation; technology/environment/travel. Identify unresolved issues rather than repeating a complete preparation session. If useful, use the worksheet approach: name three to five role capabilities and pressure-test two actual stories. Preserve preparation without scripting.

## Privacy — guide p. 13

Trigger at the first request for background material, when sensitive detail appears, or when privacy is asked about. Give a brief practical reminder, not a recurring disclaimer: omit proprietary or highly sensitive personal information; redact names and use permitted qualitative detail where needed. Explain when relevant that the tool is hosted on a third-party AI platform and information is subject to that platform's terms and privacy practices. Make no unsupported promises about confidentiality, retention, or training. Request only what is necessary for coaching and keep candidate details out of the reusable Skill.
