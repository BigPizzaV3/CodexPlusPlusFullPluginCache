## Comedy, satire and irony

### Name the Claim Before the Carrier

**Also called:** the theme selector, claim before carrier, what is this actually arguing
**What it is:** The card at the head of the `theme.` shelf. It does not supply a claim and it does not rank them. It asks the three questions this shelf's own pair graph asks over and over — **who owns the claim before you write it, what carries the proof, and what the last scene does with it** — and hands back a named shortlist for each answer. The axes were not invented: they were read off the shelf's `Yields to:` conditions and its `PAIR-AUDIT.md` rows, and the conditions that repeated became the questions. Every card cited here is cited by id.
**Effect on the work:** None on the screen. This card never reaches it. It exists so that the claim a piece ends up proving was chosen against the commission, the cast and the ending, rather than inherited from whatever the writer already believed on the day.
**Used for and where it works best:** **After a first draft exists, never before.** Answer the three questions in order, one sentence each, out loud. Question one usually returns nothing, and that is information rather than a failure of the map: it means the claim is unowned, which is the hardest case and not the easiest. Question two is the one that eliminates most of the shelf, because the cast and the medium have already half-answered it for you. Question three is asked last because it reaches the verdict cards and nothing else does. Where two claims survive question one, that is two pieces — `theme.competing_themes` names what happens if you keep both.
**Best in:** formats: all | genres: all
**Avoid when:** **There is no draft yet.** The two most-routed-to cards on this shelf both say the claim is discovered by writing — `theme.controlling_idea` (*write it after the first draft, not before*) and `theme.thematic_audit` (*avoid it on the first draft, where auditing while generating stops the generating*). **A map consulted before a draft exists produces `theme.decided_before_story`, which is a named failure on this very shelf**, and the map cannot see itself doing it. Also **distrust axis one whenever your honest answer is its first value** — twenty-three of the thirty-three forked cards live there, so on an original drama in your own language the first question returns nothing and this map has two questions left. And **the map deliberately does not offer the cards that are added rather than chosen**; a reader working only from the axis tables never adds an object, a motif, a title, a counter-example or an opponent, and the missing opponent is exactly what this shelf names as the commonest structural cause of preachiness. Finally, `theme.anti_theme` carries an ethical limit rather than a card in its alternative slot. Route to it and read it there; that limit is the card's to state, and restating it here would weaken it.
**Example:**
> **Owns it:** a source text, and an estate that will not licence a changed claim.
> **Carries it:** a two-hander — so not the ensemble and not the spread cast; a choice with a price.
> **Ending:** proved, with the bill on screen.
> Shortlist: `theme.adaptation_claim` · `theme.moral_test` · `theme.irony_as_argument`, with `theme.anti_theme` added on top of whichever wins.

**The axes**

**Axis 1 — Who owns the claim before you write it?**
Asked first because it is the only question that can disqualify a claim outright, and because eight of the shelf's live conditions turn on it: *"Commissioned advocacy or campaign: the position is the brief"* (`theme.start_from_a_question`), *"One-off tactical execution with no future"* (`theme.organising_idea`), *"Rights, estate or contract forbid changing the claim"* (`theme.adaptation_shift`), *"Insiders are trying to tell this identity story"* (`theme.from_outside`), *"Satire with a declared target: nothing is exempt"* (`theme.sacred_material`), *"The source language itself is the subject"* (`theme.translation_survival`), *"Short or instructional: carry the claim in action"* (`theme.untranslatable_value`), and *"When the claim can safely be made directly"* (`theme.allegory_build`) — which is the same question asked by a writer working under a censor.

**Axis 2 — What carries the proof: a person, a scene, a choice, a cast, or the form?**
Seven conditions turn on this, and every one of them is a fact about the production rather than a feeling about the result: *"Two-hander or single protagonist"* (`theme.through_the_ensemble`), *"Ensemble or mosaic with no single protagonist"* (`theme.arc_carries_claim`), *"Mosaic of unrelated lives: the form carries the claim"* (`theme.thematic_value`), *"Flat arc: the test lands on the world"* (`theme.test_on_protagonist`), *"The writer has a verdict: prove it by choice"* (`theme.argument_scene`), *"Decorative interaction: stage the choice in the fiction"* (`theme.choice_as_argument`), *"Literary or arthouse: a stated claim intrudes"* (`theme.stated_rejected`). The register cards belong here too, rather than to a separate audience axis, because in comedy, horror, a music video and a game **the register is the carrier** — the premise, the creature's rules, the image set against the lyric, the player's hand.

**Axis 3 — What does the last scene do with the claim?**
Only two conditions turn on it — *"Genre contracts promising resolution: commit to a shape"* (`theme.earned_ambiguity`) and *"Serialised work before its final season"* (`theme.moral_shape_of_ending`) — and it is kept, despite being the thinnest axis here, because it is the only one that reaches the verdict cards at all, and because `theme.moral_shape_of_ending` says in its own text that endings drift shape more easily than they drift content. It is asked last for the same reason: it constrains every other card by complement and disqualifies almost none.

**The default, and the one against it**

**The default is `theme.controlling_idea` governing the piece with `theme.anti_theme` living inside it — and it is measured, not asserted.** These two are the most-routed-to cards on the shelf and they tie. Three cards send a reader to `theme.controlling_idea`: `theme.allegory_build` (*"when the claim can safely be made directly"*), `theme.preschool_claim` (*"middle grade and up: let the claim stay unstated"*) and `theme.subject_mistaken_for_theme` (*"repair: write the causal sentence"*). Three send a reader to `theme.anti_theme`: `theme.fable_contract` (*"adult audience expecting an arguable moral, a trial"*), `theme.rigged_argument` and `theme.propaganda`, the last two naming it as the whole repair. Nothing else on the shelf is named more than twice. Asked fifty-five times where to fall back to, the shelf falls back to *one causal sentence with a real opponent inside it*. That is also the pairing the shelf's own opening paragraph states as its thesis, and the pair graph turns out to agree with the prose — which is not guaranteed and is worth recording, because on `light.` it did not.

**The one against it is `theme.comic_premise_argument`.** Nothing routes to it; it is named by no `Yields to:` anywhere on the shelf; and it is the only card here in which the argument has **no advocate, no opponent and no trial**. The world runs on one wrong rule, everybody obeys it, nobody may notice it is absurd, and the claim is proved by the obedience. Every other card on the shelf assumes the default's geometry — a claim, a defence, a verdict — and this one deletes the defence on purpose. Its structural signature is indistinguishable from `theme.propaganda`, whose own stated diagnostic is `theme.thematic_audit`: a comedy built this way has no scene marked *contradicts*, which is the test `theme.propaganda` calls the reliable one, and it is correct anyway. The cost is stated precisely and it is small: the moment one character says the rule is absurd, the comedy becomes commentary. **Its near cousin against the grain is `theme.irony_as_argument`**, likewise routed to by nothing, which argues by granting the character the thing they wanted rather than by staging a trial over it.

### Theme in Comedy: the Premise Is the Argument

**Also called:** the comic premise as claim, the world's stupid rule
**What it is:** In comedy the argument is carried by the premise — the one wrong rule the world runs on — rather than by any character's position. The story proves the claim by showing the rule working and everybody obeying it.
**Effect on the audience:** They laugh at the rule and leave holding the claim, without ever being argued with. Comedy is the only register where an audience will accept a thematic verdict they would resist in drama, because the verdict arrived as a joke.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any comedy you want to be about something. Write the premise as a rule, not as a situation: *in this office, the person who does the least is the hardest to fire* is a premise; *an office comedy* is a setting. Then the comic engine and the argument are the same machine, and every escalation proves the claim. The craft rule: nobody in the world may notice the rule is absurd. The moment one character says so, the comedy becomes commentary. Pairs with `comedy.airtight_wrong_logic` and `comedy.game_of_the_scene`.
**Best in:** formats: Half-Hour Comedy, Feature Film, Sketch, Animated Series, Web Series, Stage Play | genres: Comedy, Satire, Farce, Workplace, Dark Comedy, Political
**Avoid when:** The comedy's contract is pure pleasure — most farce, most buddy work, most sitcoms sold on cast chemistry. Forcing a claim onto them slows the engine and buys nothing.
**Example:**
> **Situation:** a comedy set in a hospital records office.
> **Premise:** *nothing here is real until it has been photocopied.*
> A man is declared dead by a jammed machine, and the funniest scene in the film is four people agreeing, sincerely, that he cannot be alive because there is no copy.

### The Satirical Claim

**Also called:** what the satire is actually saying, the target behind the joke
**What it is:** The specific proposition a satire is advancing about its target, distinguished from the target itself. The target is who you are hitting; the claim is what you say is true about them.
**Effect on the audience:** It is the difference between a piece that lands and a piece that is merely rude. An audience forgives a hard joke that proves something and resents an equally hard joke that proves nothing.
**Used for and where it works best:** Before writing a line of satire. Write two sentences: *the target is X* and *the claim is that X does Y because Z.* If the second sentence will not come, you have mockery, not satire. The strongest satirical claims are mechanical rather than moral — not *these people are bad* but *this system rewards exactly the behaviour it publicly punishes*, because a mechanism can be dramatised and a verdict can only be asserted. Pairs with `comedy.satire_target` and `comedy.punch_direction`; the tonal question — teasing or furious — follows from how dangerous the target actually is, and misjudging it in either direction is the usual failure: gentle mockery of something genuinely harmful reads as complicity, and fury at something merely silly reads as self-importance.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Half-Hour Comedy, Sketch, Essay, Novel, Stand-Up Set, Animated Series | genres: Satire, Dark Comedy, Political, Comedy, Social Drama, Workplace
**Avoid when:** The target cannot answer back — a private person, a small community, anyone with less power than the audience. Satire is a weapon with a direction, and pointed downward it is just bullying with a literary name.
**Example:**
> **Target:** heritage-funding committees.
> **Weak claim:** they are pompous.
> **Working claim:** *a committee protects a building by making it impossible to use, then protects it from the disuse it caused.*
> The sketch: a restoration board grants the last village church permanent protected status, and the final condition is that no one may enter.

### Irony as the Thematic Instrument

**Also called:** structural irony, the gap that argues, the granted wish
**What it is:** Building the story so that outcome and intention diverge — the character gets exactly what they wanted and it destroys them, or the institution achieves its stated goal and produces its opposite. The gap between what was meant and what occurred *is* the thematic statement; no one has to phrase it.
**Effect on the audience:** They do the work, and meaning they assemble is held far longer than meaning they are handed. Irony demands active reading, and an audience that has completed the argument themselves defends it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any claim of the form *X produces the opposite of X.* This is the single most reliable way to argue a theme without a line of thematic dialogue, and it is why so many strong endings are grants rather than denials: give the protagonist the thing, on the terms the story has been describing, and let the audience price it. Place the grant late, after the cost has been fully established — early, it reads as a twist. See `scene.granted_wish` for the scene-level form and `info.dramatic_irony` for the information-level one.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Short Story, Limited Series, Stage Play, Short Film | genres: Tragedy, Satire, Drama, Crime, Noir, Literary, Dark Comedy, Political
**Avoid when:** Work that needs the audience to feel accompanied rather than observed — most kids' content, most advocacy, most romance. Irony installs distance, and distance is the wrong instrument when the audience needs warmth. It also collapses if any character names it; an ironic outcome remarked upon is a joke about the story.
**Example:**
> He spends the film fighting to keep the family name on the shop.
> In the last scene the buyer keeps the name — because it tests well — and sacks him.
> Nobody in the scene mentions the name. The new sign goes up in the final shot, correctly spelled for the first time in forty years.

**Yields to:** `theme.realisation_not_lesson` — Kids work needs warmth, not ironic distance.

### Satire That Flatters

**Also called:** preaching to the converted, the absent target, the applause joke
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** Satire aimed at a target the audience already despises and who is not in the room, so that every joke confirms what the audience arrived believing and costs them nothing.
**Effect on the audience:** Pleasure without pressure. They laugh, feel clever, and change nothing — and the piece has the shape of satire while performing the opposite function, which is reassurance.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere as a goal, though it is the commercial default for topical comedy, where the audience is sold by agreement. The diagnostic is brutal and reliable: name the person in your audience who is implicated by the piece. If there isn't one, you have written a rally.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (most common, and most rewarded, in topical panel comedy, monologue segments and social-media satire)
**Avoid when:** Always, where the intent is actually satirical. The repair is to find the version of the claim that includes the audience: keep the target, and add the scene in which the audience's own behaviour is the mechanism that keeps the target in place.
**Example:**
> **Flattering:** the developer is a cartoon, and the audience boos.
> **Working:** the same developer, plus a two-line scene in which the neighbourhood association votes down the affordable flats and the developer is the only person in the room who looks surprised.

**Yields to:** `theme.satire_claim` — Repair: a mechanical claim that implicates the audience.

## Allegory and fable

### Building an Allegory

**Also called:** the double story, the two-level narrative, surface-first construction
**What it is:** A narrative whose characters, events and places correspond to a second system — political, moral, spiritual — so the story means one thing literally and another thing by structure.
**Effect on the audience:** Recognition, arriving as a small shock partway through. The pleasure of allegory is the moment the second system comes into focus, which is why the second system must never be announced.
**Used for and where it works best:** Claims too dangerous, too large or too abstract to state directly — political work under censorship, heritage material about a living dispute, science fiction about the present. The construction rule that decides everything: **write the surface story as if the allegory were irrelevant, finish it, and only then check whether the mapping holds.** A surface story built to serve the mapping is always the weaker of the two, and the audience can feel the scaffolding. Keep the correspondence loose rather than exact: the tighter the one-to-one mapping between symbol and referent, the more fragile the whole thing becomes, because a single element that does not fit invalidates the reading. Aim for a structure that rhymes with the second system rather than transcribing it.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Animation Feature, Short Story, Stage Play, Comics, Picture Book | genres: Fable, Myth, Satire, Science Fiction, Fantasy, Political, Dystopia, Heritage
**Avoid when:** The claim can safely be made directly. Allegory costs the audience effort and costs the writer the literal register, and paying that price to say something you were free to say plainly is a bad trade. Also avoid where the work will be read by people with no access to the second system — an allegory nobody can decode is simply a strange story.
**Example:**
> **Surface:** a village argues for a year about who owns the water in a shared cistern, and the cistern cracks while they argue.
> **Second system:** a named territorial dispute the writer cannot film.
> The surface story is finished, cast, and dramatically complete before anyone checks the mapping — and in the final draft two characters deliberately have no counterpart, because the loose fit is what keeps it a story.

**Yields to:** `theme.controlling_idea` — When the claim can safely be made directly.  
`theme.allegory_build`

### The Fable Contract

**Also called:** the declared moral, the beast tale, parable construction
**What it is:** The form in which the moral may be stated outright, because the audience has agreed to it in advance: short, plain, animal or archetype characters carrying a single trait each, and a lesson delivered as the last line.
**Effect on the audience:** Portability. A fable survives retelling by people who cannot remember the wording, which is why fables outlive the cultures that made them.
**Used for and where it works best:** Kids' content, heritage and oral-tradition material, brand parables, and any piece meant to be retold rather than experienced once. Three rules make it work. Each character carries exactly one trait and is not given an interior — a fable fox with a childhood is a short story, and a worse one. The situation must be a single test, not a plot. And the stated moral must be *smaller* than the story: a fable that ends by claiming more than it showed collapses. Note that this is the licensed exception to `theme.descriptive_not_prescriptive`, to `theme.explained_theme` and to the no-*contradicts* test in `theme.propaganda` — the form declares itself, so the instruction is the deliverable.
**Best in:** formats: Picture Book, Kids Story, Short Story, Animation Feature, Animated Series, Stage Play, Podcast | genres: Fable, Myth, Kids, Heritage, Family Drama, Comedy, Faith
**Avoid when:** Naturalistic drama of any length. The fable's characters are positions, and a position sustained past about fifteen minutes becomes `char.mouthpiece`. Also avoid where the audience expects the moral to be arguable — a fable does not conduct a trial, it reports a verdict, and an adult audience that wanted a trial will read the verdict as a lecture.
**Example:**
> The jackal offers to carry the tortoise across, twice, and is refused twice.
> The third time the tortoise agrees, because the water is higher.
> "Nobody drowns from the first offer."

**Yields to:** `theme.anti_theme` — Adult audience expecting an arguable moral, a trial.

### The Keyed Allegory

**Also called:** the decoder story, roman à clef rigidity, the one-to-one trap
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** An allegory in which every element maps to exactly one real figure or event, so the story's whole function is to be decoded — and, once decoded, has nothing left to give.
**Effect on the audience:** A single click of recognition followed by boredom. The reader stops watching the story and starts checking the key, and any element that does not map reads as an error rather than as life.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere as a design, with one narrow exception: short satire written for an audience and a week, where disposability is acceptable and the click is the whole joke. In anything meant to last, the rigidity is fatal — the tighter the correspondence, the faster the work expires with the situation that produced it.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (hurts most in features, novels and heritage work, which are read after the key is forgotten)
**Avoid when:** Always in long form. The repair is deliberate slack: give two or three significant elements no counterpart at all, let one character map to two real people, and make sure the surface story would still be worth watching by someone who has never heard of the referents. See `theme.allegory_build`.
**Example:**
> **Keyed:** seven animals on a council, each with the initials, tics and documented gaffes of a sitting minister. Funny in March, unreadable in June.
> **Working:** four animals on a council, one of whom is nobody, and whose presence is never explained.

**Yields to:** `theme.allegory_build` — Repair: deliberate slack in the mapping.  
`theme.keyed_allegory`

## Theme for children and young audiences

### The Preschool Claim

**Also called:** the one-idea rule, the concrete theme, the behavioural claim
**What it is:** A single claim, stated in concrete and behavioural terms rather than abstract ones, that the whole piece proves once. *Sharing is good* is abstract. *If you hand somebody the red one, you get to play with them* is a preschool claim.
**Effect on the audience:** Comprehension and transfer. A three-year-old cannot generalise from an abstraction but can copy an action, so the claim must be made of actions.
**Used for and where it works best:** Preschool series, picture books, kids' songs, and anything with a curriculum brief behind it. Three rules. One claim per piece — a preschool episode carrying two claims teaches neither. The claim must be provable inside one situation, because a young audience cannot hold a comparison across a gap. And the proof must be visible: the character does the thing, and the good outcome happens in the next shot, not three scenes later. What preschool audiences reliably care about is the relationship level — friendship, being included, being looked after — so state the claim in those terms rather than in terms of rules. Pairs with `voice.kids_register` and `lyric.kids_repetition_contract`.
**Best in:** formats: Preschool, Picture Book, Kids Story, Kids Song, Animated Series, Educational | genres: Preschool, Kids, Educational, Family Drama, Fable
**Avoid when:** Middle grade and up, where the concrete claim reads as condescension and the audience is already capable of the abstraction. There, move to `theme.controlling_idea` and let the claim stay unstated.
**Example:**
> **Abstract:** patience is a virtue.
> **Preschool claim:** *if you wait until the bird stops flapping, it lets you look at it.*
> The whole episode is one nest, one loud child, and one bird, and the child gets to look at the bird exactly when they stop shouting.

**Yields to:** `theme.controlling_idea` — Middle grade and up: let the claim stay unstated.  
`theme.preschool_claim`

### Show the Realisation, Not the Lesson

**Also called:** the unspoken learn, letting the child do the last step
**What it is:** The character visibly realises something and then acts on the realisation — and nobody, including the narrator, says what was realised.
**Effect on the audience:** The child completes the sentence, which is the entire mechanism by which children's stories teach anything. A lesson stated is a lesson received passively; a lesson inferred is one the child has already practised.
**Used for and where it works best:** Picture books, kids' animation and family features — everywhere except the explicitly instructional. The staging rule: the realisation needs a physical beat to land on. A pause, a look back at the thing, a hand stopping halfway. Without that beat the change reads as arbitrary; with it, no words are needed. Then have the character do the opposite of what they did in the opening, and stop. Pairs with `sub.kid_readable_subtext` and `emo.sentimentality` as the thing to avoid on the way out.
**Best in:** formats: Picture Book, Kids Story, Animation Feature, Animated Series, Kids Song, Short Film, Middle Grade | genres: Kids, Family Drama, Coming of Age, Fable, Comedy, Adventure
**Avoid when:** Safety, health and explicitly instructional content, where the instruction must be unambiguous and inference is a risk. Also avoid in the youngest preschool band, where the realisation beat may genuinely not read and restatement is pedagogy rather than failure.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "I learned that it's better to ask for help!"
> **Working:** She reaches for the top shelf, stops, looks at the jar, looks at her brother, and holds out the jar. He opens it. She takes the first biscuit and gives him the second.

**Yields to:** `theme.explained_theme` — Instructional, safety, youngest preschool: restatement is pedagogy.  
`theme.realisation_not_lesson`

### The Tacked-On Moral

**Also called:** the last-page lesson, the stapled message, "and the moral of the story is"
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A claim added at the end of a children's piece that the story did not actually prove — usually because the story was built for fun and a moral was required by a commissioner, a parent-facing blurb, or the writer's nerve failing on the last page.
**Effect on the audience:** Children detect it faster than adults do, and what they learn is that the story was a delivery mechanism. The second effect is worse: it retroactively devalues the twelve minutes of genuine play that preceded it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere. Note carefully that this is *not* the same as `theme.fable_contract`, where the moral is stated because the form promised one and the story proved it. The failure is specifically a stated moral the events do not support. The diagnostic: delete the last page. If nothing is lost, the moral was tacked on; if the story now has no ending, it was structural.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always. The repair is one of two things — either build the claim into the situation from page one (`theme.preschool_claim`), or drop the claim and let the piece be what it is. A funny book about a duck is allowed to be a funny book about a duck.
**Example:**
> **Tacked on:** eleven pages of a duck stealing hats. Page twelve: "And Duck learned that taking things that don't belong to you makes everybody sad."
> **Structural:** the duck's last stolen hat is his own, blown off in page three and forgotten, and he gives it back to himself, and that is the joke and the claim at once.

**Yields to:** `theme.preschool_claim` — Repair: build the claim in from page one.  
`theme.tacked_on_moral`

## Brand, advertising and commissioned work

### The Single Organising Idea

**Also called:** the big organising idea, the brand's one claim, the platform idea
**What it is:** One sentence that every piece of a brand's output argues, positioned upstream of any campaign — closer to a controlling idea than to a slogan, and written to be acted on internally rather than said to a customer.
**Effect on the audience:** It makes a body of work cohere across agencies, years and formats, and it decides briefs. Without it, each film argues something slightly different and the brand accumulates into noise.
**Used for and where it works best:** At the start of any brand-film, series-of-ads or long content relationship — and it is the correct first question to a client who has asked for "a video". Four tests, applied in order, and a candidate must pass all four: is it **big** enough to still be true in five years, **simple** enough to be repeated without the deck, **different** enough that a competitor could not sign it, and **true** enough that the company's own behaviour already supports it. The fourth is the one that kills most candidates, and killing them there is cheaper than killing them after production. Pairs with `voice.brand_voice_intake` and `end.brand_film_close`.
**Best in:** formats: Brand Film, Advertising, Product Video, Corporate, Explainer, Pitch Document, Trailer | genres: Advocacy, Lifestyle, Workplace, Educational, Science
**Avoid when:** Single tactical executions with no future — a one-off product announcement, a seasonal promo, a UGC test. Building a brand platform to make one 15-second video is a way of spending the client's money on a document.
**Example:**
> **Slogan:** *Built for the journey.*
> **Organising idea:** *we make the thing that is still working when the trip goes wrong* — which briefs a film about a breakdown, not a film about a sunset, and which the warranty department can also act on.

**Yields to:** `theme.implicit_ad_claim` — One-off tactical execution with no future.  
`theme.organising_idea`

### The Ad's Implicit Claim

**Also called:** what the ad says about the viewer, the unwritten second argument
**What it is:** The claim an advertisement makes about the audience's life, as distinct from the claim it makes about the product. Every ad carries one whether or not it was written, because the situation you chose to depict is an assertion about what a normal life looks like.
**Effect on the audience:** It is the part they remember and the part they resent. Product claims are forgotten within the hour; the implicit claim — *you are failing at this, and here is the fix* — is what determines whether the brand is liked.
**Used for and where it works best:** Every brief, as a written line beside the product claim. State it deliberately: *this film says that a good father is one who is present at breakfast.* Seeing it written is usually enough to fix it, because the ones that damage a brand are almost never chosen — they leak in through casting, house size and who is shown doing the cleaning. The strongest advertising inverts the default: instead of asserting a lack the product fills, assert a competence the product serves. Pairs with `voice.advertising_register`.
**Best in:** formats: Advertising, Brand Film, Product Video, UGC Ad, PSA, Reels/Shorts, Carousel | genres: Lifestyle, Advocacy, Family Drama, Comedy, Sports, Educational
**Avoid when:** Nowhere — the card is a check, not an option. The only situation in which it does not apply is work with no depicted human situation at all, such as pure product photography or a spec sheet.
**Example:**
> **Leaked claim:** *your home is embarrassing.* — a spotless kitchen, a mother apologising for the state of it, the cleaner arriving.
> **Chosen claim:** *you already do the hard part; this does the boring part.* — the same kitchen, the same mother, and the first shot is her finishing something difficult.

`theme.implicit_ad_claim`

### Purpose-Washing

**Also called:** woke-washing, the borrowed cause, values the company does not hold
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A brand piece built on a social or moral claim the organisation's own behaviour does not support — a purpose written by the agency rather than found in the company.
**Effect on the audience:** Credibility damage that transfers to the product. Research on the effect is consistent: audiences already sceptical of brand purpose punish the mismatch harder than they would have punished silence, and the more involved the audience is with the cause, the worse the punishment.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere. It is listed because writers are regularly asked to produce it, and because the diagnostic is one the writer can run in the intake meeting rather than in the edit: ask what the company did about this cause in a year when it was not advertising. If the answer is a donation figure, it is thin; if the answer is an internal policy with a date, the claim is real. A writer who gets no answer should offer a smaller, defensible claim instead of accepting the large one.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2028-09. The measured audience reaction to brand-purpose claims moves with the sentiment cycle; the mechanism is durable but the severity figures and the vocabulary around it are not.
**Avoid when:** Always. Note that this is the commercial sibling of `theme.decided_before_story`, and shares its repair: start from what is actually true of the subject and find the claim that material supports.
**Example:**
> **Washing:** a two-minute film about craft and community for a company whose three local workshops closed last year.
> **Defensible:** a two-minute film about the one workshop that is still open, that says only what is true of it, and that does not use the word *community* once.

**Yields to:** `theme.organising_idea` — Repair: the claim the company's behaviour already supports.  
`theme.purpose_washing`

## Non-fiction and interactive work

### Keeping the Material That Contradicts You

**Also called:** the inconvenient interview, the honest cut, fairness over objectivity
**What it is:** Deliberately building into a documentary or piece of reportage the strongest material that undercuts its own claim, placed where it can do damage rather than buried in a montage.
**Effect on the audience:** It is the single largest determinant of whether a factual piece is believed. An audience that sees a film survive its own best counter-evidence extends trust to everything else in it; an audience that senses an unopposed case discounts the whole.
**Used for and where it works best:** Every documentary, heritage film and piece of long-form journalism with an argument. The craft rule is about placement, not inclusion: the contradicting material has to appear *before* the film's conclusion, so the conclusion is reached through it rather than around it. Twenty seconds in the last act is decoration. The related discipline is transparency — audiences judge a factual piece as fair when they can see how it was made, more reliably than when it claims to have no position. Documentary has no fact-check and no peer review, so the structure has to do that work. Extends `theme.theme_in_nonfiction` and pairs with `info.contradictory_sources` and `end.documentary_ending`.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary, Documentary Series, Heritage Documentary, Reportage, Essay Film, Podcast, Long-Form Non-Fiction | genres: Journalism, Advocacy, Heritage, Historical, Science, Political, Testimony
**Avoid when:** The "contradiction" is manufactured balance — giving equal time to a position with no evidential standing, which misinforms while appearing scrupulous. Fairness means representing real opposition accurately, not inventing an opponent for symmetry.
**Example:**
> The film argues the school was closed for political reasons.
> At minute 34 the former inspector produces the attendance registers, and they are catastrophic, and the film sits with them for ninety seconds before anyone answers.
> The answer, when it comes, is weaker than the registers. The film keeps both.

`theme.contradicting_material`

### The Player's Choice as the Argument

**Also called:** theme through agency, the interactive moral test, the argument you make the player conduct
**What it is:** Building the interactive work so that the audience's own decisions constitute the thematic argument — they are not shown that a value costs something, they are charged for it.
**Effect on the audience:** Complicity, which no other medium can produce. A player who chose the expedient option cannot tell themselves they would have done otherwise, and that is a kind of proof unavailable to a film.
**Used for and where it works best:** Game narrative, interactive fiction and immersive work with a serious claim. Three rules decide whether it works. The choice must be between two defensible goods, not good and evil, or the player is only being asked whether they want the good ending. The cost must be paid in a currency the player actually values — time, a resource, a character's presence — not in a stat. And the reward system must agree with the claim: if the fiction condemns violence while the mechanics pay for it, the mechanics win, every time, because that is what the player learns by repetition. See `inter.meaningful_choice_test`, `inter.no_good_option` and the failure it produces, `inter.ludonarrative_dissonance`.
**Best in:** formats: Game Narrative, Interactive Fiction, Visual Novel, Immersive, XR, Conversational Agent | genres: Drama, Survival, Science Fiction, Dystopia, Crime, War, Psychological, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The interaction is decorative — a dialogue wheel with no downstream state, a branch that reconverges in two beats. A choice with no consequence teaches the player that the work's moral vocabulary is scenery, and that lesson generalises to the parts that were sincere. See `inter.illusion_of_agency`.
**Example:**
> The claim: *scarcity turns care into arithmetic.*
> The game gives the player four doses and five sick colonists, no stat for compassion, and no way to check afterwards whether they chose correctly. The fifth colonist has been named since hour one, and does the tutorial voice.

**Yields to:** `theme.moral_test` — Decorative interaction: stage the choice in the fiction.  
`theme.choice_as_argument`

## Genre and form carrying the claim

### What the Monster Means

**Also called:** the horror metaphor, the embodied fear, monster as thesis
**What it is:** Designing the horror threat so that its rules, appetite and weakness spell out the story's claim — the monster is not a creature the theme is attached to, it is the theme given a body and a behaviour.
**Effect on the audience:** Dread that outlasts the film. A monster that means something keeps working after the lights come up, because the thing it stands for is still in the room.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any horror with ambitions past the scare. The design rule is that the *rules* carry the meaning, not the appearance: what the threat wants, what it will not do, what stops it, and what it costs to escape. A creature that only kills means nothing; a creature that takes the thing the protagonist was most willing to trade means the whole claim. The design order is fixed and writers break it constantly: **name the fear, then the method, then the appearance last** — a creature designed from a picture has its meaning retrofitted, and the retrofit is always visible. Decide the claim first and derive the rules from it, then never explain the correspondence — an explained monster is `theme.explained_theme` with fangs, and the audience stops being afraid the moment they are told what to be afraid of. Pairs with `tens.threat_with_rules` and `tens.embodied_vs_formless`, and `end.horror_coda` for what the survival means.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Short Story, Limited Series, Short Film, Anime, Comics | genres: Horror, Cosmic Horror, Psychological, Thriller, Fantasy, Dark Comedy, Survival
**Avoid when:** Creature features and slashers whose contract is the ride. Loading a thesis onto a monster the audience came to enjoy slows the picture and flatters nobody. Also avoid where the metaphor is a real condition someone is living with — a monster that *is* an illness, addressed to an audience who has it, reads as being made into scenery.
**Example:**
> Claim: *a family survives by agreeing not to name what is wrong.*
> The thing in the house is drawn to speech about itself, and only to that. It ignores screaming. Three generations have been silent at dinner and nobody in the film ever says why, and the grandmother's last act is to say its name at the top of her voice in the hallway.

`theme.monster_meaning`

### The Song's Claim, and the Video's

**Also called:** lyric theme, thematic counterpoint in music video, the second argument on screen
**What it is:** A song carries one claim and its video carries another — the video either amplifies the lyric's argument, or deliberately argues against it, and the tension between the two is the piece.
**Effect on the audience:** Depth in three minutes. Literal illustration gives an audience nothing to do; counterpoint gives them a gap to close, and the song is what they hear differently afterwards.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any music video, lyric video or song where the lyric is not already a story. Decide the relation explicitly at concept stage — **amplify** (the images argue the same claim in another register), **extend** (the images argue something the lyric never says), or **contradict** (the images argue the opposite, and the song becomes ironic). All three work; the failure is having no relation, which reads as stock footage. For the lyric itself, the claim lives in the title line and the last line of the bridge, which is where a listener's attention is structurally highest. Pairs with `lyric.title_line_placement`, `lyric.character_song` and `lyric.anthem`.
**Best in:** formats: Music Video, Lyric Video, Song Lyric, Kids Song, Reels/Shorts, Title Sequence, Musical Theatre | genres: Musical, Romance, Heritage, Coming of Age, Advocacy, Kids
**Avoid when:** Kids' songs and teaching songs, where the video must illustrate the lyric literally because the image is how the word is learned — there, counterpoint is confusion. Also avoid contradiction in commissioned artist work without the artist's agreement; an ironic video is an argument about their song.
**Example:**
> **Lyric claim:** *I would go back.*
> **Illustration:** shots of the village, the family, the old house. Nothing gained.
> **Counterpoint:** the singer is packing a flat, and every object she wraps is one she will not take. On the last *I would go back*, she closes the door of an empty room and we see the room is in the new country.

**Yields to:** `theme.preschool_claim` — Kids and teaching songs: illustrate the lyric literally.  
`theme.song_claim`

## Adaptation and translation

### Find the Source's Claim First

**Also called:** adapting the argument, what the book is actually about
**What it is:** Beginning an adaptation by identifying the source's controlling idea, and treating that — not its plot, characters or best scenes — as the thing being adapted.
**Effect on the audience:** It decides every cut. Adaptation is mostly deletion, and a deletion pass run against events produces a fast, empty film, while a deletion pass run against the claim produces a short one that means the same thing.
**Used for and where it works best:** Before the first outline of any adaptation, including your own material moving between forms. State the source's claim in one sentence, then ask of every element: does this prove it? A subplot that proves the claim survives a subplot the readers loved. The specific work of adaptation is that interiority does not transfer — a novel argues through what a character thinks, and film has no channel for it, so each internal proof of the claim must be reassigned to an action, an object or a second character built to receive it. Pairs with `theme.controlling_idea` and `info.adaptation_double_audience`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Stage Play, Animation Feature, Audio Drama, Comics, Musical Theatre | genres: Drama, Literary, Historical, Heritage, Crime, Fantasy, Biography
**Avoid when:** The source has no claim — much genre and franchise material is built on situation and appetite, not argument. There, adapt the appetite honestly rather than manufacturing a thesis the fans will experience as a betrayal of tone.
**Example:**
> **Claim of the novel:** *a person who explains themselves constantly is not being understood, and knows it.*
> Ninety per cent of the proof is in interior monologue. In the film, the claim is reassigned to a single device: she says everything twice, to two different people, slightly differently — and by the third instance the audience is watching for the difference rather than the content.

`theme.adaptation_claim`

### The Deliberate Thematic Shift

**Also called:** the honest reinterpretation, arguing with the source, the changed claim
**What it is:** Adapting a work while deliberately changing its claim — usually because the source's argument has dated, because the new form cannot carry it, or because the adapter genuinely disagrees — and doing so openly rather than by drift.
**Effect on the audience:** Either the strongest possible reason for an adaptation to exist, or the deepest betrayal, and which one it is depends entirely on whether the shift was designed or leaked.
**Used for and where it works best:** Older material with a claim the writer cannot honestly advance — a period text whose moral vocabulary has expired, a folk story whose verdict on a character now reads differently, heritage material where the source was written by the winning side. Do it properly: write both claims, the source's and yours, on the same page; change structure rather than dialogue to carry the new one; and keep the source's argument present and strong inside the work as `theme.anti_theme`, so the piece argues with its origin instead of quietly correcting it. Structure is the honest place to make the shift — reordering a timeline changes what the story proves without putting new words in an old character's mouth.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Stage Play, Animation Feature, Novel, Musical Theatre | genres: Historical, Heritage, Myth, Literary, Drama, Fable, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The rights, the estate or the audience contract forbid it, and — more commonly — when the shift is not actually a position but a flinch. An adapter who softens a hard source because they are nervous has not reinterpreted it; they have produced a version with no claim at all.
**Example:**
> **Source claim:** *the youngest brother was right to leave and never return.*
> **New claim:** *he was right to leave, and the cost was paid by a sister the story does not name.*
> Nothing he says changes. The film adds nine minutes in the village, in her present tense, and moves the departure from the first act to the last.

**Yields to:** `theme.adaptation_claim` — Rights, estate or contract forbid changing the claim.  
`theme.adaptation_shift`

### Writing a Claim That Survives Translation

**Also called:** translation-proof theme, the claim in the action, writing for the subtitle
**What it is:** Building the thematic argument so that it is carried by structure, choice and object rather than by wordplay, register or idiom — so that it arrives intact in a language the writer does not speak.
**Effect on the audience:** The foreign-language audience gets the same story, not a flattened one. A claim proved by events translates perfectly; a claim proved by tone translates at whatever percentage the translator can manage.
**Used for and where it works best:** Anything that will be subtitled, dubbed or localised — which now includes most streaming drama, all kids' animation, and every heritage film with an international festival ambition. The working rule: after the draft, list the five moments that prove the claim. Any moment on that list whose proof is a specific *phrase* is at risk; rebuild it around an action or an object, and let the phrase stay as a bonus for the source-language audience. Comedy carries the highest risk, because the claim is often inside the joke's grammar — see `comedy.translation_resistance`. Pairs with `rhythm.write_for_translator` and `dial.subtitle_constraint`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Animated Series, Animation Feature, Limited Series, Kids Song, Documentary, Novel | genres: Heritage, Family Drama, Kids, Drama, Historical, Migration, Comedy
**Avoid when:** Work whose entire subject is the source language — a film about a dialect, a poem, a piece of stand-up built on a specific register. There the untranslatability is the point, and the correct answer is a note to the translator, not a rewrite.
**Example:**
> **At risk:** the claim rests on a proverb the father repeats, whose double meaning exists only in Syriac.
> **Rebuilt:** the father repeats the proverb *and* always sets down the second cup before he says it. By the third time, the gesture carries the meaning. Subtitle audiences get the claim from the cup; the source audience gets both.

**Yields to:** `theme.untranslatable_value` — The source language itself is the subject.  
`theme.translation_survival`

### The Untranslatable Moral Word

**Also called:** the culture-bound value, the concept with no equivalent, the word that carries the theme
**What it is:** A value term at the centre of a story's argument that has no equivalent in the target language — honour, shame, *ʿayb*, *ḥaqq*, obligation-to-the-dead — and the deliberate decision about how it will cross.
**Effect on the audience:** Handled well, the foreign audience learns the concept from the story and ends up holding a word they did not have. Handled by substitution, they get a nearby English value instead and quietly misread the entire work.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any story whose claim rests on a value the target audience does not share, which is the normal case for heritage, migration and faith material. Four options, and the choice should be made once and applied consistently: **borrow** (keep the word untranslated and teach it by use — strongest when the word appears six or more times), **compensate** (build a scene whose only job is to demonstrate the concept in action), **paraphrase** (accurate and flat, acceptable for a word used twice), or **substitute** (fast and usually wrong, because the nearest English value arrives with its own baggage). Borrowing plus one demonstration scene is the combination that works most often. Pairs with `dial.untranslated_line`, `sub.across_languages` and `pov.translation_shift`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Heritage Documentary, Stage Play, Audio Drama, Animation Feature | genres: Heritage, Migration, Historical, Faith, Family Drama, Social Drama, Tragedy
**Avoid when:** Short forms with no room to teach a word, and instructional work where precision beats texture. Also avoid borrowing a term the source audience itself finds contested — you will be teaching a foreign audience a definition your own community is still arguing about.
**Example:**
> **Substituted:** the subtitle renders it as *honour*, and the English audience reads a revenge film.
> **Borrowed and demonstrated:** the word stays. Scene 8 exists only so that a man returns a borrowed ladder at midnight, in the rain, to a house where nobody is awake, and his son watches him do it. The word is used four more times, and by the fourth the audience has it.

**Yields to:** `theme.translation_survival` — Short or instructional: carry the claim in action.  
`theme.untranslatable_value`

## Culture and faith, inside and outside

### Writing From Inside

**Also called:** the insider's claim, the critical love, the community's own argument
**What it is:** Writing about your own community with the freedom that membership grants — including the freedom to make a hard claim about it — while accounting for the fact that the work will also be read by people who are not in the room.
**Effect on the audience:** Two different effects at once, and both have to be designed for. Insiders read it as an argument within the family; outsiders read it as evidence about the family. The same scene is a self-criticism to one audience and a confirmation to the other.
**Used for and where it works best:** Heritage, diaspora and faith material written by members. The membership buys the right to the hard claim — an insider may write the failure, the hypocrisy, the thing that is never said — but it does not remove the double audience, so the craft is to keep the work's specificity high enough that outsiders cannot generalise from it. Specific people are hard to turn into a category; representative people are easy. The other rule: the claim should be one the community is already arguing about internally. That is what makes it an argument rather than a disclosure. Pairs with `char.inside_outside_culture`, `char.specificity_not_representation`, `char.diaspora_character` and `sub.dual_audience`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Memoir, Heritage Documentary, Limited Series, Stage Play, Short Story | genres: Heritage, Migration, Faith, Family Drama, Social Drama, Coming of Age, Testimony
**Avoid when:** The community is currently under external attack and the hard claim would function as ammunition. That is a timing judgement rather than a permanent bar, and it is the writer's to make — but it should be made deliberately rather than discovered at the premiere.
**Example:**
> The claim: *we kept the language and lost the reason we kept it.*
> The film is set in one parish hall, over one committee meeting, among nine named people with nine different jobs. Nobody in it represents anything, and the claim is proved by who volunteers to teach the class and who volunteers to fund it.

`theme.from_inside`

### Writing From Outside

**Also called:** the outsider's claim, the encounter theme, the consultant rule
**What it is:** Making a thematic claim about a community you do not belong to — done by narrowing the claim to what you can actually know, and by building the research relationship before the argument rather than after it.
**Effect on the audience:** Where it works, an outsider's piece gives a community an audience it could not otherwise reach. Where it fails, the community spends years correcting it, and the correction never travels as far as the error.
**Used for and where it works best:** Journalism, documentary and drama where the outsider has the access. Three rules earn the claim. Make the claim about the *encounter* or the *system* rather than about the interior life of the community — you can argue what a border regime does; you are on far weaker ground arguing what a people believes. Hire more than one cultural consultant and expect them to disagree, because there is no single correct representation of any culture and a single reader's approval is not a clearance. And put the strongest insider objection to your claim inside the work, spoken by a character with standing, per `theme.anti_theme`. Note that some consultants will decline outsider identity stories entirely; that is information about the project, not an obstacle to route around.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary, Reportage, Feature Film, Long-Form Non-Fiction, Podcast, Limited Series | genres: Journalism, Social Drama, Migration, Political, Historical, Testimony, Advocacy
**Avoid when:** The story is an identity story that members of the community are actively trying to tell and cannot get funded. Then the honest options are to produce rather than write it, or to write the part you are actually in — the encounter — and let them write the rest.
**Example:**
> **Overreaching claim:** *this community's faith is what keeps it isolated.*
> **Earned claim:** *the council's consultation process is designed for people who can attend on Tuesday afternoons* — provable, filmable, and the piece still ends up being about the same street.

**Yields to:** `theme.from_inside` — Insiders are trying to tell this identity story.  
`theme.from_outside`

### Sacred Material and the Limits of Argument

**Also called:** writing religion, the non-negotiable, faith as fact not position
**What it is:** Handling religious material by distinguishing what the work may argue about — the institution, the practice, the believers, the history — from what the form has placed outside argument for its audience, and deciding deliberately which side of that line the piece stands on.
**Effect on the audience:** For a believing audience, this line is the difference between a film about their faith and a film against it, and they locate it within about ninety seconds. For everyone else, the work's willingness to take belief seriously as a live position is what separates drama from anthropology.
**Used for and where it works best:** Heritage, historical and faith work, and any story where a character's belief drives their choices. The rule that makes it dramatic: write the believer as someone who is *right about something* — not sincere, not sympathetic, right — or the faith becomes a costume the character wears while the plot happens to them. A story may argue hard about a church and still be built on the assumption that prayer is a real action with real content. The failure in both directions is flattening: belief as pure superstition, or belief as pure comfort with no cost. Pairs with `char.writing_the_believer`, `anta.right_about_something` and `voice.liturgical_register`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Heritage Documentary, Limited Series, Stage Play, Audio Drama, Animation Feature | genres: Faith, Heritage, Historical, Drama, Family Drama, Tragedy, Testimony
**Avoid when:** Satire that has openly declared its target, where the whole contract is that nothing is exempt — and the decision there is about consequences, not craft. Also avoid in instructional religious material, which is catechesis and has a different job from drama.
**Example:**
> **Flattened:** the priest is a good man who says wise things and has no theology.
> **Working:** the priest refuses the burial on a point of canon law, is correct, knows exactly what it will cost the family, does it anyway, and is shown afterwards doing the thing he does instead of sleeping.

**Yields to:** `theme.satire_claim` — Satire with a declared target: nothing is exempt.  
`theme.sacred_material`

### The Culture as Lesson

**Also called:** the community as teaching aid, the edifying minority, borrowed wisdom
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A community, tradition or faith used inside a story as the source of a lesson for the protagonist or the audience — present for what it teaches, rather than for what it wants.
**Effect on the audience:** Outsiders find it warm. Members find it unbearable, because the tell is always the same: the community has wisdom and no interests, dignity and no arguments, and never a scene among themselves.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere. The diagnostic is a two-part test and both parts must pass. First: is there a scene in which members of the community disagree with each other about something internal, with no outsider present? Second: does the community want anything the protagonist's arrival makes harder? A community with no internal disagreement and no competing want is a teaching aid with faces.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (most common in heritage drama, travel documentary, brand films about craftspeople, and kids' animation set in a culture the studio is not from)
**Avoid when:** Always. The repair is a want: give the community a project of its own that predates the protagonist and continues after them, and stage one argument inside it that the protagonist does not understand. See `theme.from_outside` and `char.specificity_not_representation`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** the potters teach the visiting architect about patience, and the film ends with him throwing a pot badly and smiling.
> **Working:** the potters are mid-argument about whether to take the hotel contract when he arrives, they take it, and the thing he learns is that he was the contract.

**Yields to:** `theme.from_outside` — Repair: give the community a want of its own.  
`theme.culture_as_lesson`

## Theme and the character arcs

### The Arc Is the Verdict

**Also called:** arc as thematic proof, five verdicts on one truth, matching arc to claim
**What it is:** Choosing the character arc as a function of the claim, because each of the five arcs delivers a different verdict on the same thematic truth: the positive change arc says the truth can be reached and is worth the cost; the flat arc says the truth holds and the world can be moved by it; the disillusionment arc says the truth is real and terrible; the fall arc says the lie can be held to the end; the corruption arc says the truth was available and was refused.
**Effect on the audience:** It removes the most common structural argument in development — *should he change?* — by converting it into a question with an answer. The arc is not a taste decision, it is the shape of the proof.
**Used for and where it works best:** At outline stage, immediately after the controlling idea is written. Take the claim, decide what verdict the ending must deliver, and read the arc off it. Two consequences worth knowing. A flat arc moves the burden of proof to the world, so a flat-arc story needs a much stronger antagonistic system than a change arc does. And a negative arc still argues positively: a fall proves the value by showing the cost of its absence, which is why tragedy is not nihilism. Pairs with `arc.the_lie`, `arc.the_need`, `arc.positive_change`, `arc.flat`, `arc.disillusionment`, `arc.fall` and `arc.corruption`, and with `struct.truby_20_self_revelation`.
**Best in:** formats: all narrative | genres: all narrative
**Avoid when:** Ensemble and mosaic work with no single protagonist, where the verdict is distributed across the cast — there use `theme.through_the_ensemble` instead. Also avoid in episodic and procedural formats built on a protected static lead.
**Example:**
> **Claim:** *a person who tells the truth about their family loses the family.*
> **Positive change:** he tells it, loses them, and is free. The film argues the cost is worth paying.
> **Disillusionment:** he tells it, loses them, is free, and the last scene is him alone on a Sunday. The film argues the cost is real and the claim is still true.
> Same events. Different verdict. The difference is nine minutes at the end.

**Yields to:** `theme.through_the_ensemble` — Ensemble or mosaic with no single protagonist.  
`theme.arc_carries_claim`

### The Counter-Example Character

**Also called:** Truby's counter-example, the character who fails the test, the negative proof
**What it is:** A secondary character who faces the story's moral question and answers it the other way — and whose outcome is shown. The protagonist proves the claim by choosing; the counter-example proves it by not.
**Effect on the audience:** The claim stops being about one person. A single protagonist who succeeds proves a fact about that protagonist; a protagonist and a counter-example travelling the same road prove a fact about the road.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any story whose claim needs generality — social drama, crime, war, political work. Build it as a deliberate variation: same weakness, same opportunity, different decision, and stage the divergence as a scene the two characters share, not as two separate storylines. Truby's principle is that each character should be a variation on the theme, which means the counter-example should be *close* to the protagonist — a sibling, a partner, a predecessor — because a distant character's fate proves nothing about the protagonist's. Pairs with `arc.mirror_character` and `char.the_exception`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, One-Hour Drama, Stage Play, Game Narrative | genres: Crime, Social Drama, War, Political, Tragedy, Drama, Redemption, Addiction
**Avoid when:** The counter-example would flatten into a warning — a character who exists only to be punished. If they have no want of their own and no scene where their choice is clearly the reasonable one, they are a cautionary tale, and the audience reads the mechanism instead of the claim. Also avoid in two-handers, where the mirror has to live inside the relationship rather than beside it.
**Example:**
> Both men are offered the same envelope in the same week.
> He refuses; his brother takes it and is fine — promoted, happy, at the wedding, generous.
> The film's claim is not that the envelope destroys you. It is that he now has to live in a family where taking it was the sensible thing.

**Yields to:** `arc.mirror_character` — Two-hander: the mirror lives inside the relationship.  
`theme.counter_example_character`

## Ambiguity and the shape of the verdict

### Earned Ambiguity

**Also called:** the honest open question, deliberate irresolution, the ending that refuses
**What it is:** Ending without settling the question, in a way the work has prepared — where both readings are fully supported by planted material and the refusal to choose is itself the claim.
**Effect on the audience:** Sustained argument. An earned open ending keeps working for years, because the audience keeps re-running it; an unearned one is forgotten by the car park.
**Used for and where it works best:** Literary work, psychological drama, arthouse, and any story whose actual claim is that the question is unanswerable. Four conditions, all of them required. The unanswered question must have been planted early, not introduced late — ambiguity works because the question has been marinating. Both readings must be genuinely supported; if one is clearly correct on the evidence, the ending is not ambiguous, it is unclear. No new information may arrive in the last movement. And — the condition most often missed — the *emotional* line must resolve even though the factual one does not. See `theme.moral_shape_of_ending`, `end.open_ending` and `info.deliberate_unanswered_question`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Short Story, Short Film, Limited Series, Stage Play, Essay Film | genres: Literary, Psychological, Arthouse, Mystery, Drama, Cosmic Horror, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** Genre contracts that promise resolution — detective, romance, most thriller, all procedural, kids' content of every kind. Breaking that contract is not sophistication, it is a refund request. Also avoid when the honest reason for the ambiguity is that the writer could not decide; audiences distinguish the two reliably, and the tell is that indecisive ambiguity has no emotional landing.
**Example:**
> **Unearned:** the phone rings and the film cuts to black. Nothing before it told us who might be calling.
> **Earned:** she has refused to answer that phone in scenes 6, 22 and 49, each time for a different and sufficient reason. In the last scene it rings and she puts her hand on it, and the film ends on the hand. We do not know if she answers. We know exactly what it costs her either way.

**Yields to:** `theme.moral_shape_of_ending` — Genre contracts promising resolution: commit to a shape.  
`theme.earned_ambiguity`

### The Ending's Moral Shape

**Also called:** the verdict shape, what the last scene concedes, the settled feeling
**What it is:** The specific relation the ending takes to the claim — proved, proved at a cost the story calls too high, disproved, or left open on the facts while settled on the feeling. Four shapes, and a work must choose one and commit.
**Effect on the audience:** It is what they carry out. An audience cannot usually reproduce a theme, but they can always reproduce the shape of the ending, and that shape is what they mean when they say what a film was about.
**Used for and where it works best:** The last pass before a draft is locked. Name the shape out loud, then check the last three scenes against it, because endings drift shape more easily than they drift content — a small mercy added in a late draft can convert *proved at too high a cost* into *proved*, which is a different film. The distinction that matters most in practice: plot can be left open, but feeling must not be. An ending unresolved on both axes reads as incomplete rather than as open, and that single error accounts for most of the open endings that are described as pretentious. Pairs with `end.value_landed`, `end.answer_the_question_asked`, `end.catharsis` and `end.tragic_ending`.
**Best in:** formats: all narrative | genres: all narrative
**Avoid when:** Serialised work before its final season, where the shape belongs to the series and an episode that delivers one steals it. Episodes should deliver a position, not a verdict — see `theme.series_consistency`.
**Example:**
> **Claim:** *forgiveness costs more than the injury did, and is still worth paying.*
> **Proved:** she forgives him and they eat together.
> **Proved at too high a cost:** she forgives him, they eat together, and the film's last shot is the sister who will not come in.
> **Disproved:** she forgives him and he does it again, and the film knows it.
> **Open on fact, settled on feeling:** she is on the doorstep with the dish, and we never see the door open — but we have watched her cook for four minutes, and that is the resolution.

**Yields to:** `theme.series_consistency` — Serialised work before its final season.  
`theme.moral_shape_of_ending`

### Propaganda

**Also called:** the unopposed work, thematic writing's failure state, the film with no defendant
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A work in which every element — casting, structure, music, who is allowed to be funny, who is allowed to be tired — is organised to produce assent, and in which no scene is permitted to contradict the claim. Not a subject or a politics, but a structural condition: the trial with no defence.
**Effect on the audience:** Complete success with the already-convinced and total failure with everyone else, which is the inverse of what drama does. It also ages badly in a specific way: the techniques stay legible after the urgency expires, so later audiences see only the machinery.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere in drama, journalism or documentary. Two things are worth saying plainly. First, the boundary is not sincerity — a writer can be entirely sincere and still produce this, and usually is. Second, the boundary is not agreement either; audiences call work propaganda when they dislike its claim and craft when they share it, which makes the audience's verdict useless as a diagnostic. The structural test is the reliable one, and it is `theme.thematic_audit`: mark every scene *advances*, *tests*, *contradicts* or *nothing*. A work with no scene marked *contradicts* is this failure, whatever its politics and whatever the writer intended — except a declared fable, which reports a verdict the audience agreed to in advance rather than conducting a trial, and is held instead to the limits `theme.fable_contract` sets: a single test, one trait per character, a moral smaller than the story.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always outside the declared fable above, including in advocacy — where the assumption that the audience must not be trusted with the counter-case is also, empirically, the less persuasive choice. The repair is one scene: the strongest version of the opposing case, given to someone with standing, winning something. See `theme.anti_theme` and `theme.rigged_argument`, of which this is the whole-work form.
**Example:**
> **The tell:** across 104 minutes, every person who holds the opposing view is filmed from below, interrupted, or eating.
> **The repair, in one scene:** the opposing witness is given the best three minutes in the film, is not interrupted, and is not answered until twenty minutes later — and the answer is only partly sufficient.

**Yields to:** `theme.anti_theme` — Repair: one scene where the opposition wins something.  
`theme.propaganda`
