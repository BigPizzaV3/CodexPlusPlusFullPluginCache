## Genre-Specific Delivery

### The Rule Taught on a Casualty

**Also called:** the cold-open victim, the demonstration death, rule-setting by sacrifice
**What it is:** The threat's rule is delivered by showing someone break it and pay for it, before the protagonist is ever at risk.
**Effect on the audience:** They hold the rule as a fear rather than as a fact. Every subsequent scene is scanned against it, so the writer gets tension from ordinary blocking — a window, a light switch, a turned head.
**Used for and where it works best:** Horror, monsters, curses, contagions, any threat with conditions. The craft rule: the casualty breaks exactly *one* rule, visibly, and the audience must see the break *before* the consequence. Reverse that order and you have written a mystery, not a rule. Never state the rule aloud in the same scene — let a character articulate it two scenes later and get one detail wrong, which both confirms it and keeps it unstable. The tension side of the same mechanism is `tens.threat_with_rules`; the design of the rules themselves is `anta.monster_rules`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Series, Novel, Short Film, Game Narrative | genres: Horror, Thriller, Survival, Cosmic Horror, Mystery
**Avoid when:** the threat's horror is that it has no rules — an indifferent or arbitrary force is ruined by a tutorial. Also avoid when the piece has only one death to spend: burning it on instruction leaves the climax with nothing to cost.
**Example:**
> Stated: "Don't look at it. If you look at it, it takes you."
> Taught: "The dog went first, ears up. Marla turned to see what the dog had seen. Elias kept his eyes on the floorboards and counted to sixty, and when he looked up there was only the dog."

**Yields to:** `tens.refusing_to_name` — where the threat's horror is that it has no rules. An indifferent or arbitrary force is ruined by a tutorial, however well the casualty is staged.  
`expo.horror_rule_victim`

### The Joke That Carries the Fact

**Also called:** funny exposition, the informative setup, exposition on a laugh
**What it is:** A necessary fact is placed in the *setup* of a joke, so the audience takes delivery of it while leaning forward for the punchline.
**Effect on the audience:** They absorb the fact with their guard down, and the laugh timestamps it — information delivered on a laugh is recalled far later in the piece than the same information delivered flat.
**Used for and where it works best:** Sitcom, comic novel, comic thriller, animation, anywhere a scene must be funny *and* do work. The craft rule: the fact goes in the setup, never the punchline. A fact in the punchline is filed as a joke and not as information, and if the joke dies the information dies with it. One fact per joke. See `comedy.setup_punchline` for shape and `comedy.explained_joke` for the thing that kills it.
**Best in:** formats: Half-Hour Comedy, Feature Film, Novel, Animated Series, Sketch | genres: Comedy, Romantic Comedy, Satire, Dark Comedy, Farce
**Avoid when:** the fact is load-bearing and must be held precisely — a joke is a poor receipt, and audiences will remember the shape of it rather than the number in it. Also avoid inside a sequence you need the audience to take seriously three minutes later; a laugh has a half-life.
**Example:**
> Flat: "My brother runs the family bakery now. He took over after Dad's stroke."
> On the setup: "My brother runs the bakery now. Dad had the stroke in March, and my brother's first act as owner was to fire the only person in the building who could make bread."

**Yields to:** `expo.two_touch_rule` — where the fact is load-bearing and must be held precisely. A joke is a poor receipt: audiences remember the shape of it rather than the number in it.  
`expo.joke_carries_the_fact`

### Say It, See It, Ask For It

**Also called:** the preschool triple, triple-channel teaching, curriculum repetition
**What it is:** A word or fact for a young audience is delivered three times inside one segment through three *different* channels — spoken once, shown once without words, and then asked for, with the answer left to the child.
**Effect on the audience:** Comprehension rather than exposure. The repeated-viewing research behind shows built this way found that children answer aloud, point, and predict on the repeat; the third touch is where a word stops being heard and becomes usable.
**Used for and where it works best:** Preschool, kids songs, early-reader picture books, educational animation. The craft rule: three channels, not three sayings — same-channel repetition trains a child to tune out. And the third touch must be a genuine *ask* with a silence after it that the show does not fill; four seconds of nothing feels unbearable in the edit and is the entire mechanism. In a song this is the gap after the ask; the bar of space after a new word is a different gap, owned by `genre.kids_teaching_tempo`. Adult work inverts all of this, so do not carry the habit across. See `comedy.kids_comedy` and `char.age_specific_logic`.
**Best in:** formats: Preschool, Kids Story, Animated Series, Kids Song, Picture Book, Educational | genres: Kids, Preschool, Educational, Fable, Musical
**Avoid when:** the audience is Middle Grade or older. By about seven, the third touch reads as condescension and the pause reads as dead air. Also avoid stacking: one taught word per segment, not four. This three-channel preschool retrieval pattern is not the redundant adult rule delivery diagnosed by `expo.over_taught_rule`.
**Example:**
> Once: "That's a pulley. It helps you lift heavy things."
> Three channels: "That's a pulley." — (the bucket rises on screen; nobody speaks) — "It went *up*. Something helped it go up. Do you remember what it's called?" — (four seconds of nothing) — "A pulley! You got it."

**Yields to:** `expo.jargon_threshold` — where the audience is Middle Grade or older. By about seven the third touch reads as condescension and the pause reads as dead air.  
`expo.kids_repetition_ladder`

### Explain on the Incident

**Also called:** teaching on the whistle, commentary exposition, the rule explained when it is broken
**What it is:** A rule of a game or system is explained only at the moment it is first invoked in play, tied to the specific incident the audience has just watched.
**Effect on the audience:** The rule arrives with a picture already attached to it, so it is learned once and never needs restating. Newcomers are carried without regulars being bored, because the explanation rides an event both are already watching.
**Used for and where it works best:** Sports, competitions, invented games, courtroom procedure, any rules system an audience joins mid-stream. The craft rule: never pre-brief the ruleset. Wait for the first infringement, explain in one sentence tied to what just happened, and then leave the second occurrence completely bare — that bareness is how you tell the audience they now know it. The real skill is selection: of the six things that just happened, name the one the rule turns on. Register guidance is in `voice.commentary_register`.
**Best in:** formats: Series, Feature Film, Documentary, Reels/Shorts, Live Event, Game Narrative | genres: Sports, Underdog, Procedural, Courtroom, Action, Martial Arts
**Avoid when:** the audience must *anticipate* the rule before it is broken — if your climax depends on them knowing the penalty in advance, the rule needs `expo.rule_by_demonstration` well upstream, because an explanation arriving with the incident arrives too late to dread.
**Example:**
> Pre-briefed: "In this competition, a player who is ahead of the last defender when the ball is played may not receive it."
> On the incident: "Flag's up. — He's a shoulder past the last man when the ball leaves the boot. That's all it takes. A shoulder."

**Yields to:** `expo.rule_by_demonstration` — where the audience must *anticipate* the rule before it is broken. An explanation arriving with the incident arrives too late to dread, so the teach goes well upstream.  
`expo.game_rule_commentary`

### Assume the Canon

**Also called:** adaptation shorthand, the second-telling cut, skipping the known setup
**What it is:** An adaptation of a widely known source deliberately drops the setup the source spent chapters on and enters at the point where the informed audience is already sitting.
**Effect on the audience:** Readers of the source feel respected rather than re-taught; newcomers are carried by behaviour instead of explanation and usually do not notice what was skipped.
**Used for and where it works best:** Myth, scripture, folk tale, famous novels, historical events with a known outcome, sequels to a well-known first film. The craft rule: cut the *explanation* and keep the *demonstration*. A newcomer can ride an unexplained relationship for an hour if they can watch how two people behave; they cannot ride an unexplained rule for five minutes. Test by showing the first ten minutes to someone who does not know the source and asking only who wants what. `info.adaptation_double_audience` handles the withholding side, `info.known_ending_structure` the outcome everyone already knows.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Animation Feature, Stage Play, Series | genres: Myth, Fantasy, Heritage, Historical, Epic, Biography
**Avoid when:** the source is beloved by a small audience and unknown to everyone else — creators consistently overestimate how widely the book was read. Also avoid when the adaptation reinterprets the premise: a changed rule must be established from zero, because assumed canon will now actively mislead the people who know the original.
**Example:**
> Re-explained: "Gilgamesh was two-thirds god and one-third man, king of Uruk, and he built the great wall."
> Assumed: open on the wall, and on a woman who will not look at it. "My son cut those stones." — "Your son was paid." — "My son was taken."

**Yields to:** `expo.rule_by_demonstration` — where the adaptation reinterprets the premise. A changed rule must be established from zero, because assumed canon will now actively mislead the people who know the original.  
`expo.assumed_canon`

## Channel and Apparatus

### Voice-Over With a Standpoint

**Also called:** character narration, first-person VO, the narrating survivor
**What it is:** Narration is spoken by someone with a position in the story — a participant, a survivor, a later self — so every fact arrives coloured by an interest.
**Effect on the audience:** They receive information and characterisation in the same line, and they hold the narrator's bias as a second thing to watch, which turns exposition into a small ongoing question.
**Used for and where it works best:** Features, memoir, noir, coming-of-age, personal documentary. The craft rule: the voice must never say what the image already says. Give it exactly one of three jobs — it knows *more* than the picture (retrospect), *less* than the picture (ignorance), or it *disagrees* with the picture (irony). Build the sequence with no narration at all first, then add back only the lines the pictures cannot carry; whatever survives that is the real script. See `voice.narrating_persona`, `voice.retrospective_narrator`, `open.voiceover_open`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Memoir, Documentary, Series, Short Film, Essay Film | genres: Noir, Crime, Coming of Age, Literary, Biography, Migration
**Avoid when:** the narration is being added to rescue material that does not work. Voice-over written in post to explain a confusing sequence never fixes the sequence, and audiences hear the patch — it is the single most recognisable sound of a troubled edit.
**Example:**
> Doing the image's job: (we watch the house burn) "The house was burning."
> Doing its own: (we watch the house burn) "My mother kept the insurance papers in the freezer. She said paper survives water better than it survives fire. She was right about that, and about almost nothing else."

**Yields to:** `expo.doc_narration_contract` — Piece needs a fixed, neutral remit.  
`expo.character_voiceover`

### The Interview Cutaway

**Also called:** sync bite over B-roll, the witness cut, talking head as narrator
**What it is:** Documentary exposition is carried by an interviewee's sentence laid over footage of the thing described, so the claim and its evidence arrive in the same three seconds.
**Effect on the audience:** The fact is attributed to a person, which reads as testimony rather than assertion — and a fact someone is willing to be filmed saying carries weight a narrator's line cannot buy.
**Used for and where it works best:** Documentary, heritage films, reportage, true crime, brand films. The craft rule: cut to picture *on the noun*, not before it, and hold two or three seconds past the end of the sentence so the audience has time to look. And use an interviewee only for what only they can say — what they saw, what it cost, what they still think about. Anything a narrator could say more briefly should be narrated; see `expo.doc_narration_contract` and `info.interview_admission`.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary, Documentary Series, Heritage Documentary, Documentary Interview, Reportage, Brand Film | genres: Journalism, Heritage, History, True Crime, Testimony, Advocacy
**Avoid when:** no footage of the described thing exists and you would be laying a generic image under a specific claim. This is the standard failure of archive-poor history work: a stock shot of *a* river under a sentence about *that* river teaches the audience to stop trusting your pictures, and they will not start again.
**Example:**
> Narrated: "The mill closed in 1994, and the town lost six hundred jobs."
> Cutaway, over the chained gate: "Six hundred. — And you have to understand, it wasn't six hundred families. It was one family, six hundred times."

**Yields to:** `expo.disclosed_staging_note` — where no footage of the described thing exists. A stock shot of *a* river under a sentence about *that* river teaches the audience to stop trusting your pictures; a declared reconstruction does not.  
`expo.interview_cutaway`

### On-Screen Graphics and Kinetic Text

**Also called:** lower thirds, supers, motion typography, burned-in data
**What it is:** Facts — names, dates, quantities, definitions — are delivered as text on screen instead of spoken, freeing the narration to do something the text cannot.
**Effect on the audience:** The ear stays available for tone and argument while the eye takes the data. It also survives sound-off viewing, which is the majority condition on every feed platform.
**Used for and where it works best:** Explainers, social video, documentary, ads, any piece with numbers in it. The craft rule: one fact per super, and the super must not duplicate the spoken line word for word — eye and ear racing through the same sentence produces a stutter the viewer experiences as slowness. Say one half, show the other. Hold any super for about a second and a half minimum regardless of length; that is a reading floor, not a taste question. See `dial.text_on_screen` and `voice.caption_voice`.
**Best in:** formats: Explainer, Reels/Shorts, Documentary, Motion Graphics, Advertising, Product Video | genres: Educational, Journalism, Science, Advocacy, Lifestyle
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-09. Safe zones, caption styling and the assumption of sound-off viewing are platform conventions that have already moved twice; the craft rule survives, the measurements do not.
**Avoid when:** burned subtitles already occupy the lower third, or the piece will be watched on a phone at arm's length with three numbers stacked. Also avoid for anything emotional: a super under a grieving interviewee moves the audience's eyes off the face at the exact moment they should be on it.
**Example:**
> Duplicated: VO "Iraq exported three point four million barrels a day in 2023." SUPER: "3.4 million barrels a day, 2023."
> Split: VO "That year the country sold more oil than it had in a decade." SUPER: "3.4m barrels/day · 2023."

**Yields to:** `expo.doc_narration_contract` — for anything emotional. A super under a grieving interviewee moves the audience's eyes off the face at the exact moment they should be on it, and narration does not.  
`expo.onscreen_graphics`

### Framing Text Furniture

**Also called:** title cards, the opening crawl, chapter headings, epigraphs, date stamps
**What it is:** Context delivered as non-diegetic text at a boundary — before the film, above the chapter, under a scene change — in nobody's voice, narrator's or character's.
**Effect on the audience:** An unarguable fact with no speaker to doubt, plus a held breath. A card is also a tempo instrument: it stops the piece for a second and restarts it somewhere else.
**Used for and where it works best:** Period pieces, multi-strand stories, epics, essays, comics. The craft rule: furniture may deliver only what a scene cannot — a date, a place, a jump, a quantity. The instant a card explains a *relationship* or a *motive*, you have conceded the drama to the typesetter. The famous crawls work because they are the last writing before the picture begins and never return; a crawl that reappears at act two is a writer patching in public. See `time.passage_marker` and `pov.comics_caption_panel`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Documentary, Essay, Comics | genres: Historical, Epic, Science Fiction, War, Heritage, Literary
**Avoid when:** the piece would carry more than about four such cards. Past that the audience is reading a book with pictures attached, and every card after the fourth is read as an apology for the scene that follows. Also avoid using an epigraph to state the theme you could not dramatise.
**Example:**
> Doing drama's job: "GERMANY, 1946. Anna has not spoken to her brother since he joined the party."
> Doing furniture's job: "GERMANY, 1946" — and then the scene: two place settings, one untouched, and Anna carrying it back to the kitchen still full.

**Yields to:** `expo.page_furniture` — Medium is the printed page, not the screen.  
`expo.framing_text_furniture`

### Delivery by Found Document

**Also called:** epistolary exposition, the dossier, letters and diaries, the archive
**What it is:** World facts arrive as in-world documents reproduced for the audience — letters, reports, transcripts, logs, message threads — with the narrative assembled from them or interleaved with them.
**Effect on the audience:** They become an investigator instead of a recipient. Facts found feel earned; the same facts told feel handed over.
**Used for and where it works best:** Horror, crime, historical, science fiction, heritage, interactive. The craft rule: every document needs an author with a purpose, and that purpose must not be informing the reader — a report written to cover somebody, a letter written to persuade, a log kept because regulations require it. Second, the frame must answer why these papers are together: found in an attic, entered into evidence, compiled for a hearing. Unframed documents read as an anthology. See `voice.found_document`, `voice.document_type_voice`, `pov.found_document`, `inter.found_document`.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Limited Series, Podcast, Short Story, Comics, Interactive Fiction | genres: Horror, Crime, Mystery, Historical, Science Fiction, True Crime
**Avoid when:** the format cannot hold reading time. A page of document on screen is about eight seconds of legible text at best, and an audio drama cannot show one at all — there it must be read aloud by someone with a reason to read it aloud, which is a different scene entirely.
**Example:**
> Told: "The company had known about the contamination since March."
> Found: "MEMO — 14 March. Re: your question about the north well. Please stop putting this in writing."

**Yields to:** `expo.diegetic_text` — Format cannot hold document reading time.  
`expo.epistolary_delivery`

## Reinforcement and Redundancy

### The Two-Touch Rule

**Also called:** audio redundancy, say it twice differently, the no-rewind rule
**What it is:** In any format the audience cannot rewind or re-read, every load-bearing fact is delivered twice, in two different *kinds* of channel, separated by at least one intervening beat.
**Effect on the audience:** The fact survives a lapse of attention, a bad speaker, a noisy room — and because the second touch differs in kind, they never register that they were told twice.
**Used for and where it works best:** Audio drama, podcasts, radio, audiobooks, VO scripts, stage, live events, and burned-subtitle video where a viewer's eyes leave the frame. The craft rule: the two touches differ in kind, not in wording — once as a line, once as a sound, a consequence, an object, or another character's reaction. Two spoken statements of the same fact is `dial.known_information` in a safety vest. Separate them by at least one beat; back to back, repetition is audible as repetition. See `rhythm.listener_no_rewind`.
**Best in:** formats: Audio Drama, Podcast, Audiobook, Voiceover Script, Stage Play, Live Event | genres: Mystery, Thriller, Science Fiction, Testimony, Educational, Horror
**Avoid when:** the format permits re-reading. A novel that doubles every fact reads as distrustful, and readers who notice the doubling start skimming for the second copy. Also restrict it strictly to load-bearing facts, or a script grows by a third and loses its pace to insurance.
**Example:**
> One touch: "Nine days he's been dead."
> Two touches, two kinds: "Nine days he's been dead." … (four lines later) "Don't take the stairs on the left." — "Why not?" — "Because nobody's swept them since he fell."

`expo.two_touch_rule`

### The First-Hearing Test

**Also called:** tell it back, the comprehension pass, the one-pass check
**What it is:** You read the exposition aloud once, at performance speed, to someone who does not know the story, and then ask them to tell it back to you.
**Effect on the audience:** Nothing directly — this is a diagnostic. Its effect on the piece is that it reliably finds the sentence you were proudest of and proves nobody heard it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Every VO script, audio drama, stage play, kids script and explainer, and any dense information scene in any format. The craft rule: ask for a tell-back, never ask "was that clear?" — people always say it was clear. Whatever they cannot reproduce did not land, however well it is written, and the repair is almost never more words: it is usually fewer proper nouns and one concrete consequence. This is distinct from `rhythm.read_aloud_test`, which listens to cadence; this one listens for retention.
**Best in:** formats: Voiceover Script, Audio Drama, Explainer, Stage Play, Preschool, Documentary | genres: all
**Avoid when:** the confusion is deliberate. If the listener is meant to be uncertain at minute three, a failed tell-back is a pass — so name the intended state before you run the test, or you will sand off the design and call it clarity.
**Example:**
> Failed tell-back: ninety words, four proper nouns, and the listener reproduces "something about a treaty, and a brother."
> Rewritten: "His brother signed the treaty. Everybody in the valley lost their water. That was nine years ago, and nobody has said his brother's name since."

**Yields to:** `expo.deferred_explanation` — where the confusion is deliberate. If the listener is meant to be uncertain at minute three a failed tell-back is a pass, so name the intended state before running the test.  
`expo.first_hearing_test`

## Series-Scale Delivery

### Pilot Load Versus Season Load

**Also called:** the premise budget, front-loading the series, what episode one must carry
**What it is:** A deliberate split of the show's world information into the minimum episode one must carry and the much larger remainder distributed across the season.
**Effect on the audience:** An episode-one audience that is oriented rather than briefed — and a season that still has something to tell them in week six.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any series. The craft rule: the pilot owes exactly three things — who the show is about, what it does every week, and what is wrong. Everything else is season load. List every world fact, mark the episode where each first *matters*, and if more than about five land in the pilot you have written a bible with actors in it. Second rule: a pilot's job is episode two, so the last thing it should explain is the thing episode two would explain better anyway. See `open.series_opening`.
**Best in:** formats: Television Pilot, Series, One-Hour Drama, Half-Hour Comedy, Limited Series, Web Series | genres: Science Fiction, Fantasy, Procedural, Workplace, Crime, Political
**Avoid when:** the piece is a limited run with no season to spread into. A six-part limited series legitimately carries more in part one, because there is no week forty to hold anything back for.
**Example:**
> Pilot load: "The Institute was founded after the second collapse to regulate anyone born with the condition, and it answers to the Directorate, which answers to nobody."
> Split: pilot — "You're on the list." — "Since when am I on a list?" — "Since you were born." Episode four gets the Directorate. Episode nine gets the second collapse.

`expo.pilot_load_split`

### Re-Entry at the Episode Opening

**Also called:** the week-later reminder, cold re-orientation, re-establishing in scene
**What it is:** The first two minutes of an episode quietly restate where everyone is and what is outstanding, inside a scene that is visibly doing something else.
**Effect on the audience:** The returning viewer catches up without being told they needed to, and the viewer who is current does not feel handled.
**Used for and where it works best:** Weekly series, serialised fiction, podcasts, comics, soaps. The craft rule: re-establish through *change*, not summary. Show the one thing that has moved since last week and the audience reconstructs everything around it — a character eating alone at a table that had two people at it last episode is a complete recap. And never re-open on the image the previous episode closed on: it tells the audience that nothing happened in between. See `open.episode_opening` and `info.recap_without_recap`.
**Best in:** formats: Series, One-Hour Drama, Serialised Fiction, Podcast, Comics, Soap | genres: Drama, Crime, Mystery, Family Drama, Workplace, Political
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-09. How much of this job exists depends entirely on release cadence: a weekly drop needs far more of it than a full-season release, and platforms keep changing which they do mid-run.
**Avoid when:** the episode is designed to disorient, or the show is made for binge viewing and the previous episode ended forty seconds ago. Re-entry material in that condition reads as the show forgetting it already told you.
**Example:**
> Summary: "After the hearing last week, Sara was suspended pending review."
> Change: Sara's pass doesn't open the door. She tries it twice. Someone inside sees her through the glass, and doesn't get up.

**Yields to:** `expo.season_two_reteach` — The gap is a season, not a week.  
`expo.re_entry_exposition`

### The Recap Sequence

**Also called:** "Previously on", the montage recap, the catch-up
**What it is:** A cut-together montage of prior footage at the head of an episode, carrying only the facts this episode will actually use.
**Effect on the audience:** Reassurance, and a promise — a competent recap tells the audience that the episode is about the things in the recap, and they will watch accordingly.
**Used for and where it works best:** Serialised drama, soap, thriller series, and any show whose weekly plot depends on a fact from three episodes ago. The craft rule: cut the recap *after* the episode is locked, working backwards from what the episode needs, and include nothing the episode does not touch. Keep it under about forty seconds, and never recap the pilot's premise in episode nine — by then, recapping the premise says you do not believe the show established it. Because the audience reads the recap as a contents page, anything in it is pre-announced; see `info.telegraphing`.
**Best in:** formats: Series, One-Hour Drama, Soap, Limited Series, Procedural, Web Series | genres: Thriller, Crime, Mystery, Political, Melodrama, Science Fiction
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-09. Skip buttons, autoplay and binge release have changed what a recap is for and how many people ever see one; on some platforms it is now a minority experience.
**Avoid when:** the show is episodic or comic. A recap on a self-contained procedural implies a serialisation the episode will not deliver, and the audience spends act one waiting for a thread that does not exist. Recapping something the binge viewer watched eleven minutes ago is simply funny.
**Example:**
> Padded: forty-five seconds across six storylines, three of which this episode never touches.
> Working: eleven seconds. The gun going into the drawer. The brother saying "I'll be back Tuesday." A wall calendar. It is Tuesday.

**Yields to:** `expo.re_entry_exposition` — where the show is episodic or comic. A recap on a self-contained procedural implies a serialisation the episode will not deliver, and re-establishing in scene promises nothing.  
`expo.recap_sequence`

### Re-Teaching in Season Two

**Also called:** the returning-viewer problem, second-season onboarding, re-establishing the premise
**What it is:** A deliberate, light re-delivery of the show's core rules at the start of a new season, serving three audiences at once — those who watched a year ago, those who binged last week, and those starting here.
**Effect on the audience:** Everyone can follow the season premiere, and nobody is aware of having been caught up.
**Used for and where it works best:** Returning series, sequel novels, franchise films, long-running animation. The craft rule: re-teach only the rules the new season will *bend*, and re-teach each by putting it under a new consequence rather than restating it — a rule that is re-established by breaking it in an unfamiliar way is invisible as exposition. Never re-teach through a character who already knows; season-two audiences are the most alert people you will ever write for and they hear `dial.known_information` instantly. One genuine newcomer in the cast buys all of this for free; that is half of what new characters are for. See `char.long_series_character`.
**Best in:** formats: Series, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Animated Series, Serialised Fiction, Novel | genres: Science Fiction, Fantasy, Crime, Political, Superhero, Workplace
**Avoid when:** the season opens on the resolution of a cliffhanger. You cannot re-teach and pay off in the same scene; take the payoff clean, and do the re-teaching in episode two where there is room.
**Example:**
> Restated: "Remember, the shield only holds while the reactor stays above sixty percent."
> Re-taught by bending: "Sixty-one." — "That's fine." — "It was fine last year. We've got twice as many people inside it now."

**Yields to:** `expo.relocate_downstream` — Premiere opens on a cliffhanger payoff.  
`expo.season_two_reteach`

## Interactive and Game Delivery

### The Tutorial That Isn't One

**Also called:** invisible tutorial, teaching level, learning by gated design
**What it is:** The first space the player enters is built so that the only available action is the mechanic being taught, and progress is impossible until they perform it.
**Effect on the audience:** They believe they discovered the mechanic. Retention of a discovered verb is far higher than retention of a described one, and the player carries a small ownership of the skill into every later use.
**Used for and where it works best:** Games, XR, interactive fiction, and onboarding of any kind. The craft rule: build the room so the wrong action is *impossible*, not merely unrewarding — a player who can wander past the lesson will, and will be punished for it two hours later with no memory of what they missed. One verb per space. Make the first use consequence-free and the second use cost something. Any text you add here is a confession that the geometry failed; players do not read. See `open.tutorial_as_scene` and `inter.gated_progression`.
**Best in:** formats: Game Narrative, Interactive Fiction, XR, Visual Novel, Immersive, Educational | genres: Adventure, Action, Fantasy, Science Fiction, Survival, Educational
**Avoid when:** the mechanic is a *combination* rather than a single action. No room can teach "cancel the second attack into a dodge"; that genuinely needs a prompt, and pretending otherwise produces a corridor players hate.
**Example:**
> Text tutorial: "Press [E] to interact. Press [Q] to throw. Hold [Shift] to sprint."
> Designed: a locked door, a switch behind a grille on the far side, and one brick on the floor. Nothing else in the room. Nobody says the word "throw."

**Yields to:** `expo.numbered_step_list` — Mechanic is a combination, needs a prompt.  
`expo.tutorial_by_level_design`

### The Codex Entry

**Also called:** the in-game encyclopedia, datapad lore, the wiki inside the world
**What it is:** Optional world information stored in a readable archive the player can consult, containing nothing the main path requires.
**Effect on the audience:** The enthusiast gets a deep world to dig through; everyone else loses nothing and gets a leaner surface script. It is also the only honest home for the material a writer cannot bear to cut.
**Used for and where it works best:** RPGs, immersive sims, interactive fiction, XR, and the appendix-adjacent parts of novels. The craft rule: an entry must be readable in under a minute and must answer a question the player has *already had* — entries written ahead of curiosity are never opened. Write them after the game is playable so that none of them can become load-bearing; the test is `expo.glossary_crutch`, run by playing through with the codex locked. And give every entry an in-world author with a bias, because a neutral encyclopedia is the dullest object in any game. See `inter.skippable_layering` and `inter.quality_based_narrative`.
**Best in:** formats: Game Narrative, Interactive Fiction, Visual Novel, XR, Immersive, Novel | genres: Science Fiction, Fantasy, Dystopia, Mystery, Historical, Adventure
**Avoid when:** it is being used as a landfill for cut material. Three hundred entries nobody commissioned is the writing team's notes, published, and players can tell the difference between a world and a backlog.
**Example:**
> Neutral: "House Verrin: a mercantile family of the eastern provinces, active since the third century."
> Authored: "HOUSE VERRIN — internal assessment. Merchants. Eastern. Claim descent from the third century; produce no document earlier than 1180. Recommendation: keep buying their salt, stop repeating their history."

`expo.codex_entry`

### Exposition the Player Can Skip

**Also called:** skippable cutscene, optional dialogue, the respectful skip
**What it is:** Information is layered so that skipping any single delivery leaves the player able to continue, with the required minimum carried by something short and unskippable.
**Effect on the audience:** The player who skips never gets lost, and therefore does not resent — or skip — the parts they were enjoying. Skipping is a rhythm choice, not a rejection, and designing for it keeps the story available to the people going fast.
**Used for and where it works best:** Games, visual novels, XR, and anything with a replay audience. The craft rule: assume every cutscene will be skipped by someone, and carry the required fact in the objective text, the level geometry *and* a bark — three sources for one fact, only one of which can be dismissed. Second playthroughs are the real test: a player who skips everything must still be able to finish. See `inter.skippable_layering` and `inter.bark`.
**Best in:** formats: Game Narrative, Visual Novel, Interactive Fiction, XR, Immersive, Web Series | genres: Adventure, Action, Science Fiction, Fantasy, Mystery, Survival
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-09. Skip affordances, auto-advance and platform accessibility requirements change what "skippable" even means between console generations.
**Avoid when:** the piece's whole proposition is its text. A visual novel that engineers around its own dialogue has designed against itself; there the correct move is to make the text worth not skipping.
**Example:**
> Skippable and load-bearing: a four-minute cutscene in which the quartermaster explains that the east gate is mined.
> Layered: the cutscene still plays. The objective line reads "Reach the west gate — EAST GATE MINED." And a soldier on the wall says, to nobody in particular, "Anyone going east — don't."

`expo.skippable_by_design`

## Named Failures

### The Exposition Fairy

**Also called:** Mr Exposition, the sidekick who explains, the walking manual
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A character who exists to explain — a companion, a mentor, an AI, a fairy — with no want of their own, no capacity to be wrong, and nothing to lose.
**Effect on the audience:** They stop hearing a person and start hearing the writer. Worse, the character's presence licenses everyone else in the cast to stop being curious, so the whole ensemble goes passive around them.
**Used for and where it works best:** —
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** always, in this form. The defect is not that a character explains things; it is that explaining is all they do. Two tests: does this character ever want something the scene denies them, and are they ever wrong about a fact and corrected for it? If both answers are no, give them a want and a mistake, or distribute their lines to characters who already exist. The trope is at its worst in games, where it usually arrives as a companion who also narrates the interface, and in fantasy, where it arrives as the mentor. See `char.mouthpiece` and `char.audience_surrogate_trap`.
**Example:**
> Fairy: "That's a wraith! Wraiths can't cross running water, but they get stronger after dark. You'll need silver!"
> Repaired: "Water." — "What?" — "Get in the water." — "It's freezing." — "I know." And later, when he is wrong about the silver, it costs them a night and a hand.

**Yields to:** `expo.split_across_three_scenes` — always. The defect is not that a character explains things, it is that explaining is all they do; distribute the lines to characters who already exist, or give this one a want and a mistake.  
`expo.exposition_fairy`

### The Flashback That Explains What Was Clear

**Also called:** the redundant flashback, the illustrated line, the confirming memory
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A scene cuts back to dramatise information the audience has already inferred — the accident that was described, the betrayal that was obvious, the parent who was plainly cruel.
**Effect on the audience:** Deflation. They had assembled the picture themselves and are now shown it, which converts their inference into an instruction and retroactively cheapens the work they did.
**Used for and where it works best:** —
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** always, where the content is confirmation. A flashback earns its place only by *contradicting* or *complicating* what the audience assumed. Diagnostic: write the flashback's content as one sentence and ask whether an attentive viewer could already say that sentence. If they could, cut it. If they could say it but would put the emphasis in the wrong place, then the flashback is about the emphasis and should contain only that. It hurts most in melodrama and in true crime, where it usually arrives as a reconstruction of a thing an interviewee just described perfectly well. See `time.dramatised_flashback` and `time.unasked_flashback`.
**Example:**
> Redundant: she tells him her brother drowned. Cut to: the brother, drowning.
> Complicating: she tells him her brother drowned. Cut to: the brother, drowning — and her on the bank, not moving.

**Yields to:** `time.dramatised_flashback` — always, where the content is confirmation. A flashback earns its place only by contradicting or complicating what the audience assumed.  
`expo.redundant_flashback`

### The Over-Taught Rule

**Also called:** over-explaining the mechanism, the fourth demonstration, rule fatigue
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A rule of the world is demonstrated, then restated, then demonstrated again, then referred to once more before it is finally used — because the writer was afraid the audience missed it.
**Effect on the audience:** They feel distrusted, and then they start predicting. A rule taught four times announces that it will be decisive, so the climax it was meant to enable arrives already spent.
**Used for and where it works best:** —
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** always, past the second delivery, for an audience of Middle Grade or older. Below that the repetition is the mechanism: a word delivered through different channels (`expo.kids_repetition_ladder`), and a levelled reader written to a specification, which is required to over-teach (`expo.decodability_spec`). The working shape is: demonstrate once, cost once, use. If you fear the first delivery was too quiet, strengthen that one rather than adding a third — a loud single teach is always cheaper than three soft ones. Audiences hold rules far better than they hold names, so the anxiety is usually misplaced; the real memory problem is in `expo.proper_noun_budget`. This failure specifically kills monster films, where each extra explanation shrinks the creature; see `anta.overexplained_monster`.
**Example:**
> Over-taught: the mask must be worn indoors — shown in act one, stated by a doctor, printed on a poster, argued about over dinner, and then it matters.
> Enough: shown once, on somebody who forgot. Nobody mentions it again until the climax, when a hand goes to a face and the audience is ahead by half a second, which is exactly where you want them.

**Yields to:** `expo.rule_by_demonstration` and `expo.rule_cost_demo` — past the second delivery, except in a levelled reader, where `expo.decodability_spec` requires the over-teaching, and in preschool teaching, where `expo.kids_repetition_ladder` does. The working shape is demonstrate once, cost once, use; if the first teach was too quiet, strengthen that one rather than adding a third.  
`expo.over_taught_rule`

### Retroactive Rule Invention

**Also called:** the late rule, the ass-pull, making it up at the climax
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A previously unmentioned rule of the world is introduced at or immediately before the point of use, because the ending requires it.
**Effect on the audience:** Their working model of the world becomes worthless, so they stop predicting — and an audience that has stopped predicting has stopped being tense. The damage is retrospective: it devalues every rule you did establish honestly.
**Used for and where it works best:** —
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** always. The repair is never to justify the rule in dialogue at the moment of use; that only draws attention to it. Plant it cheaply and early, in one clause, on a minor character, while something else is happening — see `info.buried_plant`. Note that this is a different offence from `info.the_retcon`, which changes a fact the audience already holds, and from `info.the_cheat`, which withholds one they were entitled to. It hurts most in mystery and in magic-system fantasy, the two forms whose entire pleasure is the audience reasoning ahead.
**Example:**
> Invented: "The seal only opens for someone of the founder's blood" — said for the first time, in the room, with the seal.
> Planted forty pages earlier: "She's got the founder's chin, that one." — "She's got the founder's everything, and none of the money."

**Yields to:** `info.buried_plant` — always. The repair is never to justify the rule in dialogue at the moment of use, which only draws attention to it: plant it cheaply and early, in one clause, on a minor character, while something else is happening.  
`expo.retroactive_rule`

### The Recap That Spoils

**Also called:** the telegraphing recap, the previously-on giveaway
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A recap includes a shot or line whose only purpose is this episode's turn, so the audience knows the shape of the episode before it starts.
**Effect on the audience:** The twist arrives pre-announced. And an audience that has learned your recap is a table of contents will read every future recap the same way, so the cost is not one episode but all of them.
**Used for and where it works best:** —
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** always. The tell is a recap beat nobody would otherwise remember — a two-second insert of a locker, a minor character's surname, a prop with no scene around it. If the episode needs an obscure fact recalled, the episode should re-establish it in scene rather than let the recap flag it. Where the footage genuinely must appear, bury it among three other recalled items of equal weight so it carries no emphasis. It hurts worst in mystery, thriller and soap, the three forms that live on the weekly turn. See `info.telegraphing` and `expo.recap_sequence`.
**Example:**
> Spoiling: the recap ends on a held close-up of the pharmacy receipt nobody mentioned last week.
> Fixed: the receipt sits in shot during the recap's argument scene, unremarked and out of focus, and this week's episode finds it on the floor.

**Yields to:** `expo.re_entry_exposition` — always. The tell is a recap beat nobody would otherwise remember; if the episode needs an obscure fact recalled, it re-establishes it in scene rather than letting the recap flag it.  
`expo.spoiler_recap`

### The Translation-Convention Breach

**Also called:** broken language logic, the accent that slips, inconsistent subtitling
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A piece establishes a convention for rendering another language — everyone speaks the audience's language, or it is subtitled throughout, or it is accented — and then breaks it for one scene's convenience.
**Effect on the audience:** They suddenly notice the device, and once noticed it cannot be unnoticed: every later conversation is watched rather than heard. In diaspora and heritage work the breach also reads as carelessness toward the culture depicted, which costs more than the craft error does.
**Used for and where it works best:** —
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** always, unless the switch is *marked and meaningful* — a character genuinely changing language in front of others is legitimate and powerful, provided the story registers that a change occurred. The three common breaches: people who share a first language speaking the audience's language when alone together; subtitles that appear only on lines that matter; an accent that thickens when the writer wants foreignness. Write the convention as one sentence before the first draft and put it in the bible. It hurts most in migration stories, war films and heritage documentary. See `dial.untranslated_line`, `dial.subtitle_constraint` and `dial.interpreter_scene`.
**Example:**
> Breach: two Assyrian brothers alone in a kitchen speak English to each other, then switch to subtitled Syriac for the one emotional line.
> Consistent: they speak Syriac throughout, subtitled — and the switch that carries meaning is into English, when the younger one has something to say that he does not want to say in their mother's language.

**Yields to:** `dial.interpreter_scene` and `dial.untranslated_line` — always, unless the switch is marked and meaningful. A character genuinely changing language in front of others is legitimate and powerful, provided the story registers that a change occurred.  
`expo.translation_convention_breach`

## Repair

### Turn the Statement Into a Question

**Also called:** the asked fact, question conversion, exposition by enquiry
**What it is:** A block of explanation is repaired by handing it to the listener instead of the speaker — the character who needs to know asks for it in pieces, and the answer extends only as far as each question reaches.
**Effect on the audience:** They receive the information in the shape of curiosity rather than the shape of a report, and the scene acquires a rhythm it did not have.
**Used for and where it works best:** The first repair to try on any briefing, debrief, or expert scene. The craft rule: the question must be *worse* than the answer expects — specific, slightly hostile, or aimed at the wrong part. "How does it work?" is not a question, it is a cue. "Who signed for it?" is a question. And leave two of them unanswered: an explanation with holes in it reads as a world, an explanation with none reads as a document. Compare `expo.resistant_listener`, which repairs the same scene through refusal rather than enquiry, and `dial.question_control`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Series, Novel, Stage Play, Audio Drama, Podcast | genres: Thriller, Crime, Procedural, Science Fiction, Medical, Political
**Avoid when:** the asking character would plainly already know the answer. Converting a statement into a question a character has no business asking produces `expo.as_you_know` with a question mark on the end, which is worse than the statement was.
**Example:**
> Statement: "The shipment cleared customs on the fourteenth, was signed for by a freight agent who no longer works there, and went out on a truck with no plates."
> Asked: "When did it clear?" — "Fourteenth." — "Who signed?" — "Freight agent." — "Name." — "He doesn't work there any more." — "That's not a name."

**Yields to:** `expo.resistant_listener` — Repair the same scene by refusal, not enquiry.  
`expo.statement_into_question`

### Split It Across Three Scenes

**Also called:** the thirds rule, distributing the block, exposition in instalments
**What it is:** A single indigestible block is cut into three parts and placed in three different scenes, each attached to a different character's need.
**Effect on the audience:** None of the three registers as exposition, and they assemble the whole without ever having to hold it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any block longer than about half a page or forty seconds that survived `expo.deletion_test`. The craft rule: split by *who cares*, not by length — each third goes to the scene where somebody in it wants that specific piece. Then check the order, because the audience must be able to follow scene two without the third that lands in scene nine; in practice the mechanism goes first, the history second, the implication last. Very often only two of the three survive the split, which is the real benefit. Pairs with `expo.relocate_downstream` and `scene.absorb_and_delete`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Series, Documentary, Audio Drama, Stage Play | genres: all
**Avoid when:** the information's force depends on arriving whole — a confession, a diagnosis, a will being read aloud. Splitting those is not repair, it is dilution, and the audience gets three small moments instead of one large one.
**Example:**
> One block: a paragraph explaining that the land was seized in 1963, that the family's claim was never filed, and that the deadline to file passed in 1990.
> Split: scene one, a lawyer asks "Do you have the papers?" and nobody answers. Scene four, an uncle: "Sixty-three. They gave us an afternoon. We were told to leave the tractor." Scene nine, a clerk, bored: "There was a window. It shut in ninety."

**Yields to:** `expo.relocate_downstream` — Force depends on arriving whole.  
`expo.split_across_three_scenes`

### Trade the Paragraph for an Image

**Also called:** one-image exchange, visual substitution, the picture that replaces the explanation
**What it is:** A passage of explanation is deleted and replaced with a single concrete image or object that implies the same thing.
**Effect on the audience:** They infer in one second what they would have been told in twenty, and the piece gains a thing instead of a claim.
**Used for and where it works best:** Screenplays, comics, animation, prose, and video at any length — the highest-yield single repair in visual media. The craft rule: the image must show a *consequence*, not a cause. Ask "what would be physically different in this room because that sentence is true?" and put that in frame. The exchange rate is roughly one paragraph for one image and never the reverse; if it takes two images, the paragraph held two facts and one of them survives as a line. This is `expo.implication_worldbuilding` and `expo.environmental_detail` performed at scene scale rather than world scale.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Comics, Animation Feature, Short Film, Novel, Reels/Shorts | genres: all
**Avoid when:** the fact is a number, a date, a name or a rule. Images are excellent at states and useless at quantities — nobody has ever inferred "1963" from a photograph, and a rule shown without its cost is not a rule, it is a mood.
**Example:**
> Paragraph: "Since the sanctions, imported medicine had become unaffordable, and families rationed what they had, sharing prescriptions between households."
> Image: one blister pack on the kitchen table, cut in half with scissors, four names written on the foil in biro.

**Yields to:** `expo.onscreen_graphics` — where the fact is a number, a date or a name. Nobody has ever inferred "1963" from a photograph; and where it is a rule, `expo.rule_cost_demo`, because a rule shown without its cost is a mood.  
`expo.trade_paragraph_for_image`
