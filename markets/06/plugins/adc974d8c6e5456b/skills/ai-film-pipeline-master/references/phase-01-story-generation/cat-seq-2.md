## Section 6 — Planning the Run

### The Sequence Outline

**Also called:** the run sheet, the mini-movie outline, the sequence treatment, the four-line block
**What it is:** A working document, written before drafting, that reduces the whole story to one block per sequence: the question, the scenes in one line each, the climax, and the cost. Usually two to four pages for a feature or a novel.
**Effect on the audience:** None directly. It shows up as a middle that does not sag, because every run was priced before a word of it was written.
**Used for and where it works best:** Write one before drafting anything longer than about thirty pages, and rewrite it after every structural note. The craft rule is the four lines, in this order and no others: **Question** (a yes-or-no with a deadline), **Scenes** (three to six, one active line each), **Climax** (which scene, and what turns), **Cost** (what is permanently lost). No dialogue, no description, no adjectives — the moment you write a good line of dialogue into an outline you will protect the scene that contains it. The diagnostic that makes this worth the day it takes: if you cannot write the **Cost** line, the run does not exist yet, and you have found the problem in forty seconds instead of in forty pages. It is the same document `seq.sequence_map` produces from a finished draft, written in advance instead — and the two should be compared once the draft is done, because the gap between them is your actual subject.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Game Narrative, Audio Drama | genres: Thriller, Crime, Mystery, Drama, Adventure, Heist
**Avoid when:** You are a discovery writer already inside a working draft. Stopping to outline a run you are mid-way through converts momentum into administration, and the outline you produce will be a defence of what you have written rather than a plan. Outline before, or outline after; never during.
**Example:**
> **Run 5 — "Get the boy out of the country."**
> Q: Can she get him across before the visa is cancelled on Friday?
> 1. She sells the workshop to a man who wants the tools thrown in.
> 2. The broker takes the money and gives her a name, not a document.
> 3. The name is her brother-in-law, who has not spoken to her in nine years.
> 4. He agrees, on a condition she cannot tell the boy.
> 5. Thursday night: the boy asks why his uncle is driving, and she lies.
> Climax: scene 5 — the lie, not the border.
> Cost: the boy's trust, spent before the journey has started.

**Yields to:** `seq.sequence_map` — Already inside a working draft; map it after.

### The Mid-Run Turn

**Also called:** the sequence reversal, the mid-sequence pivot, the second-scene rug-pull
**What it is:** A reversal placed roughly two-thirds of the way through a sequence that changes how the goal must be pursued or what it will cost, while leaving the sequence question itself unanswered.
**Effect on the audience:** Renewal of attention without a reset. They were settling into a shape; the shape moved and the question did not, so they lean back in rather than start again.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it on any run of four or more scenes, and as the first repair when a sequence is correctly built and still reads long. The craft rule that keeps it from wrecking the run: **a mid-run turn may change the method or the price, never the question.** If it changes the question, the sequence ended there and a new one began — run `seq.boundary_test` and re-cut the seam. Place it after the second scene in a four-scene run, after the third in a six-scene run; earlier and the original method never establishes, later and it is your climax in disguise. The commonest strong form is the ally turning out to be a cost: the person helping starts wanting something.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Limited Series, Serialised Fiction | genres: Thriller, Crime, Mystery, Heist, Political, Drama
**Avoid when:** The run is short — two or three scenes — or its whole point is a single unswerving pursuit. A mid-run turn inside a three-scene run leaves no room to play out the new method, so the turn reads as a complication nobody dealt with.
**Example:**
> Q: Can he get the recording out of the building tonight?
> 1. He copies it to a drive. Method: walk out with it.
> 2. Security starts bag checks at the door — new this week, and permanent.
> — **Mid-run turn:** the only way out is to send it, which means it passes through a system with his name on it.
> 3. He asks the night cleaner to carry it. She agrees, for money he does not have.
> 4. He pays her with the one thing he has, and she carries it out in her shoe.
> The question never changed. Everything about how he answers it did, and the price went from nothing to everything.

**Yields to:** `seq.sequence_spine` — Short run, or a single unswerving pursuit.

### Entering a Sequence

**Also called:** the run's on-ramp, sequence orientation, context before pursuit
**What it is:** The specific minimum a reader must hold before a run can start generating tension, and the discipline of delivering exactly that and no more in the run's first page.
**Effect on the audience:** They start caring at scene one instead of scene two. Under-served, they spend the first scene assembling the situation and the run is effectively a scene shorter than you wrote.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it at the head of every sequence, and especially at the head of the first sequence after an act break, where writers habitually over-explain. The craft rule — **three facts, one line each:** who wants it, what counts as winning, and what the deadline is. Everything else the run needs is delivered inside the run, on the move, by someone who has a reason to say it. The test: cover everything above the run's first line of dialogue and ask whether a stranger could state what is being fought over. If not, add a fact. If they could state it twice over, you have written an `seq.errand_run` on-ramp and can cut the first scene entirely. In serial and short-form work the three facts must be spoken aloud rather than implied — see `seq.shortform_serial`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Limited Series, Audio Drama, Vertical Micro-Drama | genres: Thriller, Crime, Mystery, Drama, Adventure, Procedural
**Avoid when:** The run is deliberately disorienting — a wake-up into a situation nobody understands, a survivor in the first hour of a disaster. There, withholding the three facts *is* the sequence, and the reader's confusion is the character's. But decide that on purpose, and give them the facts by the end of scene two.
**Example:**
> Run 7 opens. Three lines, three facts:
> "Ninety minutes till they seal the ward." — deadline.
> "He's in 6B and he doesn't know I'm his sister." — who wants it, and what the complication is.
> "I just need him to sign it." — what counts as winning.
> Everything else — why the ward is sealing, what the document is, why nine years of silence — arrives later, in scenes where somebody wants to withhold it.

**Yields to:** `time.in_medias_res` — The run is deliberately disorienting.  
`seq.run_entry_context`

### The Payoff Ledger

**Also called:** the plant-and-payoff sheet, setup bookkeeping, the two-column pass
**What it is:** A two-column list kept alongside the outline: every plant in the left column with the sequence that plants it, every payoff in the right with the sequence that pays it. Nothing else — no notes, no intentions.
**Effect on the audience:** Invisible when it works. When it is absent, it shows up as the two most common reader complaints in a finished draft: "what happened to the—" and "where did that come from?"
**Used for and where it works best:** Keep it from the first outline and update it at the close of every sequence you draft. The craft rules, both about distance: **a plant is owed its payoff within three sequences**, or the reader files it as scenery and the payoff reads as a coincidence rather than a return; and **the two columns must balance at the end** — an unpaid plant is `end.unpaid_setup`, a payoff with no plant is a cheat. The sequence, not the scene, is the right unit for this, because the reader's memory is organised by run: they remember "the hospital run" and what was in it, not page 63. Where a plant must be carried further than three runs, restate it once in the middle — `info.rule_of_three_placements` is the placement rule, and `info.buried_plant` is how to hide it while you do. Compare `info.plant_and_payoff` for what makes an individual pair work; this card is only about keeping count.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, One-Hour Drama, Game Narrative, Audio Drama | genres: Mystery, Crime, Thriller, Detective, Heist, Fantasy, Science Fiction
**Avoid when:** Nothing genuine — but do not let the ledger *generate* material. A payoff invented to close a row is the reason so many third acts feel like a tidying-up. If a plant has no natural payoff, delete the plant.
**Example:**
> | Plant | Run | Payoff | Run |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | The dog is afraid of the cellar | 2 | The dog will not follow him down | 4 |
> | She cannot swim, said as a joke | 3 | The boat | 6 |
> | The brother's limp | 3 | *(unpaid)* | — |
> | — | — | The gun in the car | 8 |
> Two rows are wrong, and both are visible in four seconds. The limp is scenery pretending to be a plant: cut it. The gun in run 8 has no left-hand entry, which is why the ending felt convenient — plant it in run 5, in someone else's hands.

### Sequence Count Against Running Time

**Also called:** the run budget, sequences per hour, the twelve-minute rule
**What it is:** The working ratio between a format's length and the number of sequences it can hold, used as a planning budget rather than as a rule of form.
**Effect on the audience:** Felt as density. Too few runs for the length reads as slow regardless of scene quality; too many reads as breathless and, worse, as unimportant, because nothing was given room to cost anything.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it at outline stage, once, to size the job. The craft rule: **divide the running time by twelve minutes and that is your sequence budget** — then spend it unevenly, because `seq.length_variation` is what stops the budget becoming a grid. The working numbers, as of writing: a 110–120 minute feature carries about eight runs of 10–15 minutes, which is the whole basis of `struct.sequence_approach`; a 45-minute drama episode carries four to six, usually one or two per act (`seq.tv_act_sequence`); a 22-minute comedy carries about three across its A and B lines, the A story running roughly three beats an act; an 11-minute animated episode carries one, occasionally two; a short film is one sequence and should be outlined as one; a 60–90 second vertical episode is a *fraction* of a run, with the sequence climax landing every third or fourth episode. For prose, count clusters rather than minutes — `seq.chapter_cluster` — and expect three to six chapters per run.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Half-Hour Comedy, Short Film, Animated Series, Limited Series | genres: Drama, Thriller, Crime, Comedy, Adventure, Kids
**Source:** assumption — the package's working figure, not a published standard; set it against the running time of the outline and the cut.  
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2028-09. The 11-minute children's block, the 22-minute comedy, the 45-minute commercial hour and the sub-90-second vertical episode are distribution conventions, not craft constants; the 7–9 minute preschool episode is itself a recent shift. The twelve-minute unit underneath them is durable; the slot lengths it is divided into are not.
**Avoid when:** The budget starts generating runs. A 130-minute film does not owe anyone eleven sequences, and a film with six strong runs beats one with nine padded ones every time. Use the number to notice that you have planned fourteen runs for a feature — that is real information — and then ignore it.
**Example:**
> Same story, three budgets:
> **Feature, 115 min → 8 runs.** The investigation gets three; the confrontation gets two; the escape gets two; the return gets one.
> **Limited series, 4 × 50 min → 20 runs.** The same investigation now gets nine, which means seven of them have to be about something other than finding out — so the marriage becomes a real thread with runs of its own.
> **Vertical, 40 × 90 sec → 10 runs, four episodes each.** The investigation gets four runs; each episode must turn; the run climaxes land on episodes 4, 8, 12 and 16, and the marriage thread cannot be carried at all.
> Not one of these is the same story told at three lengths. The budget changed what the story is about.

## Section 7 — The Run Among the Other Threads

### The Subplot Run

**Also called:** the B-story sequence, thread interleaving, the secondary run
**What it is:** A sequence belonging to a subplot, cut into the A-story's runs so that two mid-level questions are live at once without either losing its shape.
**Effect on the audience:** Relief and pressure at the same time. A cut to a different question rests them from the first one while continuing to spend their time — which is why a well-placed B-run makes an A-run feel faster, not slower.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it in any work long enough to carry a second thread: a feature, an episode, a novel. Three craft rules, in order of how often they are broken. **One, the B-run gets half the scene count** — if the A-run is six scenes, the B-run is three, and it is a whole run of three, not three loose scenes. **Two, the B-run climaxes one beat before the A-run**, so its outcome becomes the A-climax's fuel or its counterpoint; a B-run that climaxes after the A-climax is an epilogue and will be read as one. **Three, never cut from an A-climax into a B-setup** — the only legal cut out of a peak is into another peak or into a trough you chose (`seq.breather_sequence`). Interleave at the scene level, not the run level: two complete runs laid end to end is two stories taking turns, which is `seq.act_rhythm` failing. In television the mechanism is standard and load-bearing — see `struct.stc_b_story` for the whole-story position and `comedy.ab_collision` for the point where the threads are made to meet.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Half-Hour Comedy, Novel, Limited Series, Procedural | genres: Drama, Crime, Romance, Comedy, Medical, Workplace, Family Drama
**Avoid when:** The piece is short or single-threaded — a short film, a 90-second vertical episode, a chamber play. A B-run inside twelve minutes takes the A-run's oxygen and delivers a thread too thin to pay. Also avoid when the subplot exists only to relieve pace; a thread with no question of its own is decoration, and readers skim decoration.
**Example:**
> A-run: "Can he get the transfer signed before the board meets?" — six scenes.
> B-run: "Will his daughter tell him she is not going back to school?" — three scenes.
> 1. **A** — he finds the signature is missing.
> 2. **A** — the finance director will sign, for a favour.
> 3. **B** — she practises telling him in the car. He takes a call.
> 4. **A** — the favour turns out to be a name on a redundancy list.
> 5. **B** — she tells her mother instead. **B-climax, one beat early.**
> 6. **A** — he signs the list.
> 7. **A** — the board meets. **A-climax:** he gets the transfer, and his wife does not look at him, and he does not know why.
> The B-run's outcome is what makes the A-climax land, and he never learns it. That is the whole design.

**Yields to:** `seq.breather_sequence` — The second thread exists only to relieve pace.  
`seq.subplot_interleave`

### The Thematic Run

**Also called:** the argument run, theme through the mid-level, the priced idea
**What it is:** A sequence built so that the price it charges, rather than anything anyone says in it, states the story's claim — usually paired with a second run that charges a different character a different price for the same want.
**Effect on the audience:** They come out holding an opinion they believe they formed. Theme delivered at sequence level is felt as evidence; theme delivered in a line is heard as instruction.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it once or twice per story, at the ends of acts, and use it as the repair when notes come back saying the theme is "on the nose". The craft rule: **run the same question through two sequences with different people, and let the difference in what it costs them be the argument.** No character need ever name it. Choose the thematic value first (`theme.thematic_value`), decide which way the story votes (`theme.controlling_idea`), and then price two runs accordingly. The second craft rule is about restraint: the sequence climax is the only place in the run where the theme may become visible, and even there it should be visible as a cost, not as a conclusion. The failure this replaces is `theme.explained_theme`, and its cheapest cousin `end.theme_recited`. A motif carried across runs rather than stated is `theme.thematic_motif`; structure carrying the claim at whole-story level is `theme.theme_in_structure`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Stage Play, Heritage Documentary, Short Film | genres: Drama, Social Drama, Tragedy, Literary, Political, Historical, Faith, Migration
**Avoid when:** The form requires the claim said out loud — a fable, a PSA, a preschool episode, an advocacy piece. There the contract is explicit (`theme.fable_contract`, `theme.preschool_claim`) and a buried argument reads as a failure to deliver. Also avoid when you do not yet know what you think; a thematic run built on an undecided claim rigs itself, which is `theme.rigged_argument`.
**Example:**
> Claim, never spoken: *loyalty in this town is a debt, and it is always collected from whoever has least.*
> **Run 4 — the brother.** Q: will he cover for his cousin at the inquiry? Four scenes. He does. Cost: a caution on his record, which nobody mentions again.
> **Run 9 — the cleaner.** Q: will she cover for the same cousin? Three scenes, identical shape — the approach, the hesitation, the lie. She does. Cost: her job, her flat, and her son's school place.
> Nobody in either run says the word loyalty. The claim is the gap between a caution and a school place, and the reader does the arithmetic themselves.

**Yields to:** `theme.fable_contract` — The form requires the claim said out loud.  
`seq.thematic_run`

### Transposing a Sequence Between Media

**Also called:** adaptation at the run level, the cluster-to-sequence conversion, re-budgeting an adaptation
**What it is:** Adapting by carrying each source sequence's question and climax into the new medium and rebuilding its scenes from scratch, rather than converting scenes one for one and discovering you are eighty pages over.
**Effect on the audience:** In the new medium, the same run of feeling. Scene-level fidelity produces the opposite: readers of the source recognise every moment and feel nothing, because the shape that generated the feeling was the run, not the moments.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it as the first pass on any adaptation, before a line is written. The craft procedure: map the source into runs (`seq.boundary_test` on prose, `seq.chapter_cluster` for the novel's own units), budget the target with `seq.count_vs_runtime`, then match them — and where the source has more runs than the target can hold, merge or cut *whole runs*, never trim scenes evenly across all of them. The rule that saves the draft: **a run may lose scenes, change location, change who is present and change medium entirely, but if its question or its climax changes you have not adapted it, you have replaced it** — which is a legitimate choice and must be made knowingly, because the source's audience will feel it (`info.adaptation_double_audience`). Interiority is the specific casualty going from prose to screen: a novel run carried by what a character thinks needs a new external obstacle in the same position, not a voice-over. Going the other way — screen to interactive — the run must survive the player controlling its length (`inter.interactive_adaptation`, `seq.player_paced_run`). Check the claim survives too: `theme.adaptation_claim`, and `theme.adaptation_shift` where you are moving it on purpose.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Animation Feature, Audio Drama, Game Narrative, Comics, Stage Play | genres: Literary, Historical, Crime, Fantasy, Drama, Epic, Biography
**Avoid when:** The source's whole value is its texture rather than its movement — a voice-driven novel, an essay, a poem. Mapping runs in material that has none produces a plot the source did not have, and the thing people loved is exactly what you deleted. Adapt those by finding the new work's runs, not the old work's.
**Example:**
> Source: chapters 11–16 of the novel, six chapters, about ninety pages. One cluster.
> Q (unchanged): will the widow let him into the house?
> Climax (unchanged): she lets him in, and he sees the second bed.
> Target: one 12-minute sequence, four scenes.
> 1. The gate. (was ch. 11 and 12 — his approach and his memory of the approach; the memory becomes a single object in his pocket)
> 2. The neighbour, who tells him the wrong thing on purpose. (was ch. 13, forty pages of interior doubt — the doubt becomes a person who lies)
> 3. The door, refused. (was ch. 14)
> 4. The door, opened. The second bed. (was ch. 15 and 16)
> Two chapters became a prop, forty pages of thinking became a neighbour, and the question and climax are untouched. That is the trade.

`seq.media_transposition`

### The Decision Run

**Also called:** the which-one sequence, the elimination run, the choice block
**What it is:** A sequence whose question is *which* rather than *whether* — the character has options and the run's scenes close them one at a time, so the climax is a choice made rather than a goal won.
**Effect on the audience:** A different pleasure from pursuit: they weigh rather than hope. Handled well it is the most reliable way to make a passive stretch active without inventing a chase.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it where a character's problem is genuinely not "can I" but "which" — a plea deal, a diagnosis with three treatments, four people who could be told, two countries. It is also the standard repair when a run has stalled because the goal is unreachable and the character has stopped pursuing it. The craft rules: **no more than four options**, because a reader cannot hold five; **eliminate in ascending order of attractiveness**, so the final refusal is the hardest and the choice that remains is the one that costs most; and **each option must be closed by someone else's action, not by the character reasoning** — an option a character talks themselves out of was never an option, it was an opinion. The sequence climax is the decision, and it must be irreversible by the end of the scene in which it is taken. Compare `scene.reaction_dilemma_decision`, which is this shape inside one scene; in interactive work the same run is `inter.no_good_option` and the elimination is performed by the player.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Stage Play, Limited Series, Game Narrative | genres: Drama, Social Drama, Medical, Political, Crime, Migration, Tragedy, Courtroom
**Avoid when:** One option is obviously right. Then the run is a delay, and readers will be four scenes ahead, waiting. Also avoid stacking two decision runs adjacently — a story that is all weighing and no pursuing goes inert, which is exactly what `seq.act_rhythm` exists to prevent.
**Example:**
> Q: which of the four does she tell?
> 1. **The husband.** He comes home having already heard a different version, and she sees what he does with a version he believes. Closed — by him.
> 2. **The lawyer.** Retained by the company as of Monday; he tells her so himself, decently. Closed — by a letter.
> 3. **Her sister.** Who arrives asking for money, and gets it, and leaves lighter. Closed — because telling her now would be payment for silence.
> 4. **The daughter**, who is fifteen.
> Climax: she tells the daughter, in the car, badly, and the daughter is now carrying it for the rest of the story.
> Four options, closed in ascending order of how much she wanted them. The one left is the one she would never have chosen first.

**Yields to:** `scene.reaction_dilemma_decision` — The choice fits one scene, not a run.  
`seq.decision_run`

## Section 8 — Framed and Embedded Runs

### The Cold-Open Sequence

**Also called:** the teaser run, the pre-title sequence, the hook block
**What it is:** A short complete sequence — usually two or three scenes, one to five minutes — placed before the title or the main body, whose climax is the question the rest of the piece will answer.
**Effect on the audience:** Commitment before they have decided to commit. A cold open that is a *run* rather than a scene puts them inside a situation with an outcome pending, which is much harder to leave than an intriguing image.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it on episodic television, on any series where viewers arrive mid-run, and on features whose main engine takes a while to start. The craft rules: **the cold open's climax must be the episode's inciting question, not merely its first event** — a teaser that ends on an atmospheric beat has spent five minutes and bought nothing; and **the question it asks must be answerable inside this episode**, or it is a trailer for a later one and the audience will feel the bait. Second structural rule: a cold open is a `seq.partial_resolution` in miniature — it closes its own small question and opens the big one in the same beat. In procedural formats the standard shape is the victim's last few minutes, ending on the discovery (`open.procedural_body`); in comedy it is a complete two-scene joke with a topper as its climax, and it may be entirely disconnected from the episode, which is the one legitimate exception to the rule above. For the single-scene version and its variants see `open.cold_open`; for the reveal placed inside one, `info.cold_open_reveal`; in song and music video, `lyric.intro_line_cold_open`.
**Best in:** formats: One-Hour Drama, Procedural, Half-Hour Comedy, Series, Web Series, Audio Drama, Documentary Series | genres: Crime, Detective, Medical, Thriller, Mystery, Comedy, Horror, True Crime
**Avoid when:** The piece opens on its strongest material anyway. A cold open in front of a strong first sequence delays the strong first sequence, and the audience arrives at it already spent. Also avoid in slow-burn literary work, where the pre-title run announces a genre contract the book will not honour (`open.wrong_promise`).
**Example:**
> Cold open, three scenes, 3 minutes:
> 1. A woman in a hire car, on the phone to someone she is clearly lying to. She is happy.
> 2. She turns off the main road because the satnav says to. The road narrows.
> 3. She stops. There is a second car, parked, empty, with its lights on and its doors open. She gets out.
> — TITLES —
> Climax of the run: she gets out. It answers the small question (is she in danger?) and opens the episode's question (what was in the other car?), and the episode must answer it.

**Yields to:** `open.cold_open` — A single scene, not a complete run.  
`seq.cold_open_sequence`

### The Embedded Past Run

**Also called:** the flashback sequence, the extended flashback, the past block, the second timeline run
**What it is:** A flashback long enough to be a sequence in its own right — three or more scenes with their own question and climax — dropped inside a present-tense run rather than delivered as a fragment.
**Effect on the audience:** They stop reading it as explanation and start reading it as story, which is the only way a flashback of any length survives. Fragments inform; runs involve.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when the past contains an event that must be *undergone* rather than known — the betrayal, the accident, the promise — and when knowing it will change how the present run's question is answered. The craft rules: **the embedded run needs its own question and its own climax**, exactly like any other sequence, and **its climax must answer a question the present run is asking right now**, so returning to the present is a gain rather than an interruption. Enter on a trigger the reader can see (`time.flashback_trigger`) and exit on the consequence, not on the trigger's mirror image. The second discipline is placement: put the embedded run at the *point of maximum present pressure*, not during a lull — a flashback used as a rest is a flashback the reader skims. For the single-scene form, `time.dramatised_flashback`; for the version that withholds the run until late, `time.withheld_flashback`, which is usually the stronger design; for a whole story framed by a past or a telling, `time.frame_narrative`. The failure this card is written against is `time.unasked_flashback`, and its explanatory cousin `expo.redundant_flashback`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, One-Hour Drama, Audio Drama, Comics | genres: Drama, Crime, War, Literary, Tragedy, Psychological, Historical, Mystery
**Avoid when:** The past material is information rather than event. If a paragraph would do it, a paragraph should do it — a five-scene run to deliver a fact is `seq.inflated_sequence` wearing a costume. Also avoid in short-form and vertical work, where a run in another time reliably loses the viewer who arrived this episode.
**Example:**
> Present run, Q: will he sign the statement against his brother? He is in the room, pen in his hand, scene 3 of 4.
> **Embedded past run — Q: who actually drove the car, nineteen years ago?** Three scenes:
> 1. Two boys and a borrowed car, and an argument about who is more sober.
> 2. The bend, the noise, the other driver's headlights still on.
> 3. The older boy tells the police it was him, and the younger one lets him — which is the climax, and it is not the crash.
> Back to the present, mid-sentence. The pen is still there. The question is no longer "will he sign", it is "which debt is he paying", and the reader had to sit through nineteen years to find out.

**Yields to:** `time.dramatised_flashback` — The past is information, not an event.  
`seq.embedded_past_run`

### The Imagined Sequence

**Also called:** the dream sequence, the fantasy run, the hypothetical block, the unreal sequence
**What it is:** A run of scenes that did not happen — a dream, a fantasy, a rehearsal in the head, an imagined version of events — played at full length rather than signalled as unreal.
**Effect on the audience:** Whatever it delivers, plus a withdrawal from a finite account. Every unreal run spends credibility: once a reader has been shown something that turned out not to have happened, they hold the next run more loosely, and that looseness does not come back.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when what a character imagines is itself the story's subject — obsession, grief, self-deception, anticipation of a thing that then goes differently. The craft rules, and both are about the bill. **One: mark it going in, or pay for it coming out.** An unmarked imagined run bought a surprise and sold the reader's trust to do it; you get one per story, and only if the reveal is the point rather than a tidy-up. **Two: the run must cost something real on waking.** A dream run whose only consequence is that the character wakes up sweating has spent five minutes of runtime and changed nothing, which is the standard reason notes say "cut the dream". If you keep it, the character must act differently afterwards, and the reader must be able to see that they do. Third rule for the nested version — a dream inside a dream: give the reader a rule they can hold, one visible marker per layer, or they disengage rather than destabilise. The fake-out death, the imagined escape and the fantasy revenge are the three most exhausted forms; if you are using one, the surrounding run has to earn it. Compare `time.unreliable_memory`, which is the involuntary version and generally the stronger one.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Stage Play, Animation Feature, Music Video, Short Film | genres: Psychological, Surreal, Arthouse, Horror, Literary, Tragedy, Fantasy, Dark Comedy
**Avoid when:** Anything that depends on an evidentiary contract with the reader — detective fiction, legal drama, documentary, a fair-play mystery (`info.fair_play_contract`). Also avoid using one to deliver backstory, which is the most common and least defensible use: the dream that helpfully replays the accident is a lore dump with a soft-focus filter. And never as the opening run of a piece; you have asked for trust before establishing any.
**Example:**
> Marked going in — she falls asleep on the bus, and the light changes.
> **Imagined run — Q: what does she say to him at the funeral?** Three scenes:
> 1. She arrives late, on purpose, and everyone turns.
> 2. She says the thing she has been assembling for six years, and it is perfect, and it lands.
> 3. His wife thanks her for coming, warmly, and means it — which ruins it, even here.
> She wakes two stops past hers.
> The cost on waking: she cannot get it back. At the real funeral, four runs later, she says nothing at all — and the reader knows exactly what she is not saying, because they sat through the version she wanted.

**Yields to:** `time.unreliable_memory` — Evidentiary contract; use the involuntary version.  
`seq.imagined_sequence`

### The Ritual Sequence

**Also called:** the ceremony run, the wedding sequence, the funeral block, the structured container
**What it is:** A sequence whose scene order is supplied by a ceremony's fixed steps — arrival, vows, speeches, burial, exchange of gifts — with a private conflict assigned to each step, so the form does the structuring and the drama does the rest.
**Effect on the audience:** Unusual dramatic efficiency. They already know the order of events, so all attention goes to what is going wrong inside it, and every deviation from the known form reads as enormous.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when a cast must be assembled and made to behave, when a story needs a deadline that is social rather than physical, and at act ends, where the ceremony's own climax can carry the act's. The craft rules: **write the ritual's fixed steps first as the spine, then assign one private conflict to each step** — the ritual is the container and the drama is what will not fit in it. And the rule that separates this from the version everyone has seen: **the conflict must come through the ceremony, not interrupt it from outside.** The objection at the wedding, the stranger at the graveside, the fire alarm — these are the form refusing to be used. The stronger version is a line of the liturgy that means something it should not to two people present, said on time, in the right order, in front of everyone. Public pressure is the second engine: `scene.public_scene` for how a witness changes behaviour, and `scene.interrupted_ritual` for the single-scene variant. This is a specific and unusually reliable case of `seq.unifying_frame`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Stage Play, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Short Film, Heritage Documentary | genres: Family Drama, Drama, Tragedy, Comedy, Heritage, Historical, Romance, Faith, Ensemble
**Avoid when:** The ceremony is unfamiliar to the audience and you have not established its steps. The efficiency comes entirely from their knowing what comes next; an unexplained rite makes them spectators rather than participants, and you must spend a scene teaching the order before you can break it. Also avoid a second ceremony run in the same story — the shape is very visible, and twice reads as a writer's habit.
**Example:**
> Frame: the funeral, 10 a.m. to the wake. Five steps, five conflicts, none of them an interruption.
> 1. **Carrying the coffin.** The eldest son is not asked; a cousin is. Nobody explains.
> 2. **The first reading.** The widow has chosen a passage that is a rebuke, and three people in the room know it.
> 3. **The eulogy.** The daughter says "he was a hard man to know" and pauses one beat too long.
> 4. **The burial.** The second family stands at the back, and the first family sees them, and the form does not permit anyone to move.
> 5. **The wake.** Ham sandwiches, and a solicitor who has been waiting all morning to do his job.
> Climax: step 4, and nothing happens in it. Nobody moves, because you cannot move at a graveside, and that is precisely why it is the biggest beat in the run.

**Yields to:** `scene.interrupted_ritual` — One scene, or the rite broken from outside.  
`seq.ritual_sequence`

## Section 9 — More Sequence Types

### The Hearing Sequence

**Also called:** the trial run, the courtroom sequence, the inquiry block, the tribunal
**What it is:** A sequence built from a formal proceeding's own order — testimony, cross, submission, verdict — in which each witness or exhibit moves the outcome, and the run's climax is the moment the verdict becomes inevitable rather than the moment it is announced.
**Effect on the audience:** Judgement. They keep their own running score, which makes it one of the very few structures where an audience is doing arithmetic voluntarily.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it wherever a formal procedure will decide something the story cares about — a criminal trial, a disciplinary hearing, an asylum interview, a school panel, a medical board. The craft rule that repairs almost every flat courtroom run: **keep a verdict meter.** Before and after every witness, write where the room stands — 70/30 against, 55/45, and so on. If two consecutive witnesses leave the meter in the same place, one of them is a briefing and should be cut or merged (`scene.merge_two_scenes`). The second rule is about order: witnesses go in ascending order of how much damage they do to the side we are following, and the worst one is someone we like. Third: **the verdict must not be the climax.** The climax is the beat where it becomes unavoidable — a concession, an admission, a piece of paper produced — and the verdict itself is the run's last few lines. Structurally this is `seq.investigation_sequence` played in public with a clock: the same escalating questions, but every answer is heard by people who will act on it.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Stage Play, Novel, Limited Series, Audio Drama, Documentary | genres: Courtroom, Crime, Drama, Political, Social Drama, Testimony, Migration, Journalism
**Avoid when:** The outcome is legally foregone and the story knows it. Then the run is a formality, and you should play the corridor instead: what people say to each other while waiting for a result nobody doubts is usually the better sequence — played for the conflict between them, not for the verdict, because a wait whose result the audience already holds is a delay (`scene.waiting_room`). Also avoid when the procedure's rules would have to be misrepresented for the drama to work — audiences who know the process will spend the run correcting you (`info.heritage_audience_correction`).
**Example:**
> Q: will the panel return her licence? Verdict meter starts at 30/70 against.
> 1. The colleague who likes her gives a good character statement, and mentions a date. **35/65.**
> 2. The date is wrong by one day, and the chair notices. **20/80.**
> 3. She corrects it herself, and in correcting it reveals where she actually was. **10/90.**
> 4. The patient's son, who everyone expects to bury her, says she was the only one who called him back. **45/55.**
> 5. Counsel asks him how he knows. He says: because she called him from her home number, at eleven at night, while suspended. **Climax — 0/100, and the run is over.** The kindest thing anyone said is the thing that ends her.
> The verdict is read in the last four lines and surprises nobody, which is correct.

**Yields to:** `scene.waiting_room` — Outcome foregone; play the corridor instead.  
`seq.hearing_sequence`

### The Negotiation Sequence

**Also called:** the deal run, the bargaining block, the hostage negotiation, the terms sequence
**What it is:** A sequence in which two parties trade concessions across several scenes, each scene changing what each side can walk away with, and the climax being the moment one side's alternative to a deal is destroyed.
**Effect on the audience:** Close reading. A negotiation run is the most verbally attentive structure there is, because the audience is tracking value and knows that a single word can move it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it wherever two parties want different things and both have something to lose by walking away — a ransom, a plea, a merger, a divorce, a marriage proposal with conditions, a supplier and a farmer. The craft rule, imported straight from negotiation practice and the single most useful thing in this card: **write each side's walk-away before you write a line.** What happens to them if there is no deal? That answer, not their desire, is their power, and the run's climax is the scene where one walk-away is taken off the table. Second rule: **every concession must buy something.** A character who gives ground for free is not negotiating, they are surrendering in instalments, and readers lose interest immediately. Third: give the two sides more than one issue, valued differently — money against time, safety against dignity — because a single-issue negotiation can only be a tug of war and has nowhere to escalate. Leverage in the small moments is relational rather than material: using a name, conceding a point that costs nothing, offering a way to save face. For the threat that can be bargained with, `tens.negotiable_threat`; for the version where the danger is all in the manners, `tens.polite_threat`; for the single-scene form, `scene.transaction_scene`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Stage Play, Novel, Limited Series, Audio Drama, Game Narrative | genres: Thriller, Crime, Political, Drama, Workplace, Gangster, Courtroom, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** One side has no alternative at all. That is not a negotiation, it is an extortion, and it should be written as one — shorter, and with the pleasure coming from how the stronger party enjoys itself. Also avoid running two negotiation sequences in an act; the shape is talky and the reader will feel the second one as a repeat.
**Example:**
> Q: will he give the family back the land, or the money?
> Walk-aways, written first: **his** — the sale completes on Friday regardless, so he loses nothing by refusing; **theirs** — they have nowhere to go on Saturday.
> 1. They ask for the land. He offers money, generously, and means it kindly, which is worse.
> 2. They refuse money. He discovers they have been to a journalist — his walk-away is now slightly worse.
> 3. He offers the land minus the well. They cannot farm without the well, and he knows it. **Mid-run turn: this is not about value, it is about who kneels.**
> 4. The grandmother offers to sign a statement saying the family were treated fairly. It costs her everything and him nothing — and it is the first concession anyone has made that actually buys something.
> Climax: he takes it, and gives them the land, and the well, and is photographed doing it. Both sides got what they wanted. Only one of them paid.

**Yields to:** `scene.no_leverage` — One side has no alternative; it is extortion.  
`seq.negotiation_sequence`

### The Siege Sequence

**Also called:** the containment run, the holdout, the bottle sequence, the under-siege block
**What it is:** A sequence in which characters hold a defined space against a pressure that cannot be defeated, structured by what is taken away from them rather than by what attacks them.
**Effect on the audience:** Claustrophobia that intensifies without new information. They begin counting the same things the characters count, which is the closest a reader gets to being inside a situation.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when the story needs sustained pressure without movement, when the cast must be forced into proximity, and as the natural container for an ensemble that has been running separately. The craft rule that makes a siege escalate: **the axis is subtraction, not attack.** One resource per scene — water, ammunition, the radio, the light, a person, the belief that help is coming — and the last thing taken must be the reason they were holding out at all. Sieges written as a series of assaults flatten by the third one, because an attack survived is an attack that proved they can survive. Second rule: establish the perimeter and its weak point in the first scene, exactly, so every later scene can be read against it (`tens.closing_space`, and `tens.shrinking_options` for the choices narrowing in step). Third: the run's climax is a decision about who goes outside, and it must be taken by the person least qualified to take it. A containment run is the inverse of `seq.journey_sequence` — the same subtraction structure with the geography held still — and it is the single most reliable use of `seq.unifying_frame`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Stage Play, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Audio Drama, Limited Series, Game Narrative | genres: Horror, War, Thriller, Survival, Disaster, Western, Ensemble, Crime
**Avoid when:** The story's engine is discovery or movement. A siege in the middle of an investigation stops the investigation, and a reader who came for a question being answered will feel the run as an interruption regardless of how tense it is. Also avoid if you have nothing to subtract — a siege with full supplies is a waiting room.
**Example:**
> Q: can the six of them hold the pumping station until the water goes down? Perimeter established scene 1: two doors, one window, a generator with four hours in it.
> 1. The radio, wet in a bag, is dead. *Taken: contact.*
> 2. The generator goes at hour three, not four, because someone left the heater on. *Taken: light, and the assumption that they can count on their own arithmetic.*
> 3. The older man's insulin is in the van, and the van is under. *Taken: time, and it now has a name attached to it.*
> 4. The boy says he saw a boat and nobody else did. *Taken: the belief that anybody is coming.*
> 5. Climax: the boy goes out to find the boat, and they let him, and the youngest person in the room is the one who made the decision they were all avoiding.
> No one attacks them at any point. The run escalates five times.

**Yields to:** `seq.journey_sequence` — Engine is movement or discovery, not containment.  
`seq.siege_sequence`

### The Infiltration Sequence

**Also called:** the way-in run, the cover sequence, the getting-inside block
**What it is:** A sequence in which a character enters a guarded space or group under a cover, structured so that each scene brings a more expert observer, and the run's question is whether the cover holds.
**Effect on the audience:** Held breath with a specific shape. Unlike a chase, the danger is verbal and social, so the audience scans dialogue for the word that will give the character away.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it after a `seq.preparation_sequence`, whose planted flaws are the ammunition this run spends. The craft rule: **escalate on scrutiny.** Scene one, someone who does not care; scene two, someone whose job it is to care; scene three, someone who knows the thing being faked; scene four, the person the cover was built to fool. Second rule, and it is what separates a good infiltration from a competence display: **the cover must fail at least once and be saved by improvisation that costs something** — a name given, a favour incurred, a witness created. A cover that holds cleanly through four scenes is a demonstration, not a sequence. Third: the audience must know the cover's weak point before the run starts, or every near miss is invisible to them (`tens.near_miss_economy`). Note that infiltration is the *resourced* form — time, preparation, the world outside available — which is exactly what its inverse, `seq.escape_sequence`, does not have.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Game Narrative, Limited Series, Comics, Audio Drama | genres: Heist, Thriller, Crime, War, Adventure, Science Fiction
**Avoid when:** The character's cover cannot plausibly be challenged — they are unknown, unremarkable and nobody is checking. Then walking in is not a sequence and should be a line. Also avoid when the audience does not understand the space; infiltration tension is geographic as well as social, and they cannot feel a wrong turn in a building they cannot picture.
**Example:**
> Q: can she get into the ward as agency staff? Weak point planted in the prep run: her badge has last month's date.
> 1. **Reception.** A bored porter waves her through without looking. Establishes the badge exists and that nobody has looked at it yet.
> 2. **The nurses' station.** The shift lead scans the badge. It beeps wrong. She says the agency always does this, with the right amount of irritation — and is told to get it fixed by Friday, which creates a deadline she did not have.
> 3. **The drugs round.** A junior doctor asks her to countersign. She does, in a name that is now written down.
> 4. **The ward sister**, who worked the same agency for nine years and starts, warmly, to reminisce.
> Climax: she gets through it by naming a real supervisor, whom the sister will now ring, on Monday — so she is inside, on a clock, with her handwriting in a drawer.

**Yields to:** `seq.escape_sequence` — No preparation, no resources, cover already blown.  
`seq.infiltration_sequence`

### The Escape Sequence

**Also called:** the break-out run, the getting-away block, the reverse infiltration
**What it is:** The inverse of infiltration: a sequence in which a character leaves a space that already knows they are in it, built from improvisation and whatever is physically present, with capabilities stripped rather than acquired.
**Effect on the audience:** Desperation rather than admiration. Where an infiltration invites them to enjoy competence, an escape invites them to invent solutions alongside a person who has almost nothing.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when a character has been caught, contained or trapped, and when the story needs a run whose pleasure comes from resourcefulness under scarcity. The craft rules, all of which follow from the one structural fact — **the escaping character cannot prepare.** So: **strip one capability per scene** (a shoe, a hand, a language, an ally, the plan) rather than adding gear; **every tool must be visible in the space before it is needed**, planted at least one scene early, because a solution produced from nowhere in an escape run reads as the writer opening a door; and **the exit must be discovered, not known** — an escape from a place whose weak point the character already knows is a heist, and should be written with `seq.infiltration_sequence` instead. Fourth, and most often missed: the escape run's climax is not the boundary, it is the choice made at the boundary — what or whom they leave behind. Crossing out with everything intact is the ending of a different genre. For the closing pursuit that usually follows, `tens.closing_pursuit`; for the escalating physical beats inside it, `seq.action_sequence`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Game Narrative, Limited Series, Comics, Audio Drama | genres: Survival, Thriller, War, Action, Horror, Adventure, Crime, Dystopia
**Avoid when:** The containment has not been established as serious. An escape from a place the reader has not felt as inescapable is exercise; spend a scene, at minimum, on the rules of the place and on someone who failed. Also avoid immediately after an infiltration run — in and straight back out is one sequence, not two, and treating it as two produces `seq.inflated_sequence`.
**Example:**
> Q: can he get out of the compound before the count at six? Established first: the wire, the dogs, and a man who tried in March.
> 1. He gets out of the hut. *Stripped: his boots, left inside so the floor does not creak — and it is January.*
> 2. The laundry route. *Stripped: the other man, who will not come and who will therefore be asked where he went.*
> 3. The fence, which he has never been close enough to see properly, and which is not what it looks like from the yard. *Stripped: the plan. He improvises with a bread tray he noticed in scene 1.*
> 4. The road, and a lorry, and a driver who has already decided.
> Climax: the driver will take one. He leaves the tray, which would identify the man in the hut, and he takes the jacket, which will not. Both of those objects were on the page before he needed them.

**Yields to:** `seq.infiltration_sequence` — The weak point is already known; it is a heist.  
`seq.escape_sequence`

### The Comic Run

**Also called:** the running-gag sequence, the escalating bit, the comic spiral block
**What it is:** A sequence structured by a gag that returns across its scenes, each return larger or more inappropriate than the last, with the sequence climax being the topper.
**Effect on the audience:** Accumulating laughter, where the fourth return gets a bigger laugh than the first joke did — because by then the audience is laughing at the pattern, and at themselves for knowing it was coming.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it to hold a comic sequence together when the plot underneath it is thin, and in any ensemble form where a bit can be handed between characters. The craft rules: **three placements, and the third in a scene where the gag has no business being** — the escalation is contextual, not volumetric, and simply making the same joke louder is the fastest way to kill it. **The sequence climax is the topper**, which must be the one version the audience did not construct for themselves (`comedy.topper`, and `beat.double_topper` for the topper on the topper). And the rule that raises a comic run from a set of jokes to a sequence: **the gag must pay a plot debt when it lands.** The thing that has been funny three times becomes the reason someone is caught, forgiven, or hired — at which point the run has a climax rather than just a biggest laugh. Fourth, on stopping: a gag that returns a fifth time without mutating is a tic; `comedy.repetition_turn` is where the repetition becomes something else, and `comedy.when_to_stop` is the exit. See `comedy.running_gag` for the gag itself, `comedy.rule_of_three` for the establish-reinforce-surprise pattern underneath it, and `comedy.callback_payoff` for the return at long distance.
**Best in:** formats: Half-Hour Comedy, Feature Film, Sketch, Animated Series, Novel, Stage Play, Web Series | genres: Comedy, Farce, Romantic Comedy, Satire, Dark Comedy, Workplace, Buddy, Ensemble
**Avoid when:** The sequence is carrying a real turn. A gag running through a run in which somebody's life changes will either deflate the turn or be deflated by it — `comedy.joke_that_stops_story`. Also avoid in short forms with no room for three placements; a gag that appears twice is not a runner, it is a repeat.
**Example:**
> Q: can he get through the day without anyone finding out he has been sacked?
> 1. **Placement one, small.** He is asked to sign for a delivery and signs someone else's name, badly. Laugh: recognition.
> 2. **Placement two, medium.** The delivery is a cake, and it now has that name on it, and the name belongs to a man who died in 2019.
> 3. **Placement three, wrong room.** The cake is carried into the two-minute silence. Nobody can say anything, because it is a silence.
> Climax — **the topper, and the plot debt paid:** the widow, who is present, says it was her husband's favourite, and thanks him, and offers him a job. He takes it. The gag put him in work.

**Yields to:** `comedy.short_form_shape` — Short form with no room for three placements.  
`seq.comic_run`

### The Stalk Sequence

**Also called:** the pursuit-in-the-house run, the horror stalk, the hunted block
**What it is:** The core horror sequence: one space, one objective, and a dread that ratchets on a short cycle while a character tries to do something ordinary and the exits close.
**Effect on the audience:** Physiological. It is the only structure in this category whose success can be measured in the reader's breathing, and it fails the instant they stop believing the space is real.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it once or twice per horror feature and once per episode, and use it as the climax of an act rather than as filler. The craft rules, as a five-beat pattern that works in prose and on screen: **(1)** establish the geography and the exit — the reader must know where the door is before anything is wrong; **(2)** the first wrongness, small and deniable; **(3)** the character's rational explanation, which the reader half-accepts; **(4)** the explanation disproved, in a way that makes the earlier detail worse in retrospect; **(5)** the exit closes, and the run's climax is the moment they realise it. Ratchet on a short cycle — a new increment of wrongness every twenty to thirty seconds of screen time, or every half page. The two rules that separate craft from noise: **the threat should mostly not act** (`tens.threat_never_acts`, `tens.dread_over_shock`) — a stalk sequence is about proximity, not contact; and **the character must be doing something ordinary throughout**, because a person who is already fleeing has nowhere to escalate to. Spend false scares carefully; each one costs credibility (`tens.false_scare_cost`). For the run's aftermath, `emo.horror_aftermath`, and for what should not be shown, `tens.implication_over_depiction`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Short Film, Audio Drama, Game Narrative, Comics | genres: Horror, Thriller, Survival, Psychological, Cosmic Horror, Crime
**Avoid when:** The character is armed, competent and expecting trouble. Then it is an action sequence, and should be built with `seq.action_sequence`. Also avoid in kids' and preschool content, where sustained dread has no permitted resolution (`anta.bounded_threat`, `anta.unresolved_kids_threat`).
**Example:**
> Q: can she finish locking up and get to the car?
> 1. **Geography.** The shop, the back corridor, the fire door with the bar that sticks, and her car visible through the front glass, forty feet away. Ordinary task: the till, then the alarm.
> 2. **First wrongness.** The alarm panel says a zone is open. Zone 4. She cannot remember which one is 4.
> 3. **Rational explanation.** The fire door sticks; it never latches properly; she has reported it twice. She goes to close it.
> 4. **Disproved.** The fire door is shut, and latched, and someone has propped the bar with a wooden wedge from the *inside*. The wedge is from the stockroom, which means it was fetched.
> 5. **Exit closes.** She turns back toward the front, and the shop lights go off in order — back, middle, front — the way they do when someone walks the switches.
> Climax: the last light. Nothing has touched her, and the run is over.

**Yields to:** `seq.action_sequence` — Character armed, competent and expecting trouble.  
`seq.stalk_sequence`

## Section 10 — Sequences by Medium

### The Kids' Run

**Also called:** the animation sequence, the preschool block, the one-run episode
**What it is:** The sequence as it survives a children's format, where the whole episode is usually a single run of two or three scenes and the constraint is attention rather than page count.
**Effect on the audience:** A child can hold one question for the length of an episode and cannot hold two. Delivered correctly, they answer the question out loud before the characters do, which is the entire engagement model of the form.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it for animated series, preschool, kids' songs and picture books. The craft rules: **one sequence per episode**, and the question must be **stated aloud by a character in the first thirty seconds and restated at the midpoint**, because a child who loses the question does not re-derive it, they leave. Two to three scenes, and the climax must be an action rather than a realisation — something a four-year-old could describe or imitate. Second rule: escalation is by repetition-with-difference, not by cost (`expo.kids_repetition_ladder`), and the third attempt is the one that works. Third: the run must close completely. `seq.partial_resolution` is wrong here — the open door that drives adult serials produces distress rather than anticipation in young viewers, and the threat must be resolved inside the episode (`anta.unresolved_kids_threat`). Working lengths: 11 minutes, usually paired into a half-hour, carries one run of three scenes; the 7–9 minute preschool episode carries one run of two, plus a song. In animation the run is locked at outline stage and cannot be re-cut later, so the sequence outline (`seq.sequence_outline`) is not optional — it is the deliverable that goes to board.
**Best in:** formats: Animated Series, Preschool, Kids Story, Picture Book, Animation Feature, Kids Song | genres: Kids, Preschool, Comedy, Adventure, Fable, Family Drama
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2028-09. The 11-minute standard block and the 7–9 minute preschool episode are current commissioning conventions and have moved twice in a decade; the one-run-per-episode principle and the stated-question rule underneath them are durable.
**Avoid when:** Writing for older children, where a second thread and a partial resolution are not only tolerated but expected — a middle-grade novel runs full `seq.chapter_cluster` architecture. Also avoid applying the stated-question rule to animation aimed at adults; it reads as condescension in about four seconds.
**Example:**
> 11-minute episode. Q, said aloud at 0:20: *"How are we going to get the kite out of the tree?"*
> 1. **Reach.** The ladder is too short. They try anyway. (Restated at the midpoint: "We still can't reach the kite!")
> 2. **Throw.** The shoe goes up and does not come down, so now there are two things in the tree, which is funnier and worse.
> 3. **Ask.** The tall neighbour they have been avoiding all episode, because of a thing in scene 1, gets both down in nine seconds.
> Climax: an action — she lifts the smallest one up, and he takes the kite himself.
> One question, three attempts, fully closed, and the last beat is a thing a child can do with their arms.

**Yields to:** `seq.chapter_cluster` — Older children; a second thread is expected.  
`seq.kids_run`

### The Player-Paced Run

**Also called:** the game sequence, the state-gated run, the sequence with no clock
**What it is:** A sequence in an interactive work, where the writer controls the order and the content of a run but not its duration — the player may cross it in six minutes or over three weeks.
**Effect on the audience:** When it holds, a run that feels authored despite being lived at the player's speed. When it does not, the sequence dissolves into a to-do list and the player cannot say what they are currently trying to do.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it for any game, interactive fiction or XR piece with a narrative spine. The craft rules: **gate on state, never on time** — a run advances because the player has done a thing, not because minutes elapsed, and every beat must be authored to survive an arbitrary gap before it. **Give the sequence question a visible, persistent owner**: an objective line, a companion who restates it, a journal entry written in the character's voice — so a player returning after two days re-enters the run instead of starting a new one (`inter.session_recap`). **Assume the run will be interrupted at its worst moment**, and never place an emotional payoff immediately after a difficulty spike the player may retry eleven times; the retry destroys it. Fourth: the climax must be a beat the writer controls, not one the player can wander past — which usually means the run funnels (`inter.branch_and_bottleneck`) before it climaxes. Optional material inside a run should be `inter.storylet`-shaped: self-contained, order-independent, and never carrying the question. Where the run gates progression rather than story, `inter.gated_progression`; where it is a designed corridor of escalating pressure, `inter.gauntlet`; and `inter.pacing_without_control` is the general problem this card is the sequence-level answer to.
**Best in:** formats: Game Narrative, Interactive Fiction, Visual Novel, XR, Immersive, Conversational Agent | genres: Science Fiction, Fantasy, Horror, Adventure, Crime, Mystery, Dystopia
**Avoid when:** Never in a game — but do not import linear sequence discipline wholesale. A run whose scenes must be experienced in one order, at one speed, is a cutscene chain, and players resent an hour of story delivered as a corridor no matter how good the corridor is.
**Example:**
> Q: can you get the doctor to testify? State-gated, four beats, no clock.
> 1. **On entering the district** — a bark from a street NPC names the doctor and why nobody will speak to her. Fires once, on a flag.
> 2. **Any time after beat 1** — three optional storylets: the pharmacist, the widow, the records office. Any one is sufficient; all three change the final scene's dialogue. Order irrelevant.
> 3. **Bottleneck** — the doctor's door will not open until one storylet has fired. The journal reads, in the player character's hand: *"She'll want a reason. I need to bring her one."*
> 4. **Climax, authored** — she agrees, and names her price, and it is the one thing the player has been collecting for the other quest line.
> Three days of real time can pass between beats 2 and 3 without the run losing its question, because the journal line is still on the screen saying what is being fought over.

`seq.player_paced_run`

### The Documentary Sequence

**Also called:** the non-fiction run, the paper-edit block, the actuality sequence
**What it is:** A sequence in factual work, built in the edit or the paper edit rather than at the shoot: a question, three or four pieces of real material that answer it in ascending order of surprise, and a climax that is something someone actually said or did.
**Effect on the audience:** Narrative pull inside material they know is not staged, which is the specific pleasure of the form — and the thing a run of well-shot, unquestioned footage never produces.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it at the outline or paper-edit stage of any documentary, heritage film, podcast series or long-form reportage. The craft rules: **write the run's question as a sentence before you choose a frame of material** — if you cannot write it, you have a topic, not a sequence, and topics are what make documentaries feel like homework. **Narration may bridge but never carry**: the moment the voice-over is doing the run's climax, the climax is an assertion (`expo.doc_narration_contract`). **The climax must be actuality** — an admission, a document produced, a door opened, a person's face changing — because a factual run's payoff has to be something that happened. Fourth rule, particular to non-fiction: the run's order is an argument, so the order must be defensible. Moving a piece of testimony next to a piece of evidence it never referred to is the point at which structure becomes dishonesty, and it is very easy to do by accident in an edit. For how factual reveals are staged, `info.doc_reveal_structure`; for opposition without a villain, `anta.documentary_opposition`; for closing, `end.documentary_ending`.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary, Documentary Series, Heritage Documentary, Long-Form Non-Fiction, Podcast, Reportage, Essay Film | genres: Journalism, Testimony, True Crime, Historical, Advocacy, Heritage, Science, Political
**Avoid when:** The material is observational and the point is duration — a slow-cinema documentary, a process film, a vérité stretch where the absence of shaping is the ethic. Imposing a question there produces a film that argues when it meant to watch.
**Example:**
> Q: did the ministry know the school was unsafe before the fire?
> 1. The head teacher, on camera, says she wrote to them. (Testimony — the claim.)
> 2. The letter, on screen, with the date. (Document — the claim has a body.)
> 3. The department's reply, which is dated eleven days later and concerns a different building. (Document — surprising, and it makes the first two worse.)
> 4. Climax, actuality: the retired inspector is handed the reply across a kitchen table, reads it, and puts it down without saying anything for nine seconds. Then: "That's my signature. That isn't my letter."
> Narration in this run is two sentences, both of them dates. The climax is a man reading a piece of paper.

**Yields to:** `doc.observational_mode` — Observational material where duration is the point.  
`seq.documentary_sequence`

## Section 11 — The Remaining Failure Modes

### The Repeated Sequence

**Also called:** the duplicate run, two runs one question, the redundant block
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** Two sequences in the same story asking the same question and answering it the same way, usually separated by enough pages that the writer did not notice, and usually because the first one did not feel like it landed.
**Effect on the audience:** A specific and damaging sensation of the story going round. They cannot always identify the repetition, but they stop believing the plot is moving, and they start reading faster.
**Used for and where it works best:** Recognising it is the technique. The tell is in the map (`seq.sequence_map`): two blocks whose question lines are paraphrases — "can he get her to trust him" at run 4 and "can he convince her he is telling the truth" at run 7. The second tell: the cost lines are similar in size. The repair is almost always **delete the first and keep the second**, because the later version is written by a writer who knows the story better, and because the material the first one was doing can be absorbed into its neighbour (`scene.absorb_and_delete`). If both must survive, the second must ask the question from the other side — she is now the one who has to be believed — which makes it a different run. What does not work is differentiating them cosmetically: new location, new supporting character, same question. The reader is tracking the question.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (worst in television, where episode-of-the-week structure hides it across weeks, and in long novels, where four hundred pages of separation makes it invisible to the writer and obvious to the reader)
**Avoid when:** —
**Example:**
> Run 4 — Q: will the father let the doctor examine the child? Climax: he refuses. Cost: a week lost.
> Run 7 — Q: will the father agree to the hospital? Climax: he refuses. Cost: a week lost.
> One question, asked twice, refused twice. Twenty-five minutes for information the audience had at minute forty.
> Repaired: run 4 goes. Run 7 keeps its scenes and inherits the one thing run 4 had that it did not — the mother's decision to go behind him — which turns run 7's question into *whose child is this*, and gives it a climax run 4 never had.

**Yields to:** `scene.absorb_and_delete` — Delete the first run, absorb what it carried.  
`seq.repeated_sequence`

### The Orphaned Sequence

**Also called:** the run that goes nowhere, the abandoned thread, the dead-end block
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A sequence that is well built — question, escalation, climax, cost — whose outcome is never referred to again, so the story proceeds exactly as it would have if the run had not happened.
**Effect on the audience:** Delayed and corrosive. They do not notice at the time, because the run works; they notice at the end, as a vague sense that the middle was longer than it needed to be, and they are right.
**Used for and where it works best:** Recognising it is the technique. The test takes four minutes: for each run on the map, name the *later* run that could not happen without it. If you cannot, you have found an orphan. Note what this test is not — it is not about whether the run was enjoyable, and orphans are very often the best-written sequences in a draft, because they were written with the freedom of consequencelessness. Three repairs in order of preference: **adopt it** — take something the run produced (an object, an enemy, a debt, a piece of knowledge) and make a later run depend on it, which is `seq.payoff_ledger` applied backwards; **relocate it** so its outcome lands somewhere it matters; or **delete it**, and take the pages. The common special case is the character introduced in an orphan run and never used, which is a `char.combine_characters` problem as much as a sequence one.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (worst in features, where twelve minutes is a tenth of the film, and in adaptations, where an orphan is usually a beloved run from the source that no longer has anything downstream of it)
**Avoid when:** —
**Example:**
> Run 6 — Q: can she find out who reported her? Four scenes, a real climax: the neighbour admits it, in tears, at his own front door. Cost: she now knows her neighbours discussed her.
> And then: nothing. The neighbour is not seen again, the report is not raised again, and the film's ending is identical with or without the run.
> Adopted: in run 9, when she needs someone to stand in the road and delay the van for ninety seconds, the only person available is him. He does it, and she has to ask him, and he says yes immediately, and neither of them mentions why.
> One scene later in the film, and a twelve-minute orphan becomes the reason the ending works.

**Yields to:** `seq.payoff_ledger` — Adopt it: make a later run depend on its output.  
`seq.orphaned_sequence`

### The Premature Climax

**Also called:** the peak that comes early, the run that finishes in the middle, the front-loaded sequence
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A sequence whose biggest beat arrives before its last scene, so the remaining scenes play as descent — technically they are still in the run, and emotionally the run ended.
**Effect on the audience:** Restlessness they blame on the wrong thing. They will say the run was "too long" when in fact it was the right length and in the wrong order.
**Used for and where it works best:** Recognising it is the technique. The tells: a scene after which everyone is explaining rather than doing; a scene that ends with someone saying a version of *so what happens now*; and, most reliably, the point where the writer's own scene descriptions get shorter. This is the near neighbour of `seq.climaxless_run`, and the distinction matters for the repair: a climaxless run has no biggest beat, a premature climax has one in the wrong place. Three repairs: **re-cut the seam** so the peak becomes the run's end and the descending scenes open the next run (`seq.boundary_test`, `seq.sequence_handoff` — this is what the overlap join is for); **raise the tail** so a later scene outweighs it, which works only if the run's question genuinely has a harder answer available; or **hold the peak back**, moving it later in the order, which usually means the information it discharges has to be withheld (`info.staged_reveal`). At whole-story scale the same fault is `end.ending_three_scenes_earlier`.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (worst where an act break depends on it: a broadcast act whose climax lands two minutes before the break sends the audience out on a lull, which is the commonest structural fault in television drama — see `seq.tv_act_sequence`)
**Avoid when:** —
**Example:**
> Run as drafted: 1. He finds the letters. 2. He confronts his mother and she admits it. **3. He meets his father, who does not know who he is.** 4. He drives home. 5. He tells his wife.
> Scene 3 is the biggest beat in the film so far, and there are two scenes after it in which nothing can possibly compete.
> Re-cut: the run ends on scene 3. Scenes 4 and 5 open the next run, and their question is not *what happened* — the reader knows — but *will he tell his wife the truth about what he did in that room*, which turns a drive and a kitchen into the start of something rather than the end of something.
> Nothing was written or deleted. The seam moved one scene.

**Yields to:** `seq.boundary_test` — Re-cut the seam so the peak ends the run.  
`seq.premature_climax`

### The Sequence That Closes the Story

**Also called:** the full resolution, the run with no residue, the satisfying stop
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** An internal sequence that answers its question completely and leaves nothing open, so the story has no reason to continue and the next run has to be started from a standstill.
**Effect on the audience:** They put the book down, and they are not troubled about it. This is the failure that does not feel like one while you are writing it, because the run is satisfying — that is exactly the problem.
**Used for and where it works best:** Recognising it is the technique. The test is one question asked of each run in the map: *what is still wrong at the end of this?* If the honest answer is "nothing", the story has ended there. The tell in the draft is the scene that follows: it will be an on-ramp, orientation, someone deciding to do a new thing for a new reason, and it will read as chapter one of a different book. The repair is `seq.partial_resolution`, with its own rule enforced — **the new problem must be produced by the win, not bolted on to it.** The win is the right place to look: what did getting this cost, who noticed, what does having it oblige them to do. A phone ringing at the end of a resolved run is the bolted-on version and buys a page. Note the legitimate exceptions: the last run of the story, the single run of a young children's episode, which must close completely (`seq.kids_run`), and the deliberate full close before a time jump in a serial, where the next season is meant to begin from a genuinely new position.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (worst in the middle of features and in mid-season episodes, and it is the standard reason a second act "restarts" at the sixty-minute mark)
**Avoid when:** —
**Example:**
> Closed: Q — can she get the loan? Climax: the bank says yes. She walks out into the sun. End of run.
> Nothing is wrong. The next run has to begin by inventing a new problem, and the audience can feel the join.
> With residue, same scenes, same yes: the bank says yes, and the guarantor is her ex-husband, whom the manager mentions by name as though she already knew, and did not.
> The question was answered. The film is now in more trouble than it was before she got what she wanted.

**Yields to:** `seq.partial_resolution` — Leave a residue produced by the win.  
`seq.full_resolution_stop`

### The False Convergence

**Also called:** the threads that meet and nothing happens, the empty collision, the coincidental gathering
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A convergence sequence that delivers the meeting it promised and produces nothing from it — the threads arrive in the same place, exchange information or pleasantries, and separate roughly as they were.
**Effect on the audience:** The most expensive disappointment in mid-level structure, because the run spent its whole length building an expectation and then honoured the letter of it. Readers rarely forgive it, and they stop trusting the next build.
**Used for and where it works best:** Recognising it is the technique. Two tells, and both are visible in the outline. **One: the threads want the same thing and are not in conflict about it** — two people hunting the same man who meet and agree to hunt him together have converged into a longer version of one thread. **Two: the meeting's outcome is information.** If what the collision produces is that somebody now knows something, you built a corridor to a briefing. The third and least forgivable variant is the coincidental gathering — everyone in the same airport, the same hospital, the same street — where the threads did not converge, they were carried there (`seq.convergence_sequence` is explicit that the reader must see it coming and that the meeting must be worse than any single thread was). The repairs: give the threads incompatible wants before they meet, so the meeting is a collision rather than an arrival; or make the meeting cost one of them something they brought with them; or, if neither is available, **do not converge them** — two threads that have nothing to say to each other are better left parallel and allowed to comment on each other from a distance (`seq.crosscut_sequence`, `scene.ironic_parallel`).
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (worst at the end of a second act, where the convergence is usually load-bearing, and in ensemble stories, where a false convergence retroactively flattens every thread that fed it)
**Avoid when:** —
**Example:**
> False: three threads, one hotel. The debtor, the collector and the wife all arrive. They meet in the lobby. The wife learns her husband owes money. The collector agrees to wait until Monday. Everyone goes upstairs.
> Everything promised was delivered and nothing happened, because all three wanted the same outcome and the collision produced a fact.
> Repaired by making the wants incompatible before the meeting: the wife has come to pay the debt herself, with money that is not hers; the collector has been told to take the man, not the money, because an example is worth more this week; the debtor has come to confess and be taken, which is the only way he can stop.
> Now every solution is somebody else's disaster, and the three of them cannot all leave the lobby.

**Yields to:** `seq.crosscut_sequence` — Nothing to say to each other; keep them parallel.  
`seq.false_convergence`
