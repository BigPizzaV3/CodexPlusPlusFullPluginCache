# Genres — growth

Entry point into the database. Each kit is a ranked short list; open the `cat-*.md`, `arcs.md`, `situations.md` or `structures.md` file named for that id prefix in `LOOKUP.md` for the full card.

A genre kit carries something a format kit does not: **required beats.** Genre in writing is not a marketing label, it is a set of obligations. Truby's position is that each genre demands fifteen to twenty specific plot events, and that a story carrying only one genre reads as formula — you need two or three others in supporting roles. Every kit below therefore has an ordered `Required beats` block. Skip a beat and the audience feels the hole even when they cannot name it.

The six kits here are the **growth** genres: stories in which the protagonist is the thing that changes, and the plot exists to make the change expensive. They share one structural risk — change is internal, and internal change is invisible. Every beat below is an attempt to make it visible.

## Coming of Age

**What the genre argues:** that the self is not discovered, it is constructed under pressure, and the construction requires the demolition of the version your parents built. Truby files this with memoir because both are about creating the self inside a system that has already assigned you a place. The question it asks is what you have to lose in order to become someone — and the honest answer is always a person, not a belief.

**Required beats:**
1. The protagonist's age and its restrictions are made concrete in a rule they cannot break: a curfew, a wage, a school, a permission slip.
2. An adult states the world's system of authority out loud, early, as though it were weather.
3. A double desire is established — something wanted in public (the team, the trip, the person) and something wanted in secret.
4. First unsupervised act. Small, and it works.
5. The peer group's entry test — a dare, a theft, a cruelty — and the protagonist's answer to it.
6. First love or first desire, specific and slightly embarrassing.
7. Discovery of an adult's weakness: the parent, teacher or hero is caught doing something ordinary and disappointing.
8. The protagonist lies to an adult and is not caught.
9. Midpoint threshold: the body event or the licence event — the injury, the period, the funeral, the drink, the letter, the keys.
10. The friendship fractures over loyalty, not over taste.
11. The protagonist does to someone weaker the thing that was done to them.
12. The consequence lands on a third party, not on the protagonist.
13. Confrontation with the parent, in which the child uses the parent's own vocabulary against them.
14. A death, a departure or a move fixes the timeline: the world will not wait for them.
15. Double self-revelation — the protagonist sees what they did, and sees why the adult did what they did.
16. Forgiveness, or the refusal of it, expressed as an action rather than a speech.
17. The leaving: a physical departure from the place, staged as the place watching them go.
18. Final image rhyming with the rule of beat 1 — now broken, obeyed by choice, or simply irrelevant.

**Supporting genres:** Family Drama supplies the opponent, because in this genre the antagonist is almost always somebody who loves the protagonist. Love Story supplies beat 6 and the first evidence that the protagonist exists outside the family's description of them. Comedy supplies survivability — without it the genre curdles into misery, since the protagonist has no power and loses most of the fights.

**Rules:** Make the protagonist's age produce concrete restrictions, not just a tone | Give the adult opponent a case the audience can accept | Let the protagonist be genuinely cruel once, at beat 11, and do not soften it | Put the consequence on someone else | Write the double self-revelation, not the single one — seeing the adult clearly is half the growth | Express the forgiveness as an action | Decide the narrating distance before page one and hold it | End on the place, not on the face

**Avoid:** Nostalgia doing the work of drama — a soundtrack and a bicycle are not a story, and the genre attracts writers who want to feel warm about their own adolescence. The protagonist as pure victim, which removes any arc: they must do harm. The parent written as an obstacle rather than a person, which makes beat 15 impossible. And the ending where the protagonist announces what they have learned, which is the genre's single most common fatality.

**The kit (ranked):**

   The master plot. Its central rule: the protagonist must lose their innocence through their own action, not have it taken.
2. **The Lie** (Arc) `arc.the_lie`
   In coming of age the lie is inherited, not chosen — which is why beat 13 has to be fought in the parent's own words.
3. **The Positive Change Arc** (Arc) `arc.positive_change`
   The default shape. Lay it against the eighteen beats and mark where the lie is tested, not stated.
4. **Age-Specific Logic: Children and Elders** (Character) `char.age_specific_logic`
   The genre's craft floor. A fifteen-year-old reasons differently, not worse, and getting this wrong voids everything.
5. **The Beloved Antagonist** (Antagonist) `anta.beloved`
   The parent, coach or older sibling who is the obstacle and is right about several things.
6. **The Ghost** (Arc) `arc.the_ghost`
   Usually a family event that predates the protagonist's memory and still sets their conduct.
7. **The Retrospective Narrator** (Voice) `voice.retrospective_narrator`
   The genre's most-used and most-abused device. Decide how much the older voice knows, and pay for the distance.

    Beat 18. The same place, the same rule, a different person standing in it.
16. **Love Without Declaration** (Emotion) `emo.love_without_declaration`
    Beats 6 and 16. Adolescents are least able to say it, which is exactly why they are good at showing it.

## Rites of Passage

**What the genre argues:** that some problems cannot be solved, only survived, and that the protagonist's competence is the thing that stops them surviving it. Snyder's three requirements are a life problem, a wrong way of attacking it, and the acceptance of a hard truth — and the hard truth is always the same one: the situation is not going to change, so you must. This is the genre of divorce, diagnosis, addiction, bereavement, redundancy, migration and parenthood.

**Required beats:**
1. Establish the protagonist's functioning life and the one competence they rely on to run it.
2. The life problem arrives from outside their control and is undeniable: diagnosis, divorce, layoff, bereavement, retirement, a birth, a border.
3. First denial: the protagonist names the problem as temporary, and names it to somebody else.
4. The wrong-way attack begins — and crucially, it is the method the protagonist is best at.
5. The method produces real, short-lived relief. Show it working.
6. A veteran appears: someone already through this passage, who is not admirable.
7. A public failure caused directly by the coping method.
8. The people closest are enlisted into the denial. One of them refuses.
9. Midpoint: the method is escalated rather than abandoned.
10. Something irreplaceable is lost through the method, not through the original problem.
11. The rock bottom scene is private and undramatic — no audience, no music, no speech.
12. The protagonist asks for help in the smallest available way, and is misread.
13. The veteran states the hard truth and is not believed.
14. Regression, after the first visible sign of progress.
15. Acceptance: the protagonist says out loud what has actually happened, in plain words, without softening it.
16. The first ordinary act performed inside the new reality — a shift, a meal, a phone call, a journey to work.
17. New equilibrium. The problem is not solved. The protagonist is different.

**Supporting genres:** Family Drama supplies the people enlisted into the denial and the one who refuses. Love Story or Buddy Love supplies the veteran, who has to be someone the protagonist did not choose. Comedy is more necessary here than anywhere except its own genre — the audience needs relief or they will leave before beat 11.

**Rules:** Make the coping method a genuine strength, not a vice | Show the method working once, properly, before you take it away | Keep the rock bottom private and small | Never let the acceptance be spoken as a lesson — plain description only | Put a regression after the first progress | Do not solve the life problem | Give the ordinary act at beat 16 more screen time than it seems to deserve | Cast a veteran the protagonist would not want to resemble

**Avoid:** Curing the disease at the end, which converts the genre into a fantasy and betrays everyone watching who has the same problem. The epiphany scene with music and a speech — this genre's acceptance is flat, verbal, unimpressive, and that is the point. The saintly sufferer, who has no wrong way to attack the problem and therefore no arc. And treating the life problem as the antagonist: the antagonist is the coping method, and the writer who forgets that writes a documentary about an illness.

**The kit (ranked):**

1. **Opposition From the Protagonist's Own Choice** (Antagonist) `anta.self_generated`
   The genre's engine. The problem is weather; the opposition is the method the protagonist keeps choosing.
2. **The Internal Antagonist** (Antagonist) `anta.internal`
   How to dramatise an opponent with no body, and how to keep it from becoming a voiceover.
3. **The Need** (Arc) `arc.the_need`
   Rites of passage is the genre where want and need are furthest apart and the want is never granted.
4. **Transformation** (Situations) `sit.tobias_transformation`
   The master pattern for a protagonist changed by an event rather than by a goal.
5. **Competence Has a Price** (Character) `char.competence_cost`
   Beat 4 in one card. The skill that built the life is the thing now destroying it.
6. **The Wound as a Rule of Conduct** (Character) `char.wound_as_rule`
   The coping method written as policy, so it shows in behaviour long before anyone names it.
7. **Grief on the Page** (Emotion) `emo.grief`
   Whatever the passage is, it runs on loss. This card is the technical floor for writing it.
8. **Grief That Resolves** (Emotion) `emo.grief_that_resolves`
   The anti-card for beat 17. Read it before you write your ending, then make the ending smaller.
9. **Emotion Through Task** (Emotion) `emo.emotion_through_task`
   Beats 11 and 16 are both tasks. The washing-up is where this genre does its emotional work.
10. **What They Do Alone** (Character) `char.alone_behaviour`
    Beat 11 is unwitnessed by definition. The genre lives in unobserved behaviour.
11. **The Cost Paid** (Endings) `end.cost_paid`
    Beat 10. Something irreplaceable must go, and it must go because of the method.
12. **Mentor and Student** (Relationship) `rel.mentor_and_student`
    The veteran of beat 6, on the correct terms: unwanted, unimpressive, ahead on the road.
13. **Understate at the Peak** (Emotion) `emo.understate_at_peak`
    Beat 15 is the highest-pressure moment in the story and must be the flattest line in it.
14. **Sentimentality** (Emotion) `emo.sentimentality`
    The anti-card. This genre attracts it more than any other. Audit beats 11, 15 and 17 against it.
15. **Anniversary Structure** (Time) `time.anniversary_structure`
    A passage needs a calendar. Dates make invisible change measurable without narration.
16. **The Quiet Ending** (Endings) `end.quiet_ending`
    Beats 16 and 17. The most honest available ending, and the hardest to sell to a producer.

## Sports

**What the genre argues:** that a measured contest is the only place where a person's private worth becomes a public number, and that the number is never the answer. The sports story is a machine for externalising interior change — every improvement is visible, every failure is scored — which is why it is the easiest growth genre to structure and the easiest to write badly. Its real question is what you become in order to win, and whether you would trade it back.

**Required beats:**
1. The discipline's rules and its scoring are demonstrated in action before anybody explains them.
2. The protagonist's current standing is fixed by a public result: a loss, a cut, a demotion, a ranking.
3. The private reason for competing, stated once to one person and never repeated.
4. The deadline is named — the date of the game, the trial, the season, the tournament.
5. The coach or veteran arrives with a method the protagonist rejects.
6. The team or partner is assembled, each member with one named deficiency.
7. First competitive test: a loss that demonstrates the exact flaw.
8. The training sequence, built on the method from beat 5, showing specific measurable improvement.
9. The rival is shown winning cleanly, without villainy.
10. A winning run — earned, and it makes the protagonist worse as a person.
11. The body or the money fails: injury, illness, cost, suspension, family pressure.
12. The protagonist betrays the team's principle in order to protect the run.
13. The coach or a teammate says the thing the protagonist cannot argue with, and leaves.
14. A disqualifying setback immediately before the final event, so it is entered at a deficit.
15. Reconciliation, performed as work rather than as apology.
16. The big game, structured so the value turns at least three times inside it.
17. The decisive moment is a choice, not a skill: pass or shoot, stay or stop, carry on or stop the fight.
18. The result, and then the cost of the result, in the same scene.
19. Final image on the private reason from beat 3.

**Supporting genres:** Buddy Love supplies the coach or the partner, and usually carries the film's emotional line. Family Drama supplies the pressure of beat 11 and the reason from beat 3. Underdog is the frequent third and the one to watch — if you take the whole of it, you have written the default film rather than a specific one.

**Rules:** Teach the scoring in action, so the audience can read the final event without help | Give the rival a clean case and a real technique | Make the training sequence show a named, measurable gain, not effort in general | Let the winning run damage the protagonist | Put the decisive moment in a choice, not an ability | Turn the value at least three times inside the final contest | Show the cost of the result before the credits | Never let the private reason be restated in dialogue after beat 3

**Avoid:** The rival as villain, which is the genre's default and its cheapest move — it converts a contest into a morality play and removes the protagonist's need to change. Training montages that show effort rather than improvement, so that the final win reads as luck. Winning as the resolution: the value must turn on something other than the scoreboard, or the story has argued that the number was the answer after all. And explaining the sport in dialogue, which is what happens when the writer did not stage beat 1.

**The kit (ranked):**

1. **The Opponent's Better Version** (Antagonist) `anta.better_version`
   The rival as the protagonist with the flaw removed. Beat 9 and the genre's whole moral argument.
2. **The Obstacle Ladder** (Antagonist) `anta.obstacle_ladder`
   The bracket, the season, the rankings — the genre's structural gift, an opposition that escalates by design.
3. **The Need** (Arc) `arc.the_need`
   The want is the trophy. The need is beat 3. Keep them separate or you get a trophy at the end and nothing else.
4. **The Time Antagonist** (Antagonist) `anta.time`
   Beat 4 and beat 16. The clock is an opponent with no motive and perfect reliability.
5. **Competence First** (Character) `char.competence_first`
   The audience will follow anyone who is good at something. Establish the skill before the problem.
6. **Competence Has a Price** (Character) `char.competence_cost`
   Beats 10 to 12. What the training took from the rest of their life, shown and not discussed.
7. **Mentor and Student** (Relationship) `rel.mentor_and_student`
   Beats 5, 13 and 15. The method rejected, the departure, the return — the film's actual relationship.
8. **Montage** (Time) `time.montage`
   Beat 8, written properly: an ordered series of measurable gains, not a mood with music over it.
9. **The Group Under Pressure** (Relationship) `rel.group_under_pressure`
   For team sports, beats 6 and 12. The squad's real hierarchy only appears when something is at stake.
10. **The Value Turn** (Scene) `scene.value_turn`
    Beat 16. A final contest is not one scene, it is six, and each must turn.
11. **Escalating Cost** (Scene) `scene.escalating_cost`
    Beat 11. The price of continuing has to rise, or the second half is just more sport.
12. **Dread of the Known Outcome** (Tension) `tens.known_outcome_dread`
    Essential for true stories where the audience already knows the score. Move the tension to the cost.
13. **The Countdown** (Tension) `tens.countdown`
    The final minutes, handled so that time itself is doing the work rather than the commentary.
14. **The Thematic Value** (Theme) `theme.thematic_value`
    Name the value the contest is measuring — loyalty, nerve, discipline — and make the scoreboard argue with it.
15. **The Cost Paid** (Endings) `end.cost_paid`
    Beat 18. Win or lose, something is spent, and it must be shown rather than implied.
16. **Answer the Question That Was Asked** (Endings) `end.answer_the_question_asked`
    Beat 19. The dramatic question was never "do they win", and the ending has to prove you knew that.

## Redemption

**What the genre argues:** that a person is not identical to their worst act, and that proving it is enormously expensive and may not work. Redemption is the only growth genre where the protagonist starts in moral deficit, which means the usual sympathy tools are unavailable — the audience must be held by interest and by the character's own clear sight of what they did. Its question is whether atonement is something you earn or something somebody else has to give you.

**Required beats:**
1. The harm is dramatised, not reported. The audience watches the character do it.
2. The character's justification is given its best case, in their own words.
3. The sentence lands: prison, exile, divorce, disgrace, the loss of the profession.
4. Time passes and the character is shown functioning inside the diminished life.
5. A survivor or witness of beat 1 re-enters the story and does not forgive.
6. The first attempt at atonement is self-serving, and is caught being so.
7. An opportunity to return to the old behaviour — taken.
8. Somebody innocent extends trust for a reason the character has not earned.
9. Midpoint: the full extent of the harm is revealed, to the audience, to the character, or to both.
10. The character confesses to the one person whose opinion costs most, and loses them.
11. A second harm occurs that the character did not cause but is blamed for.
12. The character does not defend themselves.
13. The old crew, family or profession offers reinstatement on the old terms.
14. Refusal, at a concrete price: money, safety, reputation, a relationship.
15. An act of restitution proportionate to the original harm, performed without witnesses.
16. The survivor from beat 5 learns of it, and chooses.
17. New equilibrium. The character is not restored to the old life, and the forgiveness, if it comes, is partial.

**Supporting genres:** Crime or Gangster supplies beat 1 and the old crew of beat 13, and gives the harm a scale the audience can measure. Family Drama supplies the survivor, because the most costly witness is almost always a relative. Faith or Tragedy supplies the framework in which atonement is a real category rather than a public-relations exercise.

**Rules:** Show the harm; never let it be summarised by a third party | Give the justification its strongest possible form | Make the first atonement attempt self-serving and expose it | Let the character relapse once, fully | Size the restitution to the harm — a small kindness does not settle a large debt | Perform the restitution without witnesses | Put the forgiveness in someone else's hands, and let them be free to refuse | Do not restore the old life

**Avoid:** The offscreen crime, which lets the audience forgive on discount because they never watched anything happen. Suffering mistaken for atonement — being miserable for two acts is not restitution, and the genre is full of writers who confuse the two. The victim who exists only to grant absolution at the end, which turns a person into a ceremony. And the full restoration ending: job back, marriage back, reputation back. If everything is returned, nothing was paid, and the moral argument collapses on the last page.

**The kit (ranked):**

1. **The Moral Line** (Arc) `arc.moral_line`
   The genre's spine: the line crossed at beat 1, and every subsequent scene measured against it.
2. **Remorse** (Situations) `sit.polti_34_remorse`
   Polti's pattern for the character who cannot put the act down. The situation, not the mood.
3. **The Cost of the Truth** (Arc) `arc.cost_of_truth`
   Beat 10. Confession has to remove something the character cannot afford to lose, or it is public relations.
4. **The Positive Change Arc** (Arc) `arc.positive_change`
   The shape, but run from below zero — the protagonist is not arriving at good, only at less harm.
5. **The Moral Test** (Theme) `theme.moral_test`
   Beats 7, 12 and 14. Three tests, escalating, with the last one taken when nobody is watching.
6. **Shame and Humiliation** (Emotion) `emo.shame`
   Distinguish shame from guilt precisely: guilt is about the act, shame about the self, and only one of them motivates repair.
7. **The Ghost** (Arc) `arc.the_ghost`
   Here the ghost is the protagonist's own act, which is what makes this genre structurally different from every other.
8. **The Cost Paid** (Endings) `end.cost_paid`
   Beat 14 and beat 17. Redemption is priced in this card or it is not redemption.
9. **The Sympathy Beat** (Character) `char.sympathy_beat`
   The tool for holding an audience through beats 1 to 7 — used once, early, and never repeated.
10. **The Debt Between Them** (Relationship) `rel.the_debt`
    Beats 5 and 16. The relationship with the survivor is a ledger, and the genre is about who gets to close it.
11. **Self-Sacrifice for an Ideal** (Situations) `sit.polti_20_self_sacrifice_for_ideal`
    Beat 15's pattern, and its trap: sacrifice that buys the character admiration is not restitution.
12. **The Recontextualising Reveal** (Information) `info.recontextualising_reveal`
    Beat 9. The reveal that makes the audience re-read everything they have already forgiven.
13. **Withheld Flashback** (Time) `time.withheld_flashback`
    The alternative construction: harm shown late rather than first. Powerful, and it changes the whole contract.
14. **The Wound as a Rule of Conduct** (Character) `char.wound_as_rule`
    The post-sentence life of beat 4, written as habits and refusals instead of as regret.
15. **The Emotion Nobody Earned** (Emotion) `emo.unearned_attachment`
    The anti-card. Audit beat 16 against it — this is where redemption stories most often cheat.
16. **The Earned Ending** (Endings) `end.earned_ending`
    Every element of beat 17 must be traceable to something the character did on the page.

## Fool Triumphant

**What the genre argues:** that institutions mistake fluency for competence, and that a person who cannot speak the language is therefore the only one able to see what the institution is doing. Snyder's three components are the fool, the establishment and the transmutation — and the third is the one writers skip. The fool must end as something other than a fool, or the story has merely been laughing at someone for ninety minutes. Its question is whether innocence is a strength or just an unusually lucky form of ignorance.

**Required beats:**
1. The fool is introduced doing something absurdly simple with total sincerity.
2. The establishment is introduced by its rules and its vocabulary — the jargon is the wall.
3. The fool is moved inside by accident, error, inheritance, or somebody else's scheme.
4. First misreading: the fool takes a figure of speech literally and is rewarded for it.
5. The insider is established — the one person who can see the fool is a fool and cannot say so credibly.
6. A name, a title, a uniform or a mistaken identity attaches itself to the fool and sticks.
7. The establishment's leader endorses the fool in public.
8. The insider's first attempt at exposure fails and damages the insider instead.
9. The fool's method produces a real, measurable result the establishment cannot explain.
10. Escalation: the fool is promoted beyond anything defensible.
11. The fool is asked to perform the establishment's core competence, does it wrong, and wins.
12. The ally or love interest sees the fool clearly, and stays.
13. The insider obtains proof and delivers it at the worst possible moment.
14. Public exposure. The fool is unmasked and does not lie about it.
15. The establishment turns; the fool is removed, usually with some ceremony.
16. The establishment attempts the task without the fool and fails at it visibly.
17. Transmutation: the fool returns on their own terms, under their own name, and is no longer ignorable.
18. Final image — the establishment now speaking the fool's vocabulary.

**Supporting genres:** Satire supplies the establishment and the target, without which the fool is just a nice person in a funny job. Love Story supplies beat 12, the one relationship in which the fool is seen accurately. Underdog supplies the third act, since beats 15 to 17 are an underdog structure in miniature.

**Rules:** Make the establishment genuinely competent at something, or the satire has no target | Let the fool be sincere, never knowing — the moment they are in on it, the genre is gone | Build the establishment's vocabulary first and make it specific enough to quote | Give the insider a legitimate grievance | Have the fool refuse to lie at beat 14 | Remove the fool before the end, and show the failure that follows | Write the transmutation as a change in standing, not a change in personality | Do not explain the joke through the insider

**Avoid:** The fool who is secretly clever, which is a different genre and a much less interesting one. Cruelty without a target — if the establishment is merely absurd rather than actually powerful, the film is mocking the weak. Skipping the transmutation, so the story ends with the fool still a fool and the audience uncertain whether they were supposed to be laughing at a disability. And the insider as pure villain: the insider is correct about everything except what matters, and that is the whole joke.

**The kit (ranked):**

1. **Comic Character Construction** (Comedy) `comedy.comic_character_engine`
   The fool is a fixed engine — one sincerity, applied to everything. Build it before plotting anything.
2. **The Literal Answer** (Comedy) `comedy.literal_answer`
   Beat 4, and the genre's signature move: the figure of speech taken at face value and vindicated.
3. **Status as the Engine** (Comedy) `comedy.status_engine`
   The entire plot is a status graph. The fool rises without trying; the insider falls while trying hard.
4. **Satire With a Target** (Comedy) `comedy.satire_target`
   Name the institution and the specific practice being attacked. Without this the genre is whimsy.
5. **The Incompetence Zone** (Character) `char.incompetence_zone`
   Define precisely what the fool cannot do, and keep it consistent across eighteen beats.
6. **The Flat Arc** (Arc) `arc.flat`
   The fool does not change; the world does. Get this backwards and beat 17 becomes a personality transplant.
7. **Dramatic Irony** (Information) `info.dramatic_irony`
   The audience is with the insider on the facts and with the fool on the values. That split is the comedy.
8. **The Straight Man** (Comedy) `comedy.straight_man`
   The insider's function. Give them the audience's reasoning and then let it lose.
9. **Escalation and the Comic Spiral** (Comedy) `comedy.escalation_spiral`
   Beats 7 to 11. Each promotion must be less defensible than the last and arrive faster.
10. **The Two-Audience Layer** (Subtext) `sub.dual_audience`
    Every line the fool says means one thing to the establishment and another to us. This is the technique for it.
11. **The Institutional Antagonist** (Antagonist) `anta.institutional`
    Beat 2. The opponent is a system with procedures, and it must be able to act.
12. **Entrance Against Type** (Character) `char.entrance_against_type`
    Beat 1. The introduction that tells the audience how to read the fool for the next two hours.
13. **The Name as Design** (Character) `char.name_as_design`
    Beat 6. The genre reliably hangs its transformation on a name, a title or a uniform.
14. **The Punchline the Audience Reaches First** (Comedy) `comedy.audience_gets_there_first`
    The pleasure of this genre is arriving at the joke a half-second before the establishment does.
15. **The Exception** (Character) `char.the_exception`
    Beat 12. The one person who sees the fool accurately, which is how the audience knows it is not mockery.
16. **The Mirror Ending** (Endings) `end.mirror_ending`
    Beat 18. The opening scene replayed with the establishment in the fool's position.

## Underdog

**What the genre argues:** that the distribution of advantage is not a measure of worth, and that proving it requires the disadvantaged party to be better than the favourite rather than merely as good. It is the most politically loaded genre in this file — every underdog story is an argument about a specific system — which is why the generic version is so tiring and the specific version so durable. Its question is what the attempt costs when it fails, and whether it was still worth making.

**Required beats:**
1. The scale of the mismatch is stated in numbers, not adjectives: budget, ranking, record, votes, headcount.
2. The favourite is shown being good, not being cruel.
3. The underdog's one genuine advantage is planted early and looks worthless.
4. The system's gatekeeping is demonstrated on somebody else first.
5. The underdog is refused entry and finds an irregular route in.
6. The first contest is lost, but by a smaller margin than anyone predicted.
7. Public ridicule from a credible source, not from a fool.
8. An ally defects from the favourite's side, for a specific and slightly selfish reason.
9. The resource crisis — money, time, bodies, permission — that should end the attempt.
10. Midpoint: an unambiguous win that changes how the favourite treats them.
11. The favourite responds by changing the rules, or by applying pressure off the field.
12. The underdog is offered a way out that is genuinely good: a job, a settlement, a share, a title.
13. Refusal — and the cost of the refusal is paid immediately by somebody else.
14. The betrayal or the injury that removes the plan's key piece.
15. The advantage from beat 3 becomes the only remaining option.
16. The final contest, in which the favourite performs at their best.
17. The outcome — and if the underdog loses, the ground has still moved, and the story shows exactly where.
18. Final image on the person who first said it was impossible.

**Supporting genres:** Social Drama supplies the system, which is what separates a real underdog story from a feel-good one. Sports supplies the measurable contest and the deadline. Ensemble is the frequent third, because the underdog is usually a group and the group's internal ranking is half the drama.

**Rules:** Quantify the mismatch on the first page | Let the favourite be genuinely excellent and generally fair | Plant the advantage early and make it look like a liability | Demonstrate the gatekeeping on a third party before it hits the protagonist | Make the offered way out genuinely attractive | Charge the refusal to somebody other than the person refusing | Let the favourite play at full strength in the final contest | If they lose, show precisely what changed anyway

**Avoid:** The rigged contest, where the favourite cheats — it removes the argument, because beating a cheat proves nothing about the system. Scrappiness as a substitute for a plan: heart is not a tactic, and the audience can tell. The offered way out written as an obvious trap, which makes the refusal free. And the ending where winning fixes the system, which is the lie the genre tells most often; the honest version is that one result moves one thing, and the film names which thing.

**The kit (ranked):**

1. **Underdog** (Situations) `sit.tobias_underdog`
   The master plot, and its rule: the disadvantage must be structural, not attitudinal.
2. **Rivalry of Superior versus Inferior** (Situations) `sit.polti_24_rivalry_superior_inferior`
   Polti's frame for the asymmetric contest, and a reminder that it is a rivalry, not a persecution.
3. **The Institutional Antagonist** (Antagonist) `anta.institutional`
   Beats 4, 5 and 11. The real opponent is a rulebook with people in it, and it must be able to act.
4. **The Opponent's Better Version** (Antagonist) `anta.better_version`
   Beat 2. The favourite is what the underdog would become with resources, and knows it.
5. **The Obstacle Ladder** (Antagonist) `anta.obstacle_ladder`
   The rounds, the votes, the appeals — opposition that escalates on a schedule the audience can count.
6. **The Opposition Wins Something** (Antagonist) `anta.opposition_wins`
   Beats 11 and 14. The favourite must take a real win or the second act has no weight.
7. **Desire Made Physical** (Character) `char.desire_made_physical`
   Beat 3 and beat 15. The advantage needs an object or a technique the audience can see and remember.
8. **The Shrinking Option Set** (Tension) `tens.shrinking_options`
   Beats 9 to 15. The genre's tension is the narrowing, not the contest.
9. **Competence First** (Character) `char.competence_first`
   Establish that the underdog is actually good at something before establishing that nobody believes it.
10. **The Thematic Value** (Theme) `theme.thematic_value`
    Name what the contest measures. Without it, beat 17's losing version has nothing to land on.
11. **The Group Under Pressure** (Relationship) `rel.group_under_pressure`
    For collective underdogs, beats 9 and 14: the crew reveals its ranking when the money runs out.
12. **Escalating Cost** (Scene) `scene.escalating_cost`
    Beat 13. Every refusal has to be more expensive than the last, and the bill goes to a third party.
13. **The Opponent Is Right About Something** (Antagonist) `anta.right_about_something`
    Beat 7's ridicule should be partly accurate. That is what makes the second act uncomfortable.
14. **Write the Opponent's Case** (Antagonist) `anta.write_the_case`
    Draft the favourite's position as though you agreed with it, then decide what the story answers.
15. **The Cost Paid** (Endings) `end.cost_paid`
    Beat 17. Whether they win or lose, name the price, on screen, in the same scene as the result.
16. **Answer the Question That Was Asked** (Endings) `end.answer_the_question_asked`
    Beat 18. The question was never the scoreboard — it was whether the attempt was legitimate.
