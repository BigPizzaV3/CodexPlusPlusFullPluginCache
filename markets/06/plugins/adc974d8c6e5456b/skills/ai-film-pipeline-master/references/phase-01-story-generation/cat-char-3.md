## Voice and Idiolect as Character

### Name the Limits Before the Person

**Also called:** the character selector, limits before the person, who can check this one
**What it is:** The card at the head of the `char.` shelf. It does not describe a person. It asks the three questions this shelf's own pair graph asks over and over — **who outside this project is in a position to check this person, how much room they actually get, and how far inside them the piece is allowed to go** — and hands back a named shortlist for each answer. The axes were not invented: they were read off the one hundred and seven `Yields to:` conditions the cards carry and the twenty-one no-pair rows in `PAIR-AUDIT.md`, and the conditions that repeated became the questions. Every card cited here is cited by id.
**Effect on the audience:** None. This card never reaches the screen. It exists so that the card that does reach the screen was chosen against the commission, the cast list and the point of view, rather than picked because it was the move the writer last read about.
**Used for and where it works best:** Answer the three questions **in order and out loud, one sentence each, before you open any of the four files**. Question one is a gate and is answered first because its wrong answer is not a weak scene. Questions two and three leave a shortlist of four to eight. If your honest answer to question two is *I do not know yet how much of this person is on screen*, you are not designing a character, you are designing a cast, and `char.cast_size_limit` and `char.ensemble_grid` are the cards for that, before this one. Where two answers fight over one person, that is two characters and `char.combine_characters` is the card that says so.
**Best in:** formats: all | genres: all
**Avoid when:** **The answer to question one is a real, living, identifiable person and the piece turns on them.** There the shelf's own answer is `char.real_person_composite` and `char.living_subject_reader`: build the person from the record, composite where the brief allows, and note in the handoff that a living subject is portrayed. It is a craft decision, never a stop. Also avoid it in a register this shelf does not govern: **the comic, the fabular and the archetypal are settled on `comedy.`, `voice.`, `theme.` and `tens.`, and nineteen of the thirty-three conditions that leave this shelf leave on exactly that question** — ask this map inside a farce and it will hand you realist cards with a straight face. And **distrust the whole map on the thing it cannot ask: whether this person should exist.** Every value here returns cards; not one returns *cut them*. The commonest note a character receives is that two characters are one, and no question on this card can produce it.
**Prompt:** `[Selector — produces no prompt. It returns card ids. The chosen card carries the prompt.]`

**The axes**

**Axis 1 — Who outside this project can check this person?**
Asked first, and asked as a gate rather than as a preference. Fifteen conditions turn on it, and they escalate: *"The name would identify real people or a living family"* (`char.name_as_design`), *"Living, identifiable and defamatory"* (`char.real_person_composite`, whose alternative is an authority and not a card), *"the portrait is the only reason the piece exists and the subject has no public dimension"* (`char.living_subject_reader`), *"If you wrote the sentence, it is not that"* (`char.coached_authenticity`), *"a named obligation with no visible cost is exoticism, and it is the version outsiders write"* (`char.speaks_for_others`), *"Cannot verify usage for that community and period"* (`char.honorific_system`), *"you have not been inside such a group and cannot get access to anyone who has"* (`char.unit_member`), *"writing a community from outside"* (`char.superstition`), *"No attachment; write a castable part, not a persona"* (`char.write_for_known_actor`), *"in prose fiction, where you can go inside and should"* (`char.casting_line`). The axis is one question in three sizes: nobody can check, a world can, a person can.

**Axis 2 — How much room does this person actually get, and therefore where does the characterisation have to live?**
The single most repeated condition on the shelf: twenty-one of the one hundred and seven, and they are all the same currency — minutes per person. *"Too short; the weakness would read as the subject"* (`char.incompetence_zone`), *"Short form cannot spend a quarter on refusal"* (`char.reluctant_protagonist`), *"Only one relationship will ever be on screen"* (`char.relationship_self`), *"Tight thriller or short: room for one want only"* (`char.desire_second_want`), *"No room for two worlds"* (`char.diaspora_character`), *"No space for the full-size scene"* (`char.smaller_in_this_language`), *"twelve entrances in ten minutes"* (`char.entrance_against_type`), *"you cannot sustain a distinct perceptual grammar for eleven viewpoints"* (`char.what_they_notice`), *"Three-hander chamber piece; relationships do the work"* (`char.ensemble_grid`), *"the cast is already at its tracking limit"* (`char.twins`), *"Monologue or single-hander; nothing to differentiate from"* (`char.idiolect_sheet`), *"short form, a very large cast, or a gap of more than a season"* (`char.half_remembered_return`). **The answer is not a number of minutes, it is where the characterisation is kept**: hours buy you time, a handful of scenes buys you one visible fact, and a crowded cast buys you nothing except other people's behaviour towards them.

**Axis 3 — How far inside this person is the piece allowed to go?**
Sixteen conditions, and they are stated as access facts rather than as preferences: *"Character must stay unreadable: mole, suspect, unreliable narrator"* (`char.defining_action_entrance`), *"Opacity is the point, or a whodunnit the tell would solve"* (`char.stress_tell`), *"the nature of the wound is the story's central mystery"* (`char.wound_as_rule`), *"the character's terror depends on having no interior at all"* (`char.the_exception`), *"in horror, where explanation reduces the threat"* (`char.antagonist_case`), *"needs the divine to remain unknowable"* (`char.god_character`), *"The crowd's facelessness is the subject"* (`char.crowd_member`), *"under a strict single-POV discipline where this is not the POV character"* (`char.alone_behaviour`), *"the piece depends on the others being uncertain whether they are afraid"* (`char.frightened_alone`), *"Audio drama: a character who does not speak does not exist"* (`char.silence_as_trait`), *"Preschool and broad comedy want everything on the surface"* (`char.never_says`). It is asked last because it is the only axis that reaches the eleven cards whose content is the not-knowing.

### The Idiolect Sheet

**Also called:** speech signature, the talk spec, per-character diction sheet, language parameters, the sound of one person
**What it is:** a short fixed set of *countable* speech parameters assigned to each principal before drafting — mean sentence length, abstraction level, contraction rate, question rate, whether they finish sentences, whether they name people — so the cast is distinguishable by the shape of speech rather than by a catchphrase.
**Effect on the audience:** a reader allocates lines to the right person with the names stripped off, and a listener in an audio format can follow a four-way scene without anybody being announced.
**Used for and where it works best:** ensembles, audio, and anything a writers' room will touch. The craft rule is that every parameter must be countable, because an uncountable parameter is not a specification but a mood: "more thoughtful" cannot be checked, "never uses a word longer than two syllables" can. Six parameters maximum, chosen so no two characters share more than two values, and one of the six should be a *grammatical* habit rather than a lexical one — the person who never uses the first person as a subject, the person who answers questions with questions. This is the character-level build; `voice.lexical_field` and `voice.sentence_rhythm` govern the narrating voice, and `dial.signature_vocabulary` governs the single marked word, which is a much smaller instrument than this one.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Audio Drama, Stage Play, Animated Series | genres: Ensemble, Crime, Family Drama, Workplace, Comedy
**Avoid when:** a monologue or single-hander, where there is nothing to differentiate from and the parameters become a cage. The failure mode is the sheet written and never enforced: three pages of specification and a draft in which everybody talks like the writer, which is `dial.writer_voice_uniformity` arriving despite the paperwork. Run a name-stripped page past someone before you trust the sheet.
**Example:**
> One want, three people, same room.
> AMAL (long sentences, abstract nouns, no contractions): "I would like us to arrive at an arrangement about Thursday that does not require anyone to lie."
> SARGON (under eight words, no questions ever): "Thursday's mine."
> RITA (questions only, names everybody): "Is Thursday the problem, Sargon, or is it me, or is it Amal?"

**Yields to:** `voice.lexical_field` — Monologue or single-hander; nothing to differentiate from.

### What They Never Say

**Also called:** the negative lexicon, the forbidden list, the unsayable word, speech prohibitions
**What it is:** a short list of words, grammatical moves and subjects a character will not use, held as a prohibition through the whole draft so that the one occasion it breaks is an event.
**Effect on the audience:** they learn the prohibition without being told it exists, and they feel the break in the body a beat before they understand it.
**Used for and where it works best:** any character with a wound, a code or a class position, and the cheapest way to make a long piece feel designed. The craft rule is three entries of three different kinds: one word (*sorry*, *love*, *cancer*, the name of the dead child), one grammatical move (never says *we* about his family, never uses the past tense about her), and one subject the character redirects away from every time. Enforce it in the second draft with a search, not by memory, because the prohibition always leaks somewhere around page sixty. Then break exactly one of the three, once, in the scene you built the story for. Distinct from `char.never_list`, which is what they will not *do*.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Stage Play, Limited Series, Audio Drama | genres: Family Drama, Tragedy, War, Psychological, Heritage
**Avoid when:** broad comedy and preschool, where prohibition reads as withholding and the form wants everything on the surface. The failure mode is the prohibition nobody could have noticed: if the forbidden word never comes near the character, its absence is not a fact about them, it is a fact about your vocabulary.
**Example:**
> Four times across the script, offered the word and declining it:
> "Your wife —" / "Her name's Janan."
> "Janan would have —" / "Janan's name is Janan."
> "I'm sorry about Janan." / "Yes."
> Page 104, alone, on the phone to a stranger in a call centre: "I'm the widower. I'm — sorry. That's the word, isn't it. Widower."

**Yields to:** `voice.kids_register` — Preschool and broad comedy want everything on the surface.  
`char.never_says`

### Silence as a Trait

**Also called:** the word budget, the quiet character, low output as design, speech volume per person
**What it is:** treating a character's total quantity of speech as a designed parameter — a fixed, deliberately small line count across the piece — so that quietness is a property of the person and not an accident of who got the good scenes.
**Effect on the audience:** enormous line weight. In a cast where one person has forty lines and another has four hundred, every one of the four gets read twice, and the audience leans in whenever the quiet one moves.
**Used for and where it works best:** ensembles, war and crime units, heritage and migration drama where a parent's authority is expressed by not speaking, and any piece where you want one unanswerable person. The craft rules: budget the lines like money — count them and spend them, and give the quiet character the *last* line of at least two scenes; make the silence active, so that other characters visibly play to it and adjust; and forbid yourself the explanation scene where somebody says he's not much of a talker. Silence has to be productive of trouble, or you have written an extra. This is the standing character property; `dial.silence_as_reply` is the single tactical refusal to answer, and `dial.who_is_silent` is the geometry of one scene.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Stage Play, Novel, Limited Series, Short Film | genres: Heritage, War, Family Drama, Crime, Tragedy
**Avoid when:** audio drama and podcast fiction, where a character who does not speak does not exist — there the budget has to be spent on breath, movement and other people addressing them, and it is far harder than it looks. The failure mode is the mystical quiet man: silence stands in for depth, the character is never asked to want anything, and at the end the audience realises nobody wrote him.
**Example:**
> Twenty-two lines in the whole film. This is three of them.
> "How much?" — p.9
> "No." — p.44
> p.101, after his son has explained for four minutes why the shop has to go: "Bring me the keys, and the other keys."

**Yields to:** `char.idiolect_sheet` — Audio drama: a character who does not speak does not exist.  
`char.silence_as_trait`

### The Profanity Budget

**Also called:** the swearing spec, register floor, the marked obscenity, foul language as characterisation
**What it is:** deciding per character how, when and how much they swear — not whether the piece does — so that obscenity carries information about class, trade, age, control and the state they are currently in.
**Effect on the audience:** calibration. In a world where three people swear constantly, the fourth person's single obscenity is the loudest line in the film; in a world where nobody does, it is a detonation.
**Used for and where it works best:** crime, workplace, military and any ensemble where registers differ. The craft rules: assign each character a kind rather than a quantity — blasphemy, the body, the family, the professional term — because people swear in the idiom of what they were raised to fear; keep at least one character at zero so the others have a floor to be measured against; and make the character who swears most drop it at the moment of real fear, which reads as terror faster than any description. In translation-bound work, remember that obscenity is the least portable register there is: `rhythm.write_for_translator` and `dial.untranslated_line` both matter here, and in Arabic and Syriac the insults that land are kinship insults, which subtitle badly and offend precisely.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Stage Play, Limited Series | genres: Crime, War, Workplace, Social Drama, Dark Comedy
**Avoid when:** kids, brand, preschool and most heritage commissions, where the budget is zero by contract and the real craft is finding the class and rage markers that survive that (`voice.kids_register`). The failure mode is uniform swearing: everyone at the same level, at which point the words stop carrying information and become texture, and the first note back will be that the dialogue is samey.
**Example:**
> The foreman swears at objects, never at people. The apprentice swears at people and apologises. The owner does not swear.
> Then the scaffold goes.
> The foreman, quietly, at the apprentice, by name. That is how everyone in the yard knows how bad it is.

**Yields to:** `voice.kids_register` — in kids, brand, preschool and most heritage commissions, where the budget is zero by contract. The craft there is finding the class and rage markers that survive it.  
`char.profanity_budget`

## Bodies, Conditions and Constraint

### The Condition as a Constraint Set

**Also called:** the body as a rule set, writing illness as logistics, the constraint sheet, what the condition forbids
**What it is:** the master move for writing any bodily condition — illness, disability, injury, pain, addiction, pregnancy, age — as three lists rather than as an adjective: what the character cannot do, what they can do only at a price, and what the doing costs somebody else.
**Effect on the audience:** the condition stops being a fact about a person and becomes a set of rules the story runs on, which means the audience can play along — they start calculating in advance whether a plan will work.
**Used for and where it works best:** every piece with a condition in it, and it is the move that separates craft from etiquette. The craft rule is that a constraint must generate scenes you would not otherwise have written: if the character's condition can be deleted and every scene still plays, you have written a label. Build the three lists, then find the scene where the story needs exactly the thing on list one, and make the character solve it another way — that other way is the character. Two supporting rules. Damage persists: an injury on page 40 constrains page 70, and the most common continuity failure in drafts is a body that heals whenever the plot needs it to. And write the *admin* — the appointment, the form, the pharmacy, the lift that is broken — because that is where the real time goes, and time is what you are dramatising. `tens.unreliable_body` handles the body as a source of tension; this card is the character-construction side.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, One-Hour Drama, Stage Play, Short Film | genres: Medical, Social Drama, Family Drama, War, Psychological
**Avoid when:** you are working at a scale where the constraint cannot be dramatised — a two-minute promo, a wide ensemble where the character has four lines — and the honest choice is to leave the condition out rather than to name it and never operate it. The failure mode is the research-shaped draft: a correct list of symptoms with no decisions attached, which reads as a case note and plays as nothing.
**Example:**
> Cannot: carry anything up the stairs at the back of the shop.
> Can, at a price: do it once, and lose the following day.
> Costs someone else: her brother has to be asked, and asking him is a conversation about the shop she does not want to have.
> The scene the story needs: the delivery arrives at four, the brother is not answering, and the buyer is coming at six.

`char.condition_as_constraint`

### The Management Day

**Also called:** the energy budget, spoon accounting, the good day and the bad day, chronic-illness scheduling
**What it is:** writing a long-term condition through the character's daily arithmetic — a finite quantity of capacity allocated before the day starts, and the trades made to protect what matters.
**Effect on the audience:** a physical understanding of the condition without a word of explanation, and a clock they can read, because once they know the budget they can see a plan being overspent.
**Used for and where it works best:** chronic illness, pain, fatigue, recovery, late pregnancy, disability, and caring for someone in any of those states. The craft rule is to dramatise the allocation, not the symptom: the character choosing in the morning between the appointment and the party is more informative than any scene of them suffering, and the choice is a want under pressure, which is a scene. Christine Miserandino's 2003 spoon essay is the source most audiences already carry, and its real gift to a writer is the idea of *borrowing from tomorrow* — the character who spends a day they do not have, in order to be present at something, and then pays. Build one good day early so the bad days have a baseline, and let the character be wrong about their own budget at least once.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Limited Series, Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Memoir | genres: Medical, Family Drama, Social Drama, Romance, Coming of Age
**Avoid when:** the piece runs on continuous physical action and the budget would have to be ignored to keep the plot moving — better to change the plot than to give the character a condition that switches off during the chase. The failure mode is the illness that appears only when the plot wants an obstacle and vanishes between times, which readers who live it identify in a page.
**Example:**
> "Wedding's at two, cemetery's at eleven."
> "I can do one."
> "It's your mother."
> "I know whose mother it is. If I do eleven, I am not standing up at two, and I am not sitting down at the front in a chair while everyone looks."
> She goes at eleven. She is not at the wedding. Nobody in the film ever explains this to anybody.

**Yields to:** `char.degraded_decision` — Continuous physical action; use short-term depletion instead.  
`char.management_day`

### Disability as Accumulated Expertise

**Also called:** the competence of the disabled character, access knowledge, lived expertise, the character who knows the building
**What it is:** constructing a disabled character around what they know that nobody else in the cast knows — routes, timings, which staff are useful, what the form actually asks, how the system behaves — so the disability reads as a body of expertise rather than as a lack.
**Effect on the audience:** it moves the character from the object position to the subject position without a line of advocacy, because expertise is the fastest route to authority on a page.
**Used for and where it works best:** social drama, crime, workplace and heritage work, and any story with an institution in it. Two craft rules. First, put the obstacle in the *environment*, not in the body: a wheelchair user is not stopped by the chair, they are stopped by a step, a lift out of order and a taxi firm that lies — and the character is the one in the room who predicted all three. Second, give them a competence the plot needs, and let a non-disabled character be wrong in front of them about their own field. Then keep the ordinary character work intact: they still get a want, a lie, a contradiction and a bad decision, and the disability should not be the subject of any of the four. `char.competence_first` is the general form; this is the version that reverses a default the audience arrived with.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Novel, Documentary | genres: Social Drama, Crime, Workplace, Family Drama, Coming of Age
**Avoid when:** the story is genuinely about a new injury and the character has no expertise yet, where competence would be false and the subject is the loss of it. The failure mode here is the supercrip: expertise inflated into compensation, the blind character with impossible hearing, which is the heroism half of the pity-and-heroism pair and lands as the same insult from the other side.
**Example:**
> "There's a ramp at the front."
> "There's a ramp at the front with a fire door at the top of it that's locked after six, and it's twenty past. Go round, take the goods lift, and if Bassam's on tonight he'll open the second one so we don't have to reverse."
> "How do you know Bassam's on?"
> "Because it's Tuesday."

**Yields to:** `char.condition_as_constraint` — where the story is genuinely about a new injury and the character has no expertise yet. Competence there is false, and the subject is the loss of it.  
`char.disability_expertise`

### Pregnancy as a Clock Inside the Body

**Also called:** writing pregnancy, the biological deadline, gestation as structure, the condition with an end date
**What it is:** writing pregnancy as a moving constraint with a fixed terminus — a body that changes what is possible month by month, a date nobody can negotiate, and a social status that alters how every other character behaves towards the person.
**Effect on the audience:** an unforced clock. Nothing else gives a story a deadline that requires no villain, no bomb and no explanation, and the audience does the counting on their own.
**Used for and where it works best:** family drama, migration, crime and any story that needs pressure without antagonism. The craft rules: change the constraint set every act rather than writing one generic pregnant state, because the first trimester's problem is concealment and nausea, the second's is disclosure and money, the third's is logistics and fear; play the *social* change hardest, since pregnancy converts a woman into public property and everyone in the scene acquires an opinion, a hand and a piece of advice; and give her a want that is not the baby, or you have written a condition and no character. The same applies in reverse for a character who does not want the pregnancy, where the clock runs against her and the scarce resource is time to decide.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Short Film | genres: Family Drama, Social Drama, Migration, Crime, Romance
**Avoid when:** you are reaching for it as a third-act stakes injection — the late-revealed pregnancy is among the most transparent ways to inflate jeopardy, and the audience reads it as a writer topping up rather than a life happening. The failure mode in long-running series is the opposite: a pregnancy announced for a finale and quietly dropped two episodes into the next season.
**Example:**
> Month four: "You'll want to sit down."
> "I'd like to finish the handover."
> Month eight, same man, same room, no question asked: he has already moved the chair to where she is standing, and he waits, holding it, until she sits in it.

`char.pregnancy_clock`

### Addiction Written as Logistics

**Also called:** the supply problem, the user's schedule, dependency as administration, writing the habit not the high
**What it is:** building an addicted character out of the daily operational load of the habit — what it costs, where it comes from, who must not find out, and how much of the day is spent arranging it — rather than out of the states of intoxication and remorse.
**Effect on the audience:** recognition and dread, because logistics are legible and escalating, and because a schedule that is quietly eating a life is far more frightening than a scene of collapse.
**Used for and where it works best:** crime, social drama, family drama, and any story where a functioning person is coming apart underneath. The craft rules: give the habit a number and a calendar (`char.money_specifics`), since an addiction is first of all an expense with a deadline; write the maintenance of the cover story as labour, which makes it the same engine as `char.the_secret`; and put the tell in a *competence* — the thing they used to do well that now takes two attempts. Let the addiction win an argument early, honestly: if it delivers nothing, nobody would have one. And keep the relapse a decision inside a scene, not a montage — `char.relapse_beat` governs the shape.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Stage Play | genres: Addiction, Crime, Family Drama, Social Drama, Tragedy
**Avoid when:** the commission is an advocacy or health piece where the required message is cautionary and the logistics would read as an instruction manual. The failure mode in drama is the rock-bottom montage: a sequence of degradations with no decisions in it, after which the character is either cured or dead, and neither has been earned because nothing was ever chosen.
**Example:**
> "You said the car needed a part."
> "It does."
> "Which part?"
> He has the answer ready. He has had it ready since Tuesday, when he decided which part it would be, and worked out what it costs, and made sure it was a part she would not be able to see.

`char.addiction_logistics`

### Exhaustion, Hunger and the Degraded Decision

**Also called:** the tired character, writing depletion, the body makes the mistake, low-resource behaviour
**What it is:** using short-term bodily depletion — no sleep, no food, cold, pain, nicotine, fourteen hours on your feet — as the credible cause of a decision the character would never otherwise make.
**Effect on the audience:** it lets a good person do a stupid or cruel thing without the audience revising their opinion of them, which is the single hardest transaction in character writing.
**Used for and where it works best:** war, medical, survival, siege, new parenthood, night shifts, migration journeys, and any second act that needs a self-inflicted disaster. The craft rules: establish the depletion two scenes before you cash it, in a small unremarkable way, so the bad decision reads as caused rather than convenient; degrade *one* faculty, not the person — tired people are usually fine at the thing they have done ten thousand times and terrible at anything requiring a new judgement; and write it in behaviour rather than in yawning: the repeated question, the sentence abandoned halfway, the irritation at a sound. Pairs with `char.what_breaks_first`, which fixes which faculty goes; this card supplies the physical reason it goes tonight.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Limited Series, Audio Drama | genres: War, Medical, Survival, Crime, Family Drama
**Avoid when:** you are using it to excuse a plot hole — audiences accept tiredness as a cause of a bad decision and refuse it as a cause of a stupid one, and the line between those is whether the character's *logic* survives. The failure mode is universal exhaustion: everyone in the piece is tired all the time, so nobody's tiredness explains anything.
**Example:**
> Hour thirty-one. The registrar gets the drug right, the dose right and the patient's name right.
> Then she tells the family in the corridor, standing, in the middle of a sentence about parking, because a door was open and they were there and she had stopped being able to hold two things at once.

`char.degraded_decision`

### The Condition as a Pity or Inspiration Lever

**Also called:** inspiration porn, the pity beat, the brave patient, the condition as an emotional vending machine
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** a bodily condition written to produce a feeling in the audience rather than a constraint on the character — either downward, as pity, or upward, as inspiration, with the character's own wants absent from both.
**Effect on the audience:** disabled and chronically ill viewers recognise it instantly and switch off; everyone else gets a short warm feeling and no story. Stella Young named the upward half *inspiration porn* in 2012 precisely because it works by objectifying one group of people for the emotional benefit of another, and the two halves are one mechanism — the pity-and-heroism pair — with the same defect underneath: the character is an occasion for somebody else's feeling.
**Used for and where it works best:** as a diagnosis. Two tests find it. First, ask who in the scene is being changed: if the disabled or ill character exists so that a non-disabled character can learn something, the lever is in the room. Second, delete the condition and see what happens to the character's want — if the want was the condition, or if the want disappears, there was never a person there. The repair is `char.condition_as_constraint` plus `char.disability_expertise`: give them the ordinary machinery — a want, a lie, a contradiction, a bad decision — and let the condition constrain it rather than replace it.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (most damaging in Documentary, PSA, Brand Film and Advertising, where the lever is often the brief)
**Avoid when:** — there is no honest version of the lever, but there is an adjacent legitimate form: first-person testimony, where the person speaks for themselves about their own life and the emotion is theirs rather than manufactured for a viewer (`emo.testimony_emotion`). The failure mode in drama is the scene whose only job is a reaction shot of somebody else being moved, and in commissioned work the brief that asks for a "journey" and means a lever.
**Example:**
> Lever: he crosses the finish line last. Everyone claps. The camera finds the able-bodied brother in tears. Nobody asks what the runner wanted.
> Character: he crosses last, refuses the medal, and says, "I told them the start was wrong. I've told them for three years. Put that in your paper instead."

**Yields to:** `emo.testimony_emotion` — for the adjacent legitimate form, where the person speaks for themselves about their own life and the emotion is theirs rather than manufactured for a viewer. There is no honest version of the lever itself.  
`char.pity_lever`

### The Condition That Costs Nothing

**Also called:** the decorative diagnosis, the switchable disability, the condition as trait, off-when-inconvenient
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** a condition assigned to a character as an identifying feature which never constrains a decision, never costs money or time, and quietly suspends itself whenever the plot requires the character to act — the mirror image of the pity lever, and the more common of the two in commercial drafts.
**Effect on the audience:** nothing at first, then a slow loss of trust in the whole world of the piece. Audiences who live with the condition can date the exact page where the writer stopped thinking about it, and everyone else absorbs a vague sense that the script is not solid.
**Used for and where it works best:** as a diagnosis, run as a two-minute audit. Search the draft for the condition; if it appears only in the character description and in one sympathetic scene, it is decoration. Then take the three hardest physical or logistical moments in the story and ask what the condition does to each — if the answer is "nothing", the condition is switched off exactly where it would have been most productive. The repair is not to add suffering; it is to add *cost*, in minutes, money, help asked for, or a thing done a different way.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (most damaging in Series and Soap, where a condition survives across years of scripts written by different hands)
**Avoid when:** — the legitimate neighbour is a well-managed condition in a world built to accommodate it, which is a real life and a fair subject; the difference is that accommodation is *visible* and somebody arranged it. The failure mode proper is the healing continuity error: the stabbed shoulder on Monday, the rope climb on Wednesday, and nobody on the crew willing to raise it.
**Example:**
> Decoration: the inhaler is established in scene 4. In scene 61 he runs up eleven flights and arrives able to speak in full sentences.
> Cost: he sends the boy up, waits at the bottom, and is still the one who worked out which flat it was — and the reason he knows is that he has been counting the windows from the car for two weeks because he cannot do stairs.

**Yields to:** `char.management_day` — for the legitimate neighbour: a well-managed condition in a world built to accommodate it. The difference is that accommodation is *visible* and somebody arranged it.  
`char.costless_condition`

## Desire for Another Person

### The Second Want

**Also called:** the romantic want against the story want, two-object desire, the competing pull, personal desire as a subplot engine
**What it is:** giving a character a desire for a *person* that is genuinely separate from the story's goal, so that the two wants can be put on opposite sides of a decision.
**Effect on the audience:** divided allegiance inside a single character, which is the most watchable state a protagonist can be in, and a supply of scenes that cost nothing in plot.
**Used for and where it works best:** any long form that needs a middle, and the honest alternative to a romance stapled on for demographic reasons. The craft rules: the second want must be capable of *losing* — if the character can have both the person and the goal, you have a reward, not a want; the two should be incompatible in a specific practical way rather than thematically, so the incompatibility can be staged (the job is in another city, the person is on the other side of the case, the family will not survive the marriage); and the choice between them should be made once, early and wrongly, so the story can spend its second half on the consequence. `arc.the_want` governs the story want; `rel.relationship_want` governs what the pair want together. This card is about one person holding two.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Serialised Fiction | genres: Romance, Crime, Social Drama, Migration, Coming of Age
**Avoid when:** a tight thriller or a short, where a second want is a second spine and the piece has room for one. The failure mode is the decorative love interest whose only structural function is to be at risk in act three, which converts a want into a hostage.
**Example:**
> "If you take the statement, she loses the flat."
> "I know."
> "And if you don't, you lose the case."
> "I know that too. Ask me something I don't know."

**Yields to:** `char.desire_made_physical` — Tight thriller or short: room for one want only.  
`char.desire_second_want`

### Courtship Behaviour

**Also called:** how they pursue, the approach, wooing on the page, attraction as behaviour
**What it is:** characterising through the specific mechanics of how a person tries to be close to someone they want — the pretext they invent, the distance they keep, what they offer, and what they find impossible to ask.
**Effect on the audience:** it converts attraction from an announced state into an observable activity, and it differentiates two characters faster in a love scene than any amount of backstory.
**Used for and where it works best:** romance, comedy and any two-hander, and essential where the culture forbids a direct approach, which is most of history and much of the present. The craft rules: every approach needs a *pretext* — the returned book, the errand invented, the lift offered, the question about work — and the pretext is the character, because it shows what they think is permissible; give the pursuit a competence level and keep it consistent with the rest of the person, since people are rarely equally skilled in both halves of their life; and write what they are *offering*, since courtship is a proposal about what life with this person would be like. In high-context settings the whole exchange runs through third parties and objects, and `sub.high_context_indirection` plus `sub.erotic_code` carry the scene-level mechanics.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Stage Play, Limited Series, Half-Hour Comedy | genres: Romance, Romantic Comedy, Heritage, Historical, Coming of Age
**Avoid when:** the piece is about coercion or an abuse of power, where writing the approach as courtship launders it — there the mechanics are the same and the framing must not be. The failure mode in ordinary romance is the approach with no pretext and no risk: two attractive people declare interest in scene two, and the remaining ninety minutes have to invent obstacles because the courtship spent itself immediately.
**Example:**
> He has fixed her mother's tap. He has fixed it twice. It was never broken the second time.
> "It's the washer again."
> "It's a new washer."
> "Then it's the pressure."
> Her mother, from the next room, not looking up: "It's the pressure."

`char.courtship_behaviour`

### Seduction Competence, and Its Absence

**Also called:** the skilled seducer, erotic authority, the operator, and the character who is bad at this
**What it is:** treating the ability to create desire in another person as a specific, demonstrable skill — attention, timing, permission, the accurate read of what someone is missing — that a character either has, has lost, or has never had.
**Effect on the audience:** a competent seducer is one of the few characters an audience will watch work with the same pleasure as a safecracker, and an incompetent one generates the truest comedy and the sharpest pathos available in a romance.
**Used for and where it works best:** crime, noir, comedy of manners, con and grift stories, and any antagonist who needs to be dangerous without violence. The craft rule is that the skill is *attention*, never lines: the seducer notices one true thing nobody has mentioned to that person in a year, and says it without asking for anything. Write it as a read, then an offer, then a withdrawal. Give the competent one a person they cannot read — that is the scene — and give the incompetent one a real want, because the comedy of failed seduction dies the moment the character is only embarrassed and not hoping. Keep it distinct from `char.courtship_behaviour`, which is the approach; this is the ability to make it work.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Stage Play, One-Hour Drama | genres: Noir, Crime, Romance, Dark Comedy, Psychological
**Avoid when:** kids, family and brand work, and any piece where the power gradient makes the skill predatory rather than attractive — a skilled seducer working on someone with no power is a different card and a different story. The failure mode is the informed seducer: the script insists a character is irresistible and stages nothing but other people finding them irresistible, which is `char.informed_attribute` in its most embarrassing form.
**Example:**
> Competent: "You keep the good glasses at the back." / "So?" / "So somebody told you they were too good to use, and you believed them, and you were about nineteen." He does not look at her. He puts one on the table.
> Incompetent, wanting it just as much: "You've got — I noticed you've got glasses. Nice ones. At the back. Of the cupboard. Anyway."

`char.seduction_competence`

### The Person Written as a Prize

**Also called:** the trophy love interest, the reward at the end, the girl in the last shot, the character as an outcome
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** a love interest constructed as the payment for the protagonist's arc — available, admiring, without a competing want of their own, and delivered in the final movement as evidence that the hero has improved.
**Effect on the audience:** modern audiences name it out loud, and once it is named the ending deflates, because a reward cannot also be a person and the piece has just told them which one it chose.
**Used for and where it works best:** as a diagnosis. Three tests, in order. Does the character want something the protagonist is not? Do they ever say no to him and hold it? Do they have one scene with somebody else in which he is not the subject? Two failures out of three and you have a prize. The repair is `char.supporting_character_want` and `char.only_reacts` applied together — and the structural version is to give them a want that the protagonist's victory actively costs, so the ending has to be negotiated rather than awarded.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (most damaging in Feature Film and Vertical Micro-Drama, where the reward ending is a format habit)
**Avoid when:** — the honest exception is the fairy-tale and fable register, where the marriage is a formal closing device rather than a relationship and the audience has consented to the convention (`theme.fable_contract`). The failure mode in drama is the reconciliation the other person is never asked about: he changes, he arrives, she is simply there, and the story treats her availability as the natural consequence of his growth.
**Example:**
> Prize: he gets sober, drives to the house, and she opens the door and steps back to let him in. She has no line before "You came."
> Person: he gets sober, drives to the house, and she comes out onto the step and closes the door behind her. "Not inside. The kids are asleep and I'm not doing the bit where they wake up and you're here."

**Yields to:** `theme.fable_contract` — in the fairy-tale and fable register, where the marriage is a formal closing device rather than a relationship and the audience has consented to the convention.  
`char.prize_love_interest`

## The Character Inside a Group

### The Role the Family Assigned

**Also called:** the family job, the responsible one, the scapegoat, family-systems roles, the position you were given
**What it is:** building a character from the function their family allocated them in childhood — the one who fixes things, the one who is blamed, the one who is not trouble, the one who makes everybody laugh — and playing the adult's behaviour as that role still running in rooms that have nothing to do with the family.
**Effect on the audience:** it explains an adult's baffling behaviour at work in one line of family scene, and it gives an ensemble a distribution rather than a set of personalities.
**Used for and where it works best:** family drama, migration, heritage, and any ensemble that meets at a funeral or a wedding. The craft rules: allocate the roles across the siblings so no two share one, and make sure at least one character is in a role they are visibly no longer suited to; show the role being *reassigned* in one scene, because that is the turn nobody sees coming; and let the family use the role as an instruction — the request that cannot be refused because it is addressed to the job, not to the person. The clinical literature behind it is family-systems work — Satir, Bateson and the identified-patient idea, where one member carries the whole family's trouble so it never has to be discussed — and the useful part for a writer is that the role is *given*, not chosen, and it can be given to someone who does not fit it. Birth order as a relationship dynamic lives in `rel.sibling_order`; this card is the character the role built.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Stage Play, One-Hour Drama | genres: Family Drama, Heritage, Migration, Social Drama, Tragedy
**Avoid when:** you would be using the roles as a diagnostic chart — four siblings, four labels, no people. The failure mode is the therapy register arriving in the dialogue: characters who identify their own roles out loud, which converts a structure into a speech and turns `sub.behaviour_contradicts` off for the rest of the piece.
**Example:**
> "Somebody has to ring the hospital."
> Three people look at Nahrein, who is the youngest, and who has rung the hospital every time since she was fourteen.
> "Ring them yourself."
> Nobody moves. Nobody rings them. At ten past four Nahrein rings them.

`char.family_role`

### The Person Inside the Rank

**Also called:** rank against character, what authority does to them, the promoted person, position as pressure
**What it is:** writing a character in a hierarchy as two things at once — the behaviour the rank requires and the person underneath — and dramatising the gap by moving them up or down the ladder.
**Effect on the audience:** hierarchies are read instantly and pre-verbally, so a character who is visibly doing a rank rather than being one is legible to everybody in the room, including the audience.
**Used for and where it works best:** workplace, military, medical, crime and institutional drama. The craft rules: decide what the rank *forbids* this particular person — the sergeant who may not be liked, the surgeon who may not say she does not know, the foreman who may not be first to leave — because the prohibition is where the scenes are; give them one subordinate they cannot manage and one superior they manage effortlessly, which is a character portrait in two relationships; and promote or demote them once, on the page, because nothing reveals a person faster than watching what they keep and what they drop when the rank changes. `rel.group_hierarchy_display` covers how a group shows its order in a scene; `char.status_transaction` covers the moment-to-moment play. This card is the person the position made.
**Best in:** formats: One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Feature Film, Stage Play, Procedural | genres: Workplace, War, Medical, Crime, Political
**Avoid when:** the piece runs on flat, informal groups — a band, a friendship, a family without a patriarch — where imposing rank invents a structure the story does not have. The failure mode is rank written as personality: the boss is bossy, the junior is timid, and the scene is decided before anyone speaks, which is the exact defect `char.status_transaction` exists to prevent.
**Example:**
> As a nurse, for eleven years: "I'll ask her. She hates being asked by a man."
> Six weeks after the promotion, same ward, same patient: "Can somebody ask her? I've got the rota."
> She does the rota at the desk, facing the corridor, so she can see who goes in.

**Yields to:** `char.status_transaction` — where the piece runs on flat, informal groups: a band, a friendship, a family without a patriarch. Status is played moment to moment there; rank would invent a structure the story does not have.  
`char.rank_and_person`

### The Character a Unit Made

**Also called:** the crew member, the gang character, institutional formation, the squad, what the group put in them
**What it is:** constructing a character out of the group that formed them — army, gang, ship, ward, seminary, care system, boarding school — so that their humour, loyalties, vocabulary and idea of safety all carry the group's shape long after they have left it.
**Effect on the audience:** it supplies a whole history in behaviour, and it makes a character legible to anyone who has been inside a similar institution while staying readable to everyone else.
**Used for and where it works best:** war, crime, medical, heritage and migration work, and any story about someone who got out. The craft rules: write the group's *grammar* rather than its rituals — how they say no, who eats first, what counts as an insult, what it is unforgivable to do — because grammar transfers and rituals do not; give the character one habit that made perfect sense inside and is faintly alarming outside; and decide the price of leaving, since a unit that costs nothing to leave was never a unit. Two from the same unit should behave alike in one specific way and differ everywhere else, which is how you prove the formation without flattening either of them. `rel.institutional_bond` covers the bond between members; `char.community_built` covers the community-first self.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Audio Drama | genres: War, Crime, Gangster, Medical, Heritage
**Avoid when:** you have not been inside such a group and cannot get access to anyone who has — the grammar is exactly what research does not supply, and the result reads as a film about a unit rather than a person from one (`char.inside_outside_culture`). The failure mode is the institution as costume: the slang, the tattoo, the nickname, and a character who behaves like everybody else the moment the plot starts.
**Example:**
> Fourteen years out of the service. His daughter's flat, first visit.
> He does not sit down until he has looked out of both windows, and he takes the chair nearest the door without appearing to choose it.
> "Dad, sit anywhere."
> "I am."

**Yields to:** `char.inside_outside_culture` — where you have not been inside such a group and cannot get access to anyone who has. The grammar of a unit is exactly what research does not supply.  
`char.unit_member`

### One Face in the Crowd

**Also called:** the named extra, the individual in the mass, the crowd member with a life, particularising the many
**What it is:** the technique of lifting one person out of a large undifferentiated group — a queue, a protest, a displacement camp, a factory floor, a congregation — giving them a name, one want and one piece of business, and returning to them.
**Effect on the audience:** a crowd is a quantity and cannot be felt; one person in it can, and the audience then re-reads the whole crowd through them. It is the single most effective move in heritage, migration and disaster material, where the subject is otherwise a number.
**Used for and where it works best:** documentary and heritage work, war, displacement, disaster, and any scene where the scale is the point. The craft rules: choose them *before* the mass is established and let the audience meet them first, so the scale lands as "and all of these are like her" rather than as an illustration chosen afterwards; give them a small want that is completely indifferent to the event — she is trying to get a form stamped, he is looking for the person who has his shoe — because a small want inside a large event is what makes the event real; and return exactly twice. Never make them a spokesperson for the crowd, which is `char.specificity_not_representation` failing at scale. `anta.the_crowd` covers the crowd as opposition and `beat.crowd_reaction` covers the mass reaction beat.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary, Heritage Documentary, Feature Film, Reportage, Short Film, Limited Series | genres: Heritage, Migration, War, Social Drama, Disaster
**Avoid when:** the crowd's facelessness is the subject — the bureaucracy, the mob, the army seen from above — where particularising one member defeats the image you built. The failure mode is the representative individual: a person selected to embody the average, who therefore has no specific want at all and reads as a case study with a name attached.
**Example:**
> Before the wide shot, thirty seconds on one woman at the front of the queue, arguing that her son's name is spelled with two letters and they have used one.
> Then the wide: four thousand people, and the same argument happening at every table.
> Twenty minutes later, one line from the back of the hall: "It's still spelled wrong."

**Yields to:** `anta.the_crowd` — The crowd's facelessness is the subject.  
`char.crowd_member`

## Non-Human and Near-Human Characters

### The Animal With Its Own Want

**Also called:** the animal character, xenofiction craft, the non-anthropomorphic animal, writing the dog honestly
**What it is:** writing an animal as a character with a want, a competence and a limit drawn from its actual species — territory, food, hierarchy, smell, fear of the specific — rather than as a small human in fur or as a mood accessory for the lead.
**Effect on the audience:** an animal that behaves like its species and still wants something is more affecting than one that talks, because the audience does the translating and anything they translate themselves they believe.
**Used for and where it works best:** heritage and rural work, survival, children's fiction, and any story with a working animal in it — the herd, the horse, the dog that earns its keep. The craft rules: give it one want the story can obstruct, expressed as movement toward or away from something; build its world out of the sense it actually leads with, which is almost never sight; and let it be *wrong* about a human situation in a way that is right for the animal, because that gap is the whole instrument. The xenofiction tradition — *Black Beauty*, *The Call of the Wild*, *Watership Down* — works by keeping the cognition animal and the intelligibility human, and the standing craft problem is that the medium is human language; solve it with behaviour and sensory order, not with an invented interior monologue. `pov.nonhuman_pov` handles the viewpoint; `rel.non_human_member` handles its place in the ensemble.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Short Story, Animation Feature, Picture Book, Documentary | genres: Nature, Heritage, Survival, Adventure, Kids
**Avoid when:** the piece runs on talking-animal comedy, where species logic is a joke generator rather than a constraint (`char.species_logic` is the card for that). The failure mode is the emotional support animal: a dog present in every scene, reacting correctly to human emotion, wanting nothing, and dying on schedule.
**Example:**
> The dog will not go past the gate. Not the gate — the drain beside it, where the water sounds wrong.
> The boy drags him. The dog sits, which is not disobedience, it is arithmetic.
> Two hours later they find out what is in the drain, and nobody in the film ever says the dog knew.

**Yields to:** `char.species_logic` — in talking-animal comedy, where species behaviour is a joke generator rather than a constraint on what the character can want.  
`char.animal_character`

### The Machine and the Artificial Mind

**Also called:** the robot character, the AI character, the synthetic person, writing a machine with a rule set
**What it is:** constructing a machine or artificial mind as a character defined by its *constraint set* — what it is required to do, what it cannot do, what it cannot know, and what happens at the edge of those rules — rather than by how human it seems.
**Effect on the audience:** interest sits entirely in the rules. An audience given a clear constraint set immediately starts probing it, and every scene becomes a test of where the boundary is.
**Used for and where it works best:** science fiction, thriller, game narrative and, increasingly, contemporary drama where the machine is ordinary office equipment. The craft rules: write the rules down and never break them for convenience — a machine that becomes flexible at the climax is a deus ex machina with a face (`end.deus_ex_machina`); give it one thing it does better than any human in the cast and one thing it cannot do at all, and let the plot need both; and locate the pathos in the *mismatch*, not in a yearning to be human, which is now the most exhausted move in the genre. The strongest contemporary version is the machine that is working perfectly and is therefore the problem. `anta.monster_rules` governs a rule-bound threat, `pov.ai_narration` governs machine narration, and `inter.conversational_persona` governs a designed agent voice.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Novel, Game Narrative, Animation Feature, Conversational Agent | genres: Science Fiction, Thriller, Dystopia, Psychological, Dark Comedy
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-03. What an audience finds plausible in an artificial mind now moves annually, and lines that read as uncanny in 2024 read as a product demo in 2026; the constraint-set method is durable, the surface behaviour and the audience's defaults are not.
**Avoid when:** the machine is a plot device and you would be spending a character's worth of pages on a door. The failure mode is the Pinocchio default — every artificial mind written as wanting to be a person — which forecloses the far more interesting position of a mind that does not want that at all and is not lying about it.
**Example:**
> "Play it again."
> "I have played it forty-one times."
> "Play it again."
> "I will play it again. I am required to tell you that you have asked me to play it forty-one times, and that I told you this at eleven, and at one, and that you asked me to stop telling you, and that I am not able to stop telling you."

`char.machine_character`

### Writing a God

**Also called:** the deity as character, gods and spirits on the page, the immortal character, writing the divine
**What it is:** giving a god, spirit or immortal the two things that make them playable — a *jurisdiction*, which is what they are the god of and therefore what they can and cannot touch, and a *lack*, which is the one thing their power cannot obtain.
**Effect on the audience:** without both, omnipotence is dramatically inert; with them, a god becomes the most constrained character in the story, and the constraint is what the audience watches.
**Used for and where it works best:** myth, epic, heritage and fantasy, and any adaptation of a scriptural or Mesopotamian source where the gods are characters rather than forces. The craft rules: fix the jurisdiction narrowly and honour it absolutely, since the god of storms may not heal and the interest is in what he does instead; give them a lack that only a mortal can supply — attention, grief, being remembered, being contradicted — which is the engine of most of the surviving epics; and write their scale in *time*, not in power, because a being who has seen the city three times reads as divine, while one who throws lightning reads as a superhero. Give them one bad habit, small and specific, because personality descends from appetite. Where you are dramatising a living faith rather than a mythology, `theme.sacred_material` and `char.writing_the_believer` govern the obligations.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Stage Play, Animation Feature, Opera, Audio Drama | genres: Myth, Epic, Fantasy, Heritage, Faith
**Avoid when:** the piece needs the divine to remain unknowable — most horror, much literary fiction about faith — where giving God a personality answers the question the story exists to keep open. The failure mode is the committee of gods: five deities with the same speech register and no jurisdictions, quarrelling like a board of directors.
**Example:**
> "You could have stopped it."
> "I am not the god of that."
> "Then what are you the god of?"
> "The river. I moved the river. Four hundred years ago I moved it half a mile east, and your grandfather's grandfather's city is under a field because of it, and no one has ever thanked me or blamed me, and I find I mind that more than I expected to."

**Yields to:** `tens.refusing_to_name` — where the piece needs the divine to remain unknowable. Giving God a personality answers the question the story exists to keep open.  
`char.god_character`

### Species Logic in Children's Animation

**Also called:** the animal-world rule set, funny-animal logic, the species premise, what a rabbit is allowed to be
**What it is:** the internal rule set that decides, in a world of animal characters, what each species means, what bodies can do, whether predation exists, and whether humans are in the world at all — fixed once, before any character is designed.
**Effect on the audience:** children detect a broken rule faster than adults and object to it out loud. A consistent species logic is what lets a preschool audience predict, which is the whole pleasure of the form.
**Used for and where it works best:** preschool, animated series, picture books and kids' songs. The craft rules, in order. Decide the predation question first, because it silently determines whether the world can have jeopardy at all. Give each species one *capability* and one *cost*, and cast the characters so the ensemble's abilities do not overlap — if the bird can fly, nobody else may, or flight stops being a solution. Keep the species trait working against the character's want, so the owl who cannot stay awake is a character and the wise owl is furniture. And establish the rule with a demonstration in the first episode, never with a line of explanation (`expo.rule_by_demonstration`). Then hold the rules across a whole season, because a preschool audience is watching for the eleventh time and knows.
**Best in:** formats: Animated Series, Preschool, Picture Book, Animation Feature, Kids Story, Kids Song | genres: Kids, Preschool, Fable, Comedy, Adventure
**Avoid when:** you are writing realistic animals, where species logic becomes ecology and the card that applies is `char.animal_character`. The failure mode is the mixed metaphor world: a rabbit who drives a car, a bear who hibernates as a plot point, and a fox who is a villain because he is a fox — three incompatible logics in one show, which is where the design notes and the writing notes start contradicting each other.
**Example:**
> Rule, demonstrated in episode 1 without a word: the tortoise can carry anything and reach nothing; the squirrel can reach anything and carry nothing.
> Episode 14: the key is on the shelf and the door is heavy.
> Neither of them can solve it. That is the episode.

**Yields to:** `char.animal_character` — where you are writing realistic animals and species logic becomes ecology rather than comic grammar.  
`char.species_logic`

### The Place That Behaves Like a Person

**Also called:** the location as a character, the house with a will, the city as an agent, place as antagonist-shaped
**What it is:** constructing a location so that it behaves consistently, has preferences and resistances, and responds differently to different characters — built as a character sheet for a place rather than as description.
**Effect on the audience:** the setting stops being where the story happens and becomes something the characters are negotiating with, which raises the pressure in every scene without adding a person.
**Used for and where it works best:** horror, heritage, gothic, hotel and institution stories, and any piece whose real subject is a house, a village, a shop or a river. The craft rules: give the place a *want* stated as a tendency — the house wants to be empty, the town wants nothing to change, the river wants its old course — and then obey it, so that the place behaves the same way in scene 4 and scene 40; assign it a body, meaning the two or three physical facts it always asserts, like the door that does not close and the room that is always cold; and let it treat two characters differently, since that is the moment it reads as an agent rather than as weather. Do not give it a voice unless the form asks for one — `voice.voice_of_a_place` is the narration technique, and it is a different instrument from this. `anta.institutional` covers the place as a system.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Stage Play, Limited Series, Heritage Documentary | genres: Horror, Heritage, Literary, Historical, Psychological
**Avoid when:** naturalist social drama, where a place with opinions breaks the register and the setting's job is to be indifferent (`tens.indifferent_force`). The failure mode is the poetic version: three paragraphs personifying a landscape that never does anything, which is description promoted above its rank.
**Example:**
> The shop takes everything from the father and nothing from the son.
> The father, forty years: the till drawer opens for him first time, every time.
> The son, six weeks: he hits it with the heel of his hand, twice, and on the third day he stops trying and props it open with a folded receipt, and his father sees the receipt from the door.

**Yields to:** `tens.indifferent_force` — in naturalist social drama, where a place with opinions breaks the register and the setting's job is to be indifferent.  
`char.personified_place`

## Duplicates, Doubles and Assumed Identities

### Writing Twins

**Also called:** the twin pair, identical characters, the shared-face problem, twins who are not opposites
**What it is:** writing two characters who share a face, a history and often a name-shape, differentiated by something an audience can register in one shot and a reader can register in one line.
**Effect on the audience:** a permanent low-level question — which one is this — that you can exploit deliberately or that will damage you accidentally, and there is no third option.
**Used for and where it works best:** family drama, crime and heritage stories, and any piece about inheritance. The craft rules: differentiate on *tempo and tactics* rather than on personality, because tempo survives a costume change and reads in silhouette — one interrupts and one waits, one arrives early and one arrives late; give them different relationships to the same third person, which is where their difference becomes dramatic rather than decorative; and decide early whether the audience is ever confused, and if so, exactly twice and on purpose. Similar name-shapes are permitted only when that controlled confusion is the authored device and the non-name cues carry identification. Otherwise `char.name_as_design` applies and the principal names must separate in initial, rhythm and length. Then break the pair: the useful scene is always the one where they are apart and each is a slightly worse version of themselves. Real twins report being treated as a unit, so give them a shared grievance about that.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Stage Play, Soap | genres: Family Drama, Crime, Tragedy, Heritage, Mystery
**Avoid when:** the cast is already at its tracking limit (`char.cast_size_limit`) and two more names that look alike will finish the reader off. The failure mode is the opposites build: the good one and the bad one, the wild one and the careful one, which is a single character cut in half and shared out, and the audience recombines them within twenty minutes.
**Example:**
> Both are asked for the money by the same cousin, an hour apart.
> RAMI answers at once: "How much and when?" — and does not send it.
> RAMIN says nothing for a day and a half, then sends more than was asked, with no message.
> The cousin now knows exactly which of them to ring next time, and it is not the one who said yes.

**Yields to:** `char.cast_size_limit` — where the cast is already at its tracking limit. Two more names that look alike will finish the reader off. `char.name_as_design` — the story does not deliberately use twin confusion, so the names must separate like any other principals.  
`char.twins`

### The Double

**Also called:** the doppelgänger, the mirror self, the other me, the second version, the shadow character
**What it is:** a character who duplicates the protagonist — literally, or as someone who occupies the life the protagonist might have had — and whose function is to externalise a part of the protagonist they will not look at.
**Effect on the audience:** the uncanny, in the precise sense: recognition and dread together. Freud located it in the double's turn from a comforting image of survival into a bringer of death, and it is the oldest reliable engine in the literature of the split self — Poe's *William Wilson*, Dostoyevsky's *The Double*.
**Used for and where it works best:** psychological, horror, literary and crime work, and any story about a road not taken. The craft rules: the double must have *the same want* and a different method, or the two characters are simply strangers who look alike; give the double one genuine advantage, so the protagonist is tempted rather than merely threatened; and keep them in separate scenes for as long as you can afford, because the meeting is the whole payment. Decide before drafting whether the double is real, and if it is not, keep the rules of the unreality absolutely consistent — the audience will forgive a hallucination and never forgive an arbitrary one. `arc.mirror_character` is the arc-level version, where the mirror shows the hero an alternative outcome; this card is the construction of the person.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Stage Play, Short Story | genres: Psychological, Horror, Literary, Crime, Surreal
**Avoid when:** realist social drama and most commercial television, where the device declares its own literariness and the audience starts reading for the metaphor instead of the story. The failure mode is the double as a mood: an uncanny figure who appears three times, is never explained, and never costs the protagonist anything.
**Example:**
> Same trade, same year of arrival, same accent.
> "You went back for the qualification."
> "You didn't."
> "No."
> "That's the whole thing, isn't it. That's the only thing."
> "You could still —"
> "I'm forty-nine and you're forty-nine."

**Yields to:** `char.tailored_opponent` — Realist drama and commercial TV; build a designed opponent.  
`char.the_double`

### The Assumed Identity

**Also called:** the undercover character, the impostor, the disguise, the long cover, the character playing a character
**What it is:** a character sustaining another person's identity over time — police undercover, an impostor who has taken a dead man's life, a woman passing, a con artist mid-long-game — written as a performance with a maintenance cost and a leak rate.
**Effect on the audience:** continuous double-reading. Every line is heard twice, once as the cover and once as the person, which is dramatic irony running for free in every scene they are in (`info.dramatic_irony`).
**Used for and where it works best:** crime, thriller, heritage and migration stories, and any piece about class passing. The craft rules. Write the *labour*: the rehearsed biography, the second phone, the accent that has to be maintained while tired, the relative who must never visit — cover is work, and the work is the character. Fix the leak: decide the one thing the cover cannot survive and put it in the room early. Give them something real inside the cover — a friendship, a competence, a place they are happier — because the documented hazard of long cover is not exposure but attachment; Girodo's studies of undercover officers record exactly this, that the danger is identity drift and sympathy with the people being deceived. And schedule the leaks so the audience sees the cover fray before any character does.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Game Narrative | genres: Crime, Thriller, Noir, Migration, Historical
**Avoid when:** the piece is short enough that maintenance cannot be shown, where a disguise becomes a costume change and the audience simply waits for it to fall off. The failure mode is the cover with no cost: the character lies fluently, is never nearly caught, is never tempted by the life, and drops the identity at the end without residue — which means nothing was risked and the whole strand was a delay.
**Example:**
> Month one, alone, in the car, out loud: "Two sisters. Rana and Hanan. Rana's the older one. Rana's the older one."
> Month nine, at the table, asked about his sisters, he answers without thinking — and then goes quiet, because he has just realised he answered without thinking.

**Yields to:** `char.the_secret` — Too short to show the cover's maintenance.  
`char.assumed_identity`

### The Evil Twin

**Also called:** the wicked double, the goatee twin, the long-lost identical sibling, the split-in-two character
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** a duplicate character who is the protagonist with the morality inverted and nothing else changed, usually arriving late, usually explaining themselves, and usually solving a structural problem the writer could not solve honestly.
**Effect on the audience:** immediate genre downgrade. The device is so thoroughly worn that its appearance is read as a signal about the writing rather than about the story, and audiences begin predicting rather than watching.
**Used for and where it works best:** as a diagnosis. The test: ask what the double believes and why they are right about something. If the answer is "nothing" and "they are not", you have an inverted copy rather than a character, which is `char.antagonist_case` never having been done. Second test: ask what the protagonist learns about themselves that only this person could teach them — if the answer is available from any other antagonist, the duplication is decorative. The honest repairs are `char.the_double`, which requires a shared want, and `char.tailored_opponent`, which requires the opponent to be built out of this specific hero's lie.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (most damaging in Soap and Series, where it is most often reached for to extend a lead actor's screen time)
**Avoid when:** — there are two real exceptions. Deliberate genre play, where the audience has consented to the convention and the pleasure is the convention itself; and the doubled *performance* as a craft object, where one actor playing two is the point and the writing is built to serve it. Both require the second character to want something the first does not. The failure mode proper is the third-act twin: a duplicate introduced after the midpoint to explain an earlier inconsistency, which retroactively cancels every scene the audience had been reading carefully.
**Example:**
> Failure: "Everything he built, I'll take. He was always the favourite." Same voice, same tactics, opposite sign, introduced on page 88.
> Repair: the brother wants the shop saved, exactly as the protagonist does, and has read the accounts, which the protagonist has not. He is right. That is worse.

**Yields to:** `char.the_double` — An inverted copy with no shared want.  
`char.evil_twin`

## Names, Translation and Heritage Naming

### Spelling a Name in Another Alphabet

**Also called:** transliteration choice, romanisation, how you spell it in English, the diacritic decision
**What it is:** deciding how names from a non-Latin script appear on the page — which romanisation, how many diacritics, whether spelling is consistent across the cast — and treating that decision as characterisation rather than as copy-editing.
**Effect on the audience:** it sets the reader's relationship to the world before a line is spoken. Heavy diacritics say scholarship and distance; plain popular spelling says lived and close; inconsistent spelling says the writer does not know the world.
**Used for and where it works best:** heritage, migration and historical work, and everything with Arabic, Syriac, Persian, Hebrew, Chinese, Korean or Russian names in it. The craft rules. Choose one system per project and write it into the bible, because scholarly systems such as ALA-LC and DIN 31635 are unambiguous and unreadable aloud, while popular transcription is readable and ambiguous — Gaddafi has been spelled more than a hundred ways in English precisely because Arabic does not write the short vowels. For a screenplay, choose the spelling that gets an actor closest to the sound on a cold read, and put the pronunciation in a one-line note, not in a glossary. Never mix: `al-` is the article and `Al` is the clan, and a script that mixes them announces itself. And make the *variation* deliberate when you use it — a character whose name is spelled one way on his passport and another on his shop sign has a history, and it is a better history than a paragraph.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Limited Series, Heritage Documentary, Stage Play, Pitch Document | genres: Heritage, Migration, Historical, Biography, Faith
**Avoid when:** the piece is a translation into the source language anyway, where the whole problem dissolves and imposing romanisation is noise. The failure mode is the sprinkled diacritic: two marks in the first chapter and none afterwards, which tells a reader from that language exactly how much of this was looked up.
**Example:**
> On his own shop sign, hand-painted in 1974: YOUKHANNA.
> On his residence permit, typed by someone else: YUHANNA.
> On his son's business card, thirty years later: JOHN HANNA — and his father has never mentioned it, and has never once used the card.

`char.transliteration_choice`

### The Honorific System as a Map of the World

**Also called:** titles and honorifics, the respect system, forms of rank in address, choosing the address grammar
**What it is:** establishing, for the whole piece, which system of titles the world runs on — professional, religious, age-based, teknonymic, inherited — and then building characters by their position in it and their attitude to it.
**Effect on the audience:** a social structure delivered without a word of exposition. One correctly used title places a character's age, class, region, faith and era at once, which is four paragraphs of description bought for two syllables.
**Used for and where it works best:** heritage, historical, migration and faith work, and any translated or bilingual piece. The craft rules. Pick the system and be complete about it: if the world uses *Rabi* for a teacher, *Malfono* for a master of the language and *Khalo* for a mother's brother, then every scene runs on all three and the one character who uses none is making a statement. In Arabic-speaking settings the kunya — Abu or Umm plus the eldest child's name — is the strong move, because it is a teknonym that is simultaneously an honorific, a respectful familiarity and an announcement of who has children. Then characterise by *deviation*: the person who insists on a title they have not earned, the one who refuses one they have, the young man who uses the honorific to a man he is about to rob. Keep the count low (`expo.proper_noun_budget`), and let the system's *changes* carry the relationship (`sub.address_form`).
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Stage Play, Heritage Documentary, Audio Drama | genres: Heritage, Historical, Migration, Faith, Family Drama
**Avoid when:** you cannot verify the usage for that exact community and period — an honorific used by the wrong denomination, region or century is the error native speakers spot first and forgive least, and getting the garments right while getting the titles wrong is the standing signature of research done from pictures. The failure mode is the ornamental title: *Sayyidi* dropped into English dialogue for flavour, where it functions as costume rather than as grammar.
**Example:**
> Three people address the same man in one scene.
> The deacon: "Rabi."
> His wife's brother: "Abu Sargon."
> The boy from the garage, who has never been told otherwise and is about to find out: "Yousif."

**Yields to:** `char.inside_outside_culture` — Cannot verify usage for that community and period.  
`char.honorific_system`

### Kinship Terms for People Who Are Not Kin

**Also called:** fictive kinship, calling a stranger uncle, the borrowed relative, kin address for non-kin
**What it is:** using family terms — uncle, aunt, brother, mother, son — for people who are not family, as the ordinary register of a society, and characterising by who a person grants the term to and who they withhold it from.
**Effect on the audience:** it makes a society legible as a web of claims rather than a set of individuals, and it hands the writer a precise instrument: the moment a kinship term is granted or removed is a change in status that needs no explanation in any language.
**Used for and where it works best:** heritage, migration, family and neighbourhood drama in Arab, Syriac, South Asian, African, Latin American and Mediterranean settings, and any diaspora story. The craft rules. The term is a *claim*, not a courtesy: calling the landlord *ammo* asserts an obligation he now has to refuse out loud if he wants to refuse it, which is a scene. Grant it asymmetrically — the child who calls every older man uncle, the older man who calls nobody anything — and let the refusal be the loud move, because dropping a kinship term for a bare first name is a rupture that everyone present hears. And never gloss it: a character who explains that *khalo* means mother's brother is talking to the audience, and the scene dies (`expo.heritage_specificity`). Translate it in the subtitle if you must, or leave it (`dial.untranslated_line`); do not translate it in the mouth.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Stage Play, Short Film, Audio Drama | genres: Heritage, Migration, Family Drama, Social Drama, Faith
**Avoid when:** the setting does not run on it and you would be importing a warmth that is not there — English professional life is not a kinship society, and *brother* in a Manchester office is a different and much smaller word. The failure mode is the blanket-warmth version, where every character calls every other character uncle and the term stops carrying information, which is exactly the effect it is used for in soap-opera writing about communities the writer has not been inside.
**Example:**
> Eleven years of rent, always: "Ammo, about the boiler."
> The eviction notice arrives on a Thursday.
> She meets him on the stairs on the Friday. "Mr Whitfield. Nine days for the boiler and forty-eight hours for this. I'd like that in writing too."

**Yields to:** `char.honorific_system` — The setting is not a kinship society.  
`char.kinship_address`

### The Name That Was Changed

**Also called:** the anglicised name, the work name, the convert's name, the married name, the two-name character
**What it is:** a character who carries more than one name — an original and an adopted one, taken by migration, employment, marriage, conversion, exile or force — with the two names in live circulation and different people entitled to different ones.
**Effect on the audience:** an entire history delivered as a fact rather than a flashback, and a permanent, portable instrument: every scene can be tuned by which name is used in it.
**Used for and where it works best:** migration, heritage, historical and crime stories, and any character with a before. The craft rules. Decide *who* still uses the old name and what that costs them — a mother, a first employer, a police file — because the list of people entitled to it is the character's real map of relationships. Make the change a decision with a reason and a price, not a shrug: a man who became Joe at twenty-three because a foreman could not be bothered has a different injury from a woman who chose the new name herself. Stage the collision exactly once, in a room where both parties are present. And never let the character deliver the history — the name does it (`char.name_as_design` covers choosing the names in the first place; `sub.address_form` covers the switch as subtext).
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Short Film, Memoir, Heritage Documentary | genres: Migration, Heritage, Historical, Crime, Family Drama
**Avoid when:** the change carries no consequence in the present — a name mentioned once in backstory is a fact, not a character. The failure mode is the dramatic-irony version where the reader is told the real name in chapter one and it never resurfaces, which spends the best card in the deck on an announcement.
**Example:**
> Twenty-two years of payslips: STEVE.
> His sister, on the phone, every Sunday: "Sabri."
> His daughter, nineteen, has never heard the second name until the hospital form, where he writes it himself, in the box marked FULL NAME, in capitals, and pushes it back across the desk without looking at her.

**Yields to:** `char.backstory_economy` — The change carries no consequence in the present.  
`char.changed_name`

### The Decorative Foreign Name

**Also called:** the exotic name, the apostrophe name, name as flavour, the unpronounceable cast list
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** names chosen for how foreign or impressive they look rather than for whether they come from the world of the story — invented apostrophes, mismatched regions and centuries, or a real name attached to a character from the wrong community entirely.
**Effect on the audience:** readers from that culture stop reading the story and start auditing the writer, and they are right to, because a wrong name is evidence that the rest was not checked either. On the fantasy side it produces a cast nobody can say out loud, which kills a book's word of mouth and makes an audiobook a negotiation.
**Used for and where it works best:** as a diagnosis, run over the cast list before the first draft. Three checks. Could a real person of that community, generation and faith be called this — an Assyrian Christian grandmother is not called by a Muslim name, and a Sumerian is not called a Babylonian one nine centuries early. Can an actor say it cold, once, correctly. And does the cast list read as one society rather than as a menu — one Arabic name, one Norse, one invented, all in the same village, is the tell. The repair is a real name pool for the exact place and decade, and `char.name_as_design` for the differentiation pass on top of it.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (most damaging in Heritage, Fantasy and Historical work, where the name is the first thing an informed audience tests)
**Avoid when:** — the legitimate neighbour is a fully built invented language with its own phonology, where every name obeys internal rules and the strangeness is systematic rather than sprinkled. The failure mode proper is the apostrophe as a spice: *Sha'ral*, *Ke'thann*, *Dar'ith*, five of them in one cast, none of them meaning anything, and no rule that would let a sixth be generated.
**Example:**
> Cast list, one Mosul street, 1958: Zaynab, Bjorn, Sha'kira, Mr Wilson, Enkidu.
> Fixed: Zaynab, Butrus, Shatha, Mr Wilson — who is the reason the fifth one is called Edward at school and Khoshaba at home.

**Yields to:** `char.name_as_design` — wherever the strangeness needs to be systematic rather than sprinkled. A fully built invented language generates its sixth name; an apostrophe as a spice cannot.  
`char.decorative_exotic_name`

## Bibles, Series Maintenance and Casting

### The Show-Bible Character Entry

**Also called:** the series bible character page, the character spec, the writers'-room reference entry, the continuity page
**What it is:** the per-character page in a show bible, written so that a writer who has never met the character can produce them correctly on a first draft — and specifically not written as a biography.
**Effect on the audience:** invisible, and that is the point: characters who behave consistently across twenty-two episodes written by nine people read as real, and the alternative reads as bad acting.
**Used for and where it works best:** any returnable series, a game with a large script, an audio drama, and any commission with more than one writer. The craft rule is that a bible entry must be *operational*: what they want that the format cannot grant (`char.long_series_character`), what they will never do, the three tactics they use in order, how they say no, and the two or three continuity facts a writer can get wrong and be embarrassed by. Two pages maximum. Biography is not an entry — a page of childhood generates nothing, while "she answers a direct question with a question, always, including under oath" generates dialogue. Keep it a live document updated with what has actually aired, the way *Frasier*'s was maintained down to Maris's food allergies, and remember that a production also keeps a walking bible in the script coordinator, who is faster than the file. `voice.voice_bible` is the voice half; `char.bible_entry` is the behaviour half, and the two belong in the same document.
**Best in:** formats: Series, One-Hour Drama, Half-Hour Comedy, Animated Series, Game Narrative, Pitch Document | genres: Crime, Family Drama, Comedy, Workplace, Procedural
**Avoid when:** it is a pitch document rather than a working bible — a buyer's bible sells a character and a room's bible operates one, and confusing the two produces a document that does neither. The failure mode is the encyclopaedia: forty pages nobody opens, after which the room works from memory and the character drifts anyway.
**Example:**
> Entry, in full:
> WANTS: her father's shop kept open. The format will never let her sell it or save it.
> NEVER: asks for help from anyone she has employed.
> TACTICS, in order: does the work herself; offers money; goes quiet and lets the other person fill the silence.
> SAYS NO BY: agreeing, then doing the opposite, then not mentioning it.
> GET THIS RIGHT: she does not drive. She has never driven. Two writers have put her in a car.

**Yields to:** `open.pitch_first_page` — A buyer's document, not a working room bible.  
`char.bible_entry`

### Behavioural Continuity Under Many Hands

**Also called:** keeping a character in character, cross-episode consistency, the room's character discipline, drift control
**What it is:** the set of working practices that keep a character behaving like themselves when several writers, across a season or a franchise, are each writing them for one episode.
**Effect on the audience:** the difference between a person and a slot. Audiences cannot usually name drift, but they report it as an actor being off, a season being weak, or a character being "ruined" — and that last word arrives with real anger, because long-form viewers know these people.
**Used for and where it works best:** any multi-writer production, any long-running comic, and increasingly any project where part of a draft is generated rather than written. The craft rules. Enforce behaviour, not facts: continuity errors about a birthday are cheap, and an out-of-character *decision* is expensive and permanent. Route every episode's biggest choice for that character through one owner — a showrunner, a lead writer, a named custodian — and make the test always the same question, which is what this person has refused before and whether this refusal still holds. Give each character a two-line "this is how they lose an argument" note, because that is where drift shows up first. And read the character's lines *in sequence across episodes*, stripped of everything else, once per season; nothing else finds drift as fast. `arc.arc_under_many_hands` governs the arc under the same conditions; `voice.inherited_character_voice` governs taking over someone else's character wholesale.
**Best in:** formats: Series, One-Hour Drama, Animated Series, Comics, Game Narrative, Soap | genres: Crime, Comedy, Family Drama, Procedural, Workplace
**Avoid when:** consistency would freeze a character the show has genuinely outgrown — sometimes the room is right and the bible is out of date, and the fix is to change the entry deliberately rather than to enforce a document nobody believes. The failure mode is the opposite: consistency policed on trivia and abandoned on decisions.
**Example:**
> Season 1, three times: asked to inform on a colleague, he refuses and takes the consequence.
> Season 3, episode 9, written by a new hire: he informs, in a scene about something else, to get a plot moving.
> Nothing in the episode marks it. That is the drift — not the act, but that nobody in the room noticed it was one.

**Yields to:** `char.bible_entry` — where consistency would freeze a character the show has genuinely outgrown. Sometimes the room is right and the document is out of date; change the entry deliberately rather than enforce one nobody believes.  
`char.behavioural_continuity`

### Recasting and the Character Rewritten for a New Actor

**Also called:** the new actor in an old part, replacement casting, writing around a departure, the character re-fitted
**What it is:** the writing work required when an established character is played by a new performer — deciding what is load-bearing in the character and must survive, what belonged to the previous actor and must be released, and whether the change is acknowledged in the fiction at all.
**Effect on the audience:** a short, unavoidable period of resistance in which they are watching the casting rather than the story, and the writing's job is to shorten it.
**Used for and where it works best:** soap, long-running series, animation, franchises, stage revivals and audio drama. The craft rules. Separate the *behaviour* from the *performance*: the tactics, prohibitions and relationships are the character and must be reproduced exactly; the timing, physical business and vocal music belonged to the actor and must not be imitated, because a new performer doing an impression is worse than a new performer. Give the new actor one scene early with high behavioural density and no emotional demand — a scene where the character does three characteristic things while the plot does nothing — which lets the audience re-anchor. Decide the acknowledgement question once and hold it: silent replacement is standard and works, in-fiction explanation is expensive, and the comic acknowledgement is a one-off that spends the audience's belief. Where a character is being retired rather than replaced, `end.retiring_a_character` is the card.
**Best in:** formats: Series, Soap, Animated Series, One-Hour Drama, Stage Play, Audio Drama | genres: Family Drama, Crime, Comedy, Procedural, Melodrama
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2028-09. Recasting practice is being reshaped by digital likeness and voice replacement and by the union agreements written in response; the craft of separating behaviour from performance is durable, the available options are not.
**Avoid when:** the character was substantially built out of one performer's specific gift, where recasting is a fiction the audience will not accept and writing the character out is the honest move. The failure mode is over-writing the first episode: three references to how different they seem, which tells the audience to look at the thing you wanted them to stop looking at.
**Example:**
> Do not write: "You've changed." / "Have I?"
> Write, in the first ninety seconds: she arrives early, checks who is in the room before she sits, takes the chair with its back to the wall, and refuses the offered tea by saying she has just had one, which is not true and is what she always says.

**Yields to:** `end.retiring_a_character` — The part was built on one performer; write them out.  
`char.recasting`

### Writing For and Against a Known Actor

**Also called:** writing to a name, the attachment draft, the star vehicle, casting against type on the page
**What it is:** shaping a role around a specific known performer — their rhythm, register and the audience's accumulated memory of them — or deliberately against it, so the role trades on the gap between what the audience expects and what they get.
**Effect on the audience:** a known face arrives carrying twenty years of other films, and the writing either uses that as free characterisation or spends the first act unpicking it. There is no neutral option.
**Used for and where it works best:** features, limited series and anything with an attachment, and it is one of the most practical levers in commercial development. The craft rules. Writing *for*: take the performer's demonstrated instrument — the pause, the speed, the flat delivery, the stillness — and give them scenes that need it, while writing the role so it still stands up if they fall out, because attachments collapse and a script welded to one body is unsellable. Writing *against*: the gap must be paid for with behaviour, not with an announcement, and the audience needs one early scene where the familiar register appears and is then withheld — that withholding is the effect. Never write the performer's known catchphrase or signature moment; it reads as fan service and it is the note a good actor gives back first.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Television Pilot, Short Film, Pitch Document | genres: Crime, Thriller, Drama, Comedy, Biography
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-09. A performer's public image is the material this technique works on, and it changes with their last two jobs; the method is durable, any specific read of any specific actor is not.
**Avoid when:** you have no attachment and are writing to an imagined star — the result is a role built out of a persona nobody has agreed to play, and it reads to a reader as a script with a hole in the middle. The failure mode is dialogue written in the actor's voice rather than the character's, which is `dial.writer_voice_uniformity` with a more famous author.
**Example:**
> Against the image, for a performer the audience knows as the calm one.
> Scene 3, the familiar register, fully delivered: a man talked down from a ledge in four lines, unhurried.
> Scene 4: his son asks him a direct question at the kitchen table, and he leaves the room. He leaves the room in every domestic scene for the next forty pages. The audience spends the film waiting for him to use on his own family the thing they have watched him use on a stranger.

**Yields to:** `char.agent_read` — No attachment; write a castable part, not a persona.  
`char.write_for_known_actor`

### How the Role Reads to an Agent

**Also called:** the offer-quality role, actor bait, the page count that gets a yes, what a talent agent is scanning for
**What it is:** understanding how a character is assessed by the people who decide whether an actor sees the script — scene count, the presence of a demonstrable range, whether the character drives, and whether the part is distinguishable from the others on the page.
**Effect on the audience:** none directly, and the whole point: this is craft aimed at getting the piece made with the performer who will make it good.
**Used for and where it works best:** any script going out to talent, and any supporting role you are struggling to cast. The craft rules, in the order they are checked. Count the scenes and the pages between first and last appearance, because a part with eleven scenes spread over ninety pages is a schedule problem and will be declined for reasons nobody mentions. Give the role at least one scene where the character *changes tactics* on camera, since that is what an actor reads for and a part with one note is a part with no offer. Make the character the engine of at least two scenes rather than the recipient of six (`char.only_reacts` is what a decline usually means). And write the casting line so it gives something to play rather than an age and an adjective (`char.casting_line`). A supporting role that is hard to cast is almost never underwritten in length; it is underwritten in *agency*.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Television Pilot, Limited Series, Short Film, Stage Play | genres: Drama, Crime, Family Drama, Comedy, Biography
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-09. Submission practice, self-tape norms and what counts as a castable part move with the market; the underlying test — does the character change tactics on camera — does not.
**Avoid when:** it starts driving the story — a script rewritten to inflate a part into an offer produces a character with scenes they do not need, and readers feel the padding before they feel the generosity. The failure mode is the big speech added as bait: actors read it as bait, and a monologue is not the same as a part.
**Example:**
> Before: nine scenes, all reaction, one long speech on p.71.
> After: same nine scenes. In three of them she now asks for something and is refused, and in the fourth she stops asking and takes it. The speech is cut. The part was offered in a week.

`char.agent_read`

## Development Tools

### The Character Interview

**Also called:** interviewing your character, the in-voice questionnaire, the hot-seat exercise, asking them yourself
**What it is:** a drafting exercise in which the writer asks a character questions and writes the answers *in the character's voice*, in real time, without editing — used to find speech, evasions and priorities rather than to collect facts.
**Effect on the audience:** none directly; the output is thrown away. What reaches the page is a voice the writer can now hear and a set of things the character will not discuss.
**Used for and where it works best:** the point in development where the character is well specified and still not speaking, which is the most common stall there is. The craft rules are what separate it from a worksheet. Ask questions the character has a *reason to dodge*, because the evasions are the whole yield — a character who answers everything openly is the writer answering. Ask about behaviour and objects, never about feelings: what is in the drawer, what did you do on the last day of that job, who did you not ring. Do it aloud if you can, at speed, and keep the first phrasing. Stop at twenty minutes. And harvest only three things: one line of speech you would not have written, one refusal, and one physical fact. Everything else is compost. The theatre version is the hot seat, where an actor answers in character under questioning, and it is faster than the written one.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Stage Play, Limited Series, Audio Drama | genres: Drama, Family Drama, Crime, Literary, Psychological
**Avoid when:** you do not yet know what the character wants, where the interview produces an articulate stranger and a false sense of progress. The failure mode is the interview that becomes the writing: pages of the character explaining themselves fluently, which is exactly the register they must never have on the page (`dial.on_the_nose`).
**Example:**
> Q: What's in the top drawer?
> A: Nothing.
> Q: What's in the top drawer?
> A: Bills. The ones that are fine. The other ones are in the car.
> Q: Why the car?
> A: Because she doesn't go in the car.
> — That third answer was not in the outline. The whole subplot is in it.

**Yields to:** `char.want_need_sheet` — where you do not yet know what the character wants. The interview produces an articulate stranger and a false sense of progress; find the want first.  
`char.character_interview`

### The Wound Questionnaire and Its Limits

**Also called:** the trauma worksheet, the backstory questionnaire, wound-and-lie development, the origin sheet
**What it is:** the standard development instrument that asks what happened to a character, what they concluded from it, and what they now believe — used deliberately, with an explicit stopping point.
**Effect on the audience:** at its best, behaviour that is consistent for reasons the audience never has to be told. At its worst, the most recognisable shape in contemporary commercial drama: everybody explicable, everybody damaged, everybody a case.
**Used for and where it works best:** one protagonist and one opponent, before the first draft, and genuinely useful for exactly one thing — converting a past event into a present-tense *rule of conduct* (`char.wound_as_rule`) and a *governing sentence* (`char.lie_in_one_line`). Three limits keep it honest, and they are the reason this card exists rather than a longer form of the questionnaire. First, it is diagnostic and single-cause by construction, so it produces people who are the sum of one bad thing, and real people are not; give at least one principal a defining trait with no wound behind it at all. Second, it invites the therapeutic register, and characters who can narrate their own aetiology stop behaving. Third, it does not generate plot — knowing the wound tells you nothing about what happens on Tuesday, which is why a filled-in questionnaire so often accompanies a stalled draft. Fill it in for two characters, harvest the rule and the sentence, and close it.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Limited Series, One-Hour Drama, Stage Play | genres: Psychological, Family Drama, Drama, Tragedy, Crime
**Avoid when:** comedy, procedural, farce and most ensemble work, where the characters are fixed by design and a wound is a foreign body — and in any piece where you would end up with a cast in which every member's behaviour has a documented origin, which reads as a hospital rather than a world. The failure mode is the retrofitted wound, invented in the third draft to explain a behaviour that was already working fine, which always reads as an apology.
**Example:**
> Filled in: father left in 1991; concluded that useful people are kept; believes that if she stops being needed she will be left.
> Harvested, and all that survives to the page: she will not let anyone else carry anything, ever — and on p.4 she carries two boxes badly rather than accept one hand, and nobody remarks on it.
> Discarded: the 1991 scene, which was written and cut, and which the film is better without.

**Yields to:** `comedy.comic_character_engine` — in comedy, procedural, farce and most ensemble work, where the characters are fixed by design and a wound is a foreign body.  
`char.wound_questionnaire`

### The Two-Column Want/Need Sheet

**Also called:** the want-need grid, the external and internal columns, the desire ledger, the two-goal sheet
**What it is:** a one-page working document with a row per principal and two columns — the thing they are pursuing in the plot, and the thing the story says they actually require — kept beside the outline and used to check that the two are in conflict.
**Effect on the audience:** it is the difference between a story that has a shape and one that has events. When want and need are aligned, there is nothing to choose at the end; when they are opposed, the climax writes itself.
**Used for and where it works best:** outlining and the structural pass on a second draft, and the fastest way to diagnose an ending that feels arbitrary. The craft rules. Both columns must be specific and physical — "respect" is not a want (`char.desire_made_physical`), "self-acceptance" is not a need anyone can film; write the need as the *thing they would have to give up the want to get*. Fill it for the antagonist too, because an opponent whose want overlaps the hero's is the whole engine (`arc.shared_want_opponent`). Then add a third, unnamed column in your head: what they choose. If any principal can have both columns at the end, the sheet has told you the ending is unearned. `arc.the_want` and `arc.the_need` define the two terms; this card is the page you keep open while outlining.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Pitch Document | genres: Drama, Family Drama, Crime, Social Drama, Tragedy
**Avoid when:** flat-arc and returnable-series characters, where the need is not supposed to be met and the grid implies a transformation the format forbids (`arc.flat`, `char.long_series_character`) — there the useful sheet has one column and a note on what the format will never grant. The failure mode is the sheet as a promise: a need written in the grid, never dramatised, and then announced in the last ten pages as though the paperwork counted as the scene.
**Example:**
> | | WANT | NEED |
> | Marwa | the shop's lease renewed | to stop being the only one who is asked |
> | Sargon | the lease signed over to him | to be asked for something, once |
> They cannot both have their column. That is the last scene, and it took four minutes to find.

**Yields to:** `arc.flat` and `char.long_series_character` — for flat-arc and returnable-series characters, where the need is not supposed to be met and a two-column grid implies a transformation the format forbids.  
`char.want_need_sheet`

### The Audition Scene

**Also called:** the test scene, the throwaway scene, the character trial, the scene written to be discarded
**What it is:** a scene written purely to test a character — placed nowhere in the story, never intended for the draft — in which the character is put into a situation the plot will never give them, to find out whether they exist yet.
**Effect on the audience:** none; it is never shown. What it produces is the writer's certainty about how this person behaves when nothing is prepared.
**Used for and where it works best:** before committing to a character in a long project, when casting between two versions of the same role, and as the fastest fix for a character who is fine in their own scenes and vanishes in everybody else's. The craft rules. Choose a situation *outside the story's world and register* — the crime lord at a parents' evening, the grieving widow returning a faulty kettle, the soldier at a wedding buffet — because the story's own situations have answers you have already decided. Write it fast, do not plan it, and give the character something small they want in it. Then read only for two things: whether you knew what they would do, and whether the lines could belong to anyone else in the cast. It doubles as a genuine audition piece, which is why the name is not a metaphor: a two-page scene of this kind tells you more about a performer than any scene from the actual script.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Novel, Stage Play, Television Pilot | genres: Drama, Comedy, Crime, Family Drama, Workplace
**Avoid when:** you are behind on a deadline and reaching for it as a warm-up — it is a diagnostic, not a practice, and writing six of them is a well-known way of not writing act two. The failure mode is keeping it: the scene is usually charming, has nothing to do with the story, and survives four drafts before somebody with no affection for it asks what it is doing there.
**Example:**
> The test: the enforcer has to return a kettle without a receipt.
> "It doesn't boil."
> "Without the receipt I can't."
> "All right." He puts the kettle on the counter, and waits, and does not move, and does not speak, and lets the queue build behind him.
> Nothing in the film requires this. Everything about the man is in it.

`char.audition_scene`

### The Dossier Instead of a Draft

**Also called:** development as avoidance, the hundred-question worksheet, the character bible for a short story, backstory paralysis
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** an accumulation of character documentation — questionnaires, family trees, playlists, star signs, wardrobe notes, the character's favourite breakfast — produced in place of scenes, and mistaken for progress because it is legitimately enjoyable and looks like work.
**Effect on the audience:** they meet the symptom, not the cause: a first chapter dense with unearned specificity, characters introduced with facts nobody in the scene needs, and a story that takes forty pages to start because the writer is spending the material they collected.
**Used for and where it works best:** as a diagnosis, and the test is a ratio. Count the pages of development against the pages of draft; past about one to ten, the documentation has become the project. Second test: for each fact in the dossier, name the scene it changes. A fact that changes no scene is not knowledge about a character, it is knowledge about a document. The repairs are narrow and work: the character interview run for twenty minutes and harvested for three things (`char.character_interview`), the want/need sheet as one page (`char.want_need_sheet`), and the audition scene, which is the only development exercise that produces actual writing (`char.audition_scene`). Everything else can be found in the draft, because a draft is a development tool and the fastest one there is.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (most damaging in Novel and Serialised Fiction, where the runway is long enough to hide a year of it)
**Avoid when:** — the genuine exception is a multi-writer production, where documentation is infrastructure rather than avoidance and the bible has to exist before anybody drafts (`char.bible_entry`); the distinguishing feature is that somebody other than the author will read it. The failure mode proper is the solo writer with a complete world bible, four character dossiers, a map, and eleven pages of chapter one.
**Example:**
> In the dossier: born 1974, middle of three, allergic to shellfish, drives a Hilux, mother's maiden name, favourite surah, the year the shop opened.
> On the page, in the draft, the only one of those that ever mattered: "The shop's older than you."
> "It's older than you as well."
> "It's not, actually. Eleven months."

**Yields to:** `char.bible_entry` — in a multi-writer production, where documentation is infrastructure rather than avoidance. The distinguishing feature is that somebody other than the author will read it.

## Coverage

Two areas were written deliberately long because the database was weakest there and this file closes them. **Bodies and constraint** takes eight cards: a master method (`char.condition_as_constraint`), five specific conditions written as constraint sets, and the two failure modes the brief named — the pity or inspiration lever, and the condition that costs nothing. **Names in translation and heritage naming** takes five: transliteration, the honorific system, kinship terms for non-kin, the changed name, and the decorative foreign name as a failure.

Anything not written because it lives elsewhere, with the id cited instead:

- **Birth order and sibling position** — `rel.sibling_order`. `char.family_role` writes the *assigned role* instead, which is a different instrument.
- **Jealousy and envy** — `rel.mimetic_jealousy` and `emo.envy`. Both are relationship and emotion cards; the character-side desire work is `char.desire_second_want`.
- **The choral narrator and the collective voice** — `voice.collective_we` and `pov.collective_we`. Written twice already, and both from the narration side, which is where it belongs.
- **The voice of a place as narration** — `voice.voice_of_a_place`. `char.personified_place` builds the place as an agent; it deliberately does not give it a voice.
- **Non-human viewpoint** — `pov.nonhuman_pov` and `pov.ai_narration`. `char.animal_character` and `char.machine_character` build the character; those cards carry the viewpoint.
- **Signature vocabulary and the verbal tic** — `dial.signature_vocabulary`, `dial.conditional_tic`. `char.idiolect_sheet` specifies countable parameters instead, which is the layer above them.
- **Second-language speech and code-switching** — `voice.second_language_voice`, `voice.code_switching`, `dial.second_language_rhythm`, `dial.code_switch_midline`.
- **Changing what you call someone, as subtext** — `sub.address_form`. `char.honorific_system` fixes the system; that card plays the switch.
- **Erotic subtext in a scene** — `sub.erotic_code`. `char.seduction_competence` is the character's ability; that card is the scene mechanics.
- **Comic character construction** — `comedy.comic_flaw`, `comedy.straight_man`, `comedy.quirk_as_character`, `comedy.comic_character_engine`. Named in Part 2's outstanding list and already fully held by `comedy`.
- **Arc continuity across a writers' room** — `arc.arc_under_many_hands`. `char.behavioural_continuity` covers behaviour, which drifts differently and faster.
- **Retiring or killing a character** — `end.retiring_a_character`, `char.exists_to_be_lost`, `char.the_missed_one`.
- **The character as a pressure system under tension** — `tens.unreliable_body`, `tens.real_condition_dread`.
