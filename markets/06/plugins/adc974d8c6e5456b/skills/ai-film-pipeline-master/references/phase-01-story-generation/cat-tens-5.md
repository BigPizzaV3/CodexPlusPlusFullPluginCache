### The Pursuit That Pauses

**Also called:** the stalled chase, the chase that becomes a hiding scene, the siege pause, hunter in the room, the suspended pursuit, the chase that stops and starts again
**What it is:** A pursuit that stops mid-sequence without resolving — the hunted goes to ground in a space the hunter is now standing in, and the sequence runs on stillness until something restarts it as a pursuit again.
**Effect on the audience:** It is the hardest point in a chase to look away from, because motion has ended and none of the pressure has. A running audience is watching an outcome; a stopped audience is doing arithmetic about a room, and they cannot stop doing it. The freeze is also the one thing the audience's own body knows: attentive immobility is a real defensive state that sharpens perception rather than shutting it down, which is why a well-written pause feels more awake than the run before it.
**Used for and where it works best:** The middle of any pursuit long enough to have a middle, and the only reliable way to raise a chase's ceiling without adding pursuers. This is a **junction card**: it owns the two transitions, motion into stillness and stillness back into motion, and nothing else. Draw the boundaries hard. `tens.closing_pursuit` is a chase where both parties keep moving and a measured gap is the instrument; `tens.chase_turn` is a chase whose *question* changes while the legs keep going; `tens.hiding_beat` is a scene that is already a concealment when it opens and is still one when it closes, and `tens.sound_you_must_not_make` is the pressure inside that concealment. Those four are the states. This card is the doors between them. Five rules. **The stop must be the hunted's decision, not the hunter's failure** — the moment the hunter loses them, you have written an escape and the sequence is over; the hunted stops because running has become the worse of two options, and the audience must be able to say why in one sentence. **The hunter must be competent and patient, and must prove it inside the pause**: a pause only holds if stopping is a strategy rather than a mistake, so give him one visible piece of correct reasoning the hunted can hear but not see — the radio turned off, the back of a hand against the kettle, the tap tested for a drip. A hunter who stops because he is lazy or thick permanently downgrades the threat, and per `tens.near_miss_economy` you never get it back. **In the pause, the writer's instrument changes from speed to inventory.** Chase prose is verbs, elision and short lines; pause prose is nouns. Three registers, and you need all three: what is physically in the room and its distances, what the hunted can hear and how far away it is, and what their body is doing that they are not choosing — breath that has become a thing happening to them, a knee that has begun to shake, a swallow they can hear from the inside. **Budget the pause in facts, not seconds.** It dies the instant the audience stops learning, not when the clock runs out, so plan roughly one new fact per twenty seconds of screen time or per half page — about the room, the hunter, or the body — and break it when you run out. In practice that puts the ceiling around ninety seconds on screen and three or four pages in prose, longer only if you hand the pause a second character, which converts it into `tens.hiding_beat` and changes what you are writing. **And the chase must resume at a different level than it stopped at** — faster because the hunter now knows, or slower into the stalk of `seq.stalk_sequence`. Resuming at the same speed tells the audience the pause was an intermission. On breaking it, there are exactly three doors and they are not equal. **The hunted acting is the strongest** — it is a choice, it returns agency at the point the character has least of it, and it re-enters the pursuit on their terms. **The hunter deciding is second**, and the honest choice when the hunted genuinely has nothing left, provided the audience can reconstruct the decision afterwards from something they were shown. **A third thing arriving is the weakest, and it is weak for a structural reason, not a taste one**: it takes the transition out of both characters' hands, so the sequence's hinge is paid for by neither of them, and whenever the arrival happens to favour the hunted it is `tens.unearned_reprieve` in a costume. It has one legitimate use — when the third thing makes the situation worse for both parties at once. Relatives: `tens.waiting_as_weapon` for empty time in a loaded room without a pursuit around it, `tens.shrinking_options` for what the pause should cost, `tens.crowd_as_cover` for the version where the stop happens in public and the hunted has to keep performing.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Game Narrative, Short Film, Stage Play | genres: Thriller, Horror, Crime, Survival, War, Noir
**Avoid when:** The pursuit is your opening. A cold-open chase is a promise of velocity and an audience who does not yet know either party cannot value a stillness between them — keep it running and pause the second one. Avoid in vertical and short form, where a stop is a swipe and the whole device costs more seconds than the form has (`tens.micro_form_tension`). Avoid if you have already spent a full concealment scene in the same act, because the audience will read the pause as that scene returning and start looking for the wardrobe. And the honest medium limit: **in audio drama a pursuit that stops is indistinguishable from a scene that ended.** The listener hears running, then a room, and concludes you cut. If you want this in sound, one element must run continuously across the transition — the same rain, the same traffic, the breath that was in the run still in the room — or the listener loses the geography and spends the pause doing admin instead of being afraid (`tens.room_tone_silence`).
**Example:**
> **Failing:** He sprinted into the house and dived behind the sofa, holding his breath. The man came in, looked around, and went out again. Then the chase was on once more.
> **Working:**
> Two gardens, the fence, the gap by the bins — and the back door of number nine is open, because it is Tuesday and on Tuesday she airs the house. He goes in and stops, because a man running down a street is a man a car can follow and a man in a kitchen is nobody.
> Under the worktop, in the space where the bin should be.
> Inventory, then. A tea towel on the rail, damp. Four inches of floor between his knee and the doorway. Her radio, left on, some phone-in about parking. His own breathing, which has stopped being something he is doing and become something being done to him, and which he can hear over the radio, which means it is loud, which means —
> The man comes in and does not hurry. He turns the radio off. He puts the back of his hand flat against the kettle.
> Warm.
> He says nothing at all. He crosses to the hall door and opens it and looks up the stairs, and for the whole of that his shoe is four inches from a knee.
> Then he goes up. Eleven stairs. The fourth one talks.
> On the fourth stair, he stops.
> And the man under the worktop decides not to wait to learn what the stopping means. He is up and out through the open door into Tuesday and running again, and on the way past he takes the damp tea towel off the rail with his shoulder.
> It is on the kitchen floor. It is the only thing in that house that is out of place. The man on the stairs comes back down and looks at it for quite a long time, and then he does not hurry either.

**Yields to:** `tens.micro_form_tension` — in vertical and short form, where a stop is a swipe and the device costs more seconds than the form has.

### The Dream Logic Discount

**Also called:** the unreality tax, the negotiable-reality problem, the cost of "none of this is happening", dream-stakes collapse
**What it is:** The price paid in tension the moment an audience learns that what they are watching may not be real. Suspense requires the audience to believe consequences are possible; dream logic removes that guarantee retroactively — and the discount, once applied, does not expire.
**Effect on the audience:** They keep watching and stop paying. Attention survives; investment does not. From that point every threat is met with an appraisal instead of a fear — *is this one real?* — and the appraisal is faster than the fear, so it always wins. This is the same arithmetic as forty years of reversible comic-book death: the audience's first response to a killing stops being grief and becomes a calculation about whether it will hold.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use unreality when the character's interior is the subject and the plot is the vehicle, not the reverse — guilt, denial, grief, addiction, dissociation, the thing they will not look at awake. The governing rule: **pay for the licence up front, once, and never twice.** One declared breach of reality is a genre; two is a policy, and a policy means nothing on screen can cost anything. There are exactly three honest ways to keep tension inside unreality. **One: make the dream's consequences real in the waking world.** Wounds that persist, time that is gone, a person who does not come back. This is by a distance the strongest, because it is the only one that restores the thing suspense actually runs on — real consequence — rather than managing the loss of it. *A Nightmare on Elm Street* is built on nothing else: you have to sleep, and what happens there bleeds here. **Two: make the dream obey rules the audience can learn.** Fear needs prediction; a world where anything can happen is a world where nothing in particular is about to. Demonstrate the rule on a minor character early (`tens.rule_demonstration`), then the audience can do the frightening arithmetic themselves (`tens.threat_with_rules`). **Three: announce the unreality before it happens, not after.** This is hygiene rather than an engine — it prevents the theft, it does not generate stakes — but it is cheap and it works: the audience who was told the rules can be surprised, while the audience who was tricked can only be annoyed. If you can afford only one, take the first. **Where this card stops:** `time.dream_sequence` builds the sequence — grammar, length, what may be in it; this card prices what having one does to everything around it. `tens.false_scare_cost` is a single jolt sold as a threat and charged one unit of trust at a time; this is the whole currency being devalued at once, and unlike the fake scare it cannot be paid down by delivering later. `tens.threat_never_acts` is a real danger that fails to spend itself; here the danger spends itself freely and the spending does not register, which is the harder problem, because more violence makes it worse. `tens.unearned_reprieve` steals one escape the story did not pay for; this steals the possibility of ever paying. And `inter.fail_state_as_story` is the constructive inverse worth studying: it makes losing carry content, where this makes even dying carry none.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Limited Series, Game Narrative, Animation Feature, Short Story | genres: Horror, Psychological, Surreal, Science Fiction, Fantasy, Arthouse
**Avoid when:** The work runs on plot suspense — thriller, procedural, survival, heist. And note precisely what *it was all a dream* destroys: not the scene it ends, but every scene that came before it. The ending is merely bad; the retroactive clause is the damage, because it converts hours of supplied tension into a transaction the audience is told they should not have made. They apply the lesson forward, to your next work as well as your next act, which is why this is the one failure a strong final image cannot repair. The interactive version is the same bill in a different currency: a level revealed afterwards to have been a simulation tells the player their choices were not choices, and no amount of later branching buys that back (see `inter.fail_state_as_story` for failure that does carry weight, and `inter.permadeath_writing` for the opposite contract). **Sustained unreality is a different case and deserves an honest answer.** Surreal and dream-logic work — where nothing is stable and no waking frame is coming — does not build suspense, and writers who claim otherwise are describing a different feeling. It trades suspense for dread. Suspense needs *if this, then that*; dread needs only that something is wrong and that the character's condition is true. So the anchor in that mode is never physical: hold the emotional stakes literal while the physical logic dissolves. The shame is real, the person who is gone is gone, the job was lost, the debt is owed. A Lynchian room curdles precisely because the humiliation in it is not dreamed. Lose that and you have `tens.dread_no_consequence`.
**Example:**
> Reality not yet negotiable: "The lock is on the inside." She tries the handle anyway. It does not move, and she has known for two minutes now that the next thing she hears will be the stairs.
>
> Reality already negotiable — same beat, one page after a dream was revealed as a dream: "The lock is on the inside." She tries the handle anyway. It does not move, and the stairs begin, and the reader, who has been taught, waits politely to find out whether this one counts.

**Yields to:** `inter.fail_state_as_story` — in interactive work, where failure has to carry weight rather than be revoked. A level revealed afterwards to have been a simulation tells the player their choices were not choices.  
`tens.dream_logic_cost`

### Tension Against a Known Outcome

**Also called:** the changed question, documentary suspense without suspense, restored contingency, the last ordinary morning, non-fiction tension
**What it is:** In documentary and history the ending is not yours to withhold — the audience arrives holding it — so you stop asking *what happens* and build the piece on a question the record leaves genuinely open: how, at what cost, who decided, and how do we know any of this.
**Effect on the audience:** They stop waiting and start leaning in. Attention shifts from outcome to mechanism, which is a slower and more durable grip than suspense, and it survives the moment the outcome arrives instead of dying there.

**Used for and where it works best:** Heritage and historical film, biography, archaeology and archive work, true crime with a public verdict, war and disaster history — anything whose last scene is already in the audience's schoolbook. This is the working answer to the format's central problem, and it has four instruments, in rough order of leverage.

**One: the knowledge gap.** The people in the material did not know. That asymmetry is the only free tension non-fiction gets, and it costs nothing to use — it is `info.dramatic_irony` with the record supplying the irony for you. Write the scene from inside their knowledge, not yours. The craft rule: **never let your narration know more than the person on screen while you are inside their moment.** The instant the voice says "little did they know", the gap collapses into commentary and the scene is over.

**Two: the restored decision.** Find a choice in the record that could plausibly have gone the other way, and put the camera on it before it was made. It must be a real option, and you must be able to show that it was real — the strongest proof is that somebody else in the same situation took it, which is evidence rather than speculation. Restored contingency is what makes a fixed outcome feel authored by people instead of by fate, and it is the line between this card and `anta.fate`. Do not fabricate the road not taken; if the record does not support an alternative, say the option was not there and find a different moment.

**Three: the detail that was not in the textbook.** The audience knows the headline and nothing under it, so everything under it is new. This is `expo.heritage_specificity` doing tension work rather than authority work: the price, the weight, the month, the hand the tablet is written in. The rule is that the detail must be *load-bearing* — it has to change how the known event reads, not merely decorate it. Three of these per segment, no more, and `expo.proper_noun_budget` governs the names inside them.

**Four: the process question — how do we know this at all.** Turn the evidence into a second line of suspense running under the first: who found this, what survived, what is missing and why. Documentary's own pleasure is the acquisition of knowledge, and a film that shows the acquisition has a live question even when the history does not. It also enforces the format's honesty, because the three registers — known, inferred, disputed — become the plot instead of a disclaimer. The silence in a record is the most usable form of this: the point where the sources stop is a real cliffhanger about the evidence, and nobody in the audience knows where it is.

**The structural move underneath all four: withhold the known outcome's shape, not its fact.** Everyone knows the empire fell. Nobody knows what the last ordinary morning looked like, what was in the storeroom, who was still doing their job, what the fall sounded like from four streets away. Announce the fact early and without ceremony — pretending to guard it is the single most common cheat in the genre — then spend the film on the shape, which you genuinely hold and they genuinely do not. Pair with `seq.documentary_sequence` for how the segment carries it, `pov.retrospective_distance` for the voice that admits it is speaking from now, and `end.mirror_ending` for the return to the present.

**The honest limit, stated plainly: this does not produce suspense. It produces dread and investment.** Suspense needs a live uncertainty about the outcome, and you do not have one. What you have is the emotional engine of `tens.known_outcome_dread` attached to non-fiction instruments, plus the forward pull of a question about process. A piece that pretends otherwise — music swelling toward a result the audience learned at fourteen, a narrator asking "would the city hold?" — reads as a cheat, and the audience that knows the most catches it first and stops trusting everything after it. The named failure is `tens.asserted_tension`.

**When the known outcome is a catastrophe the audience is already braced for** — a massacre, a siege, a deportation, a sinking — the braced audience is pre-defended. They have pre-felt the blow and will not feel it again on cue. Three moves. Take the weight off the catastrophe and put it before or after: the ordinary morning, or the aftermath (`tens.aftermath_not_act`), because a braced audience cannot be moved by the blow but can be moved by what it interrupted. Play the event itself flat, short and quiet — the harder they have braced, the more any rhetoric on top reads as manipulation and hands them permission to disengage. And keep the opposition unaimed (`anta.ambient_pressure`, `tens.tension_without_villain`); inventing a face for a catastrophe to make it dramatic is this format's worst temptation and will cost you the specialists first and the community second.

**Best in:** formats: Heritage Documentary, Documentary, Documentary Series, Essay Film, Long-Form Non-Fiction, Podcast | genres: Heritage, Historical, Documentary, Testimony, War, Tragedy

**Avoid when:** The piece is commemorative — an anniversary film, a memorial, a community history made with the descendants in the room. Restoring the decision to the moment before it was made turns, in that context, into second-guessing the dead, and "it could have gone otherwise" lands as an accusation against people who were there or whose grandchildren are watching. Also avoid it where the record is too thin to support contingency: with no alternative you can evidence, the restored decision becomes a dramatised guess wearing documentary clothes, which is the collapse of inference into fact this format is judged on. And do not run it under sixty seconds — the process question needs room to become a question at all.

**Example:**
> **Flat:** "In 612 BC the Medes and the Babylonians besieged Nineveh. After three months the city fell, and with it the Assyrian empire."
>
> **Held on the moment before the decision:** "Everything we know about that summer, we know from a Babylonian tablet. There is no Assyrian account of the fall of Nineveh. No letter, no complaint, no inventory. The city that wrote down everything for three hundred years, that kept a library because it assumed the future would want to read it, stops writing.
>
> So start where the writing stops. The chronicle gives us the month, and the month means heat, and it means the siege is young. Withdrawing west is a real option at this point — we can say that, because it is what the next Assyrian king does two years later, at Harran, and it keeps him alive for a while. Nobody in Nineveh takes it. We do not know why. Anyone who tells you why is guessing, and you should be able to hear it in their voice when they do.
>
> What we have instead is the last of the administrative tablets. Counts. Jars, measures, a good hand, the work done properly. Not a warning. Not a plan. Somebody was still counting."

**Yields to:** `emo.commemorative_register` — on an anniversary film, a memorial or a community history made with the descendants in the room. Restoring the decision to the moment before it was made lands there as an accusation against people who were present.  
`tens.known_outcome_documentary`

### Jeopardy a Child Can Hold

**Also called:** containable jeopardy, the visible frame, the recovery beat, safe-scare pacing, the child-scale tension curve, writing peril for a viewer who has to sleep afterwards
**What it is:** The pressure curve for children's and family work: real danger built to full strength, then bounded by something the child can see the edge of — a stated time, a place the threat cannot leave, a rule it obeys, an ally within reach — and discharged afterwards in a recovery beat that the plot does not need.
**Effect on the audience:** Thrill instead of distress, and the difference is arousal the child can put down. A child at full alert with no visible frame does not experience suspense, they experience helplessness — and they take it to bed, attached to an object in their own room. With the frame visible they lean forward, shout at the screen, and ask to watch it again.
**Used for and where it works best:** All children's narrative, and the single thing most often got wrong in it. **Softening the threat is not the mechanism and does not work** — a child reads a weak danger in about four seconds, stops paying in, and talks over the scene. Build the danger at full strength and bound it instead. Four instruments, in the order you specify them. **Resolve inside the unit** — the peril opens and closes inside the episode, the chapter, the song. **Give the threat a want, not an appetite for destruction** — a thing that wants the ball, the cake, to be left alone, has a stopping condition the child can picture and act against, which is why `tens.adaptive_threat` is forbidden here and `tens.negotiable_threat` and `tens.threat_with_rules` are the native forms. **Put a competent ally inside the frame before the peril starts** — visible, named, reachable, and structurally forbidden to solve it, because an ally who wins the scene is `tens.unearned_reprieve` and the child stops paying in for good; the ally exists to make the ceiling visible, not the outcome. **Then write the recovery beat.** This is the part adult readers cut as redundant and it is the entire reason the child sleeps. It runs after the plot has finished, contains one physical action and not only a line, and its length is the only checkable number in this card: **roughly a tenth of the peril's running time, and never under about ten seconds on screen or a full paragraph on the page.** This inverts `tens.trough_placement` and `tens.release_valve`, whose adult rule is that the release must be shorter than the tension and cut early; in children's work the release runs to completion, because completion is the deliverable. Relatives: `anta.bounded_threat` specifies the *opponent* and its three bounds — this card is the pressure curve and the discharge, and the two are written together; `emo.kids_emotion` supplies the naming vocabulary the recovery beat borrows one line of; `char.species_logic` decides upstream whether the world can have jeopardy at all; `anta.preschool_problem` is the opponent for forms too short to run this curve; `end.child_carry_ending` is where the beat lands.

The bands differ in checkable ways, not in degree. **Preschool (2–5):** they are frightened by what *looks* frightening and cannot reliably sort fantasy from real, so the resolution must be shown rather than explained — the shadow is revealed to be a coat, in frame. They also cannot hold anticipatory fear productively, so no clock and no dread in advance: `tens.countdown` is a five-to-eight instrument, and here the scare and its undoing sit in one continuous run of shots, inside ninety seconds. An ally is physically present in frame. No threat to a caregiver. Recovery is behavioural and social — contact, a returning-to-normal shot, the ordinary sound coming back. **Five to eight:** the upgrade is anticipation — this is the first band that can be frightened by what is *about to* happen, so a short visible clock, `tens.bomb_under_table` and `tens.hiding_beat` become available and are the reason kids' adventure gets good here. The threat may be genuinely real rather than a misunderstanding, the ally may be off-screen provided they are named and reachable, and the recovery beat can now carry a cognitive line — the explanation begins to work around eight, and until then it is decoration over the behavioural part. **Middle grade (8–12):** monsters stop working and real dangers start — injury, death, a parent's safety, losing your place. The bound moves off the threat and onto the arc: peril may run across chapters, the ending may cost something real, and a chapter-end cliffhanger is legitimate craft rather than a defect. What must still close inside the unit is the *emotional* one. The adults get limited or removed, because from eight the reader requires the child to solve it, and a rescuing adult reads as the book not trusting them.
**Best in:** formats: Animated Series, Preschool, Picture Book, Kids Story, Animation Feature, Middle Grade | genres: Kids, Preschool, Adventure, Family Drama, Fable, Fantasy
**Avoid when:** The piece is about a loss that does not resolve — a death, a separation, an illness at home. Bounding requires a frame that closes, and there is not one; applying the recovery beat to a permanent loss produces the story that says it is fine, which is the single false note children detect fastest and the reason they stop trusting a book. That material wants `emo.kids_emotion` and `end.child_carry_ending` instead: the feeling is named and carried out of the room, not closed. The two failure directions inside the technique are worth stating plainly. **Over-bounded** — the frame is so visible the child never pays in; the diagnostic is that you can name, at the moment the threat first appears, the specific thing that will undo it, which is `tens.diluted_stakes` and `tens.threat_never_acts` in a kids' register and reads as being talked down to from about six upward. **Left open** — `anta.unresolved_kids_threat` owns this one completely, including its invisible half, which is a threat resolved in plot and never acknowledged in feeling; read that card, do not re-derive it.
**Example:**
> **Unbounded — same peril, no frame:**
> The dog comes out from behind the cars. It does not bark. Ama runs, and it follows her the whole length of the yard, and it is still following when she gets the gate open and falls out onto the pavement. She lies there. The gate swings. End card.
>
> **Bounded — same dog, same fear:**
> Ama's ball goes over the scrapyard wall. Tomas is up on the wall and cannot get down. "Mr Okonjo locks up at six. Don't be in there at six."
> The dog comes out from behind the cars. It does not bark. It is looking at the ball.
> It gets as far as the third car and stops, because there is a chain. Ama can see the chain. So can we.
> She rolls the ball to the far end of the yard. The dog goes after it. She walks out. The bell goes for six behind her.
>
> **The recovery beat, in full — this is the part that gets cut:**
> Outside, Ama sits down on the kerb, hard, like her legs have finished.
> TOMAS: You're shaking.
> AMA: I know.
> They sit. A car goes past. Tomas takes his sleeve and wipes the scrapyard off her hands, one hand and then the other.
> TOMAS: He was on a chain the whole time.
> AMA: I know. I saw the chain. I was still scared.
> TOMAS: Yeah.
> He gives her the ball back. She holds it and does not get up.
> AMA: I'm going to sit here for a minute.
> TOMAS: Okay.
> They sit there for a minute. An actual minute. Then they walk home.

**Yields to:** `emo.kids_emotion` and `end.child_carry_ending` — where the piece is about a loss that does not resolve. Bounding needs a frame that closes; applied to a permanent loss it produces the story that says it is fine, which is the single false note children detect fastest.  
`tens.kids_bounded_threat`

### The Subtitle That Arrives Before the Line

**Also called:** subtitle pre-emption, the spoiler cue, reading ahead of the actor, the burned punchline, caption spoilers, the eye beats the ear
**What it is:** A subtitled or burned-caption piece delivers its text to the eye faster than the performer delivers it to the ear, so the reveal at the end of a spoken line is already known while the line is still being said.
**Effect on the audience:** They receive the turn twice — once silently, once late — and the second arrival is a formality. The laugh comes a beat early and dies alone, the reveal lands on an audience that has already adjusted, and the performance looks slow because the viewer has been waiting inside it. They rarely blame the subtitle; they blame the actor, the edit, or the writing.
**Used for and where it works best:** Assume pre-emption on anything that ships with subtitles, and design against it. **The numbers make it unavoidable.** Two lines is the ceiling, 42 characters a line on Netflix and 37 on the BBC; reading speed is capped at **20 characters per second for adults and 17 for children** in Netflix's current English guidance — the older 17/13 figures were raised, and a piece timed to them fails delivery QC rather than failing on screen, which is the expensive way to find out. Arabic has its own published limits, which are on `dubbing.arabic_subtitle_rate`, and the platform's own current page is the only safe source for any of these: they change, and this card names them only so you know what kind of number to go and look up. Minimum cue duration is 20 frames — five-sixths of a second — and a cue may hold up to seven seconds. The cue is timed to the *first frame of audio*, and when speech starts within half a second after a shot change the in-time is pulled back to the cut, so the text legitimately precedes the first syllable; general captioning tolerance allows anticipation up to about 1.5 seconds. Do the arithmetic on a normal line: a seven-word English line is roughly 38 characters, read at a 20 CPS ceiling in about 1.9 seconds, spoken at 150 words a minute in about 2.8 — add the lead-in and the last word of the cue sits in the eye a full second before it reaches the ear. On a full two-line cue the gap is larger, and Karamitroglou's six-second rule (74 characters, six seconds) describes a *comfortable* rate of about 12 CPS, not the speed at which an eye actually clears a short cue. **Four repairs, in order of cost.** Move the reveal to the end of the spoken line, so the subtitle physically cannot outrun it — a punch in the first three words is already gone. Split the cue, so the turn opens the second cue rather than closing the first; this is the cheapest fix and the one that survives translation. Carry the turn on something a subtitle cannot pre-empt — an action, an object entering frame, a face, a held silence. And write the original knowing it will be read, which is the discipline in `dial.subtitle_constraint`. **The case worth the most is the one where you write both channels:** an English voiceover with burned Arabic subtitles, same author. A translator inherits your word order and can only re-time; you can re-order the *information*, push the named fact later in the English so the Arabic cue has room, break the Arabic cue at your own breath rather than at a line-fill, and end both lines on the same noun so the two channels land together. See `rhythm.arabic_rtl_rhythm` for what right-to-left does to that line. **How this differs from `rhythm.subtitle_beat`:** that card is legibility — can they read it in the time it is up, and does the break fall at a grammatical joint. This card is tension — did they learn it too soon. Same 42-character box, opposite question, and a cue can pass the first test perfectly while failing this one completely.
