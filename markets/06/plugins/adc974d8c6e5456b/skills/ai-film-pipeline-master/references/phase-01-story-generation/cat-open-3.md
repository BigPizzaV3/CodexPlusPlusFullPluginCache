### The Nut Graf

**Also called:** the nut graph, the nutgraf, the billboard, the "so what" paragraph, the why-we-care graf, the context section, the promise graph, the setup beat
**What it is:** The paragraph that arrives after the opening scene and steps back from it — naming what the piece is about, why it matters and why now, in the writer's own plain voice rather than through a character.
**Effect on the audience:** Permission to keep going. The lede bought their attention with one person; the nut graf converts that attention into a reason, and a reader who has been given a reason will tolerate a much longer middle than one who is still being charmed.
**Used for and where it works best:** Long-form journalism, essays, heritage and explanatory writing, and every narrated video that opens on a scene. **Position:** paragraph three to five after an anecdotal lede — second or third in a news feature, later in a magazine piece. What changes if it comes later is not style but risk: past roughly the fifth paragraph the reader is running purely on the anecdote's own charm, so a late nut graf is a bet that the scene can carry the whole piece alone. Make that bet consciously; and the later it lands, the more it must read as the writer pausing to reflect rather than announcing, or it sounds like the article restarting. **The job, in one paragraph:** zoom from the one to the many — the lede is a person, the nut graf is the class that person belongs to — state the stake, attach the timeliness peg, and carry exactly one number or one dated fact. One. Two and it has become the body. **The craft rule that separates it from everything near it: it answers "so what" without answering "what happens" — it sells the piece without spending it.** A bad nut graf summarises the article, an itinerary in prose: *this story will examine, we will look at, as we shall see*. A good one names what is at risk, for whom, and states it as a claim the piece has not yet proved, so the reader stays to watch it get proved. Two tests: could you paste this paragraph into the email to your editor and have them understand the piece? And, delete it — does the piece still have a visible reason to exist? **The version that matters beyond print — the line after the hook:** a heritage documentary, an explainer or a long-form video has this exact problem at the same position, roughly ten to twenty-five seconds in, after the cold open and before the body, and the writer usually does not know that newspapers met it in the 1940s, when the anecdotal lede and its nut graf became the Wall Street Journal's signature form. Same rule, same failure: *in this video I'll cover three things* is the summarising nut graf in narration, and it is where the retention curve breaks. Say the stake instead. Boundary with its neighbours: `open.essay_first_move` owns the lede and notes that a turn exists — this card is the turn's paragraph; `open.stated_promise` names the deliverable and `open.explainer_promise` names the reader's deficit, both aloud and at the very top, while this sits after a scene and names neither; `open.first_second_hook` is the scroll-stop, not the reason. It is also the one paragraph where the instinct behind `expo.absorb_into_argument` is wrong — do not dramatise it, do not put it in someone's mouth; the direct address is the point. `theme.descriptive_not_prescriptive` is what keeps it a nut graf instead of an editorial, and `pov.reportage_stance` sets the voice it is spoken in.
**Best in:** formats: Reportage, Long-Form Non-Fiction, Essay, Documentary, Explainer, Podcast | genres: Journalism, Heritage, Advocacy, Educational, Historical, Science
**Avoid when:** The piece's entire value is the unfolding — a single-character profile, a pure narrative feature — where a full nut graf lands as a spoiler and flattens the voice for four hundred words. There the move is to shrink it to one sentence of promise and let it stay ambiguous, not to write it bigger. Never in fiction; `open.question_planted_first` is the fiction-side answer to the same need. And the real failure mode nobody names: a writer who cannot write the nut graf usually does not have one, and the reporting is the thing that is missing. The industry response is to write a vaguer paragraph, which produces the piece that sounds authoritative and says nothing. Go back out instead. Watch also for the nut graf that promises a different article from the one that follows — see `open.wrong_promise` — and for the one that has quietly become the conclusion, which is `theme.explained_theme` arriving 3,000 words early.
**Example:**
> **Lede:** *Abu Sarmad raised the shutter at half past five and counted the bottles that had not sold. Eleven — the same eleven as last week, and the week before. The electricity bill on the counter was for a month in which the shop had been dark for nine days.*
> **Nut graf:** *He is one of about four thousand shopkeepers on this side of the river billed twice for the same current: once by the ministry for power that did not arrive, and once by the neighbourhood generator that replaced it. Since the tariff order in June, both bills have been legal. Nobody in the chain has done anything he could be prosecuted for, and that is the difficulty — what is happening to this shop is not a crime, it is a policy working exactly as written.*
> **Weak nut graf, same piece:** *This story will examine the electricity crisis and its impact on small business owners. It will look at the tariff order, speak with generator operators and ministry officials, and consider possible solutions. As we will see, the situation is complex.*
> **The same beat, narrated, at 0:14:** *"Four thousand shops on this side of the river are paying two bills for one current. Both of them are legal. That is what we went to find out about."*

**Source:** reference — Dean Starkman, "Listening to Kilgore", Columbia Journalism Review, 2 March 2009, https://www.cjr.org/the_audit/a_few_words_from_barney_kilgor.php, checked 2026-09-24; supports that the anecdotal lede and its nut graf became closely identified with the Wall Street Journal after Barney Kilgore became managing editor in February 1941, while saying who first invented them is hard to determine.  
**Yields to:** `open.question_planted_first` — Fiction, where the stake cannot be stated in the writer's voice.
