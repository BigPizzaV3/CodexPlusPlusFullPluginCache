# Genres — form

Entry point into the database. Each kit ranks a short list of techniques by `id`; open the `cat-*.md` file named for that id's prefix (or `arcs.md`, `structures.md`, `situations.md`) for the full card.

A genre kit carries something a format kit does not: **required beats.** Genre in writing is a set of obligations, not a marketing label. The audience arriving at a comedy or a tragedy has bought a contract, and the beats below are its clauses. Miss one and the reader registers a void they cannot name. Hit all of them and nothing else, and you have written formula: the cure is Truby's — carry two or three other genres in supporting roles, and let one of them argue against the primary.

**An honest warning about this particular file.** The seven kits here are not plot genres. They are genres of **register and stance** — defined by the writer's attitude to the material rather than by what happens in it. A crime story owes you a body; a satire owes you nothing in particular except a target. So the beat lists below are looser than the ones in `genres-primal.md` or `genres-inquiry.md`, and they are loose in two different ways. Comedy, Satire, Tragedy, Melodrama and Horror-Comedy do have a real event sequence, inherited from the plot genres they ride on, and their beats are given in order. **Psychological and Arthouse** and **Surreal and Absurdist** genuinely do not: their obligations are real but the order is the writer's choice, and those two kits give an **unordered** list, marked as such. Faking a sequence for them would be a lie dressed as rigour, and you would write to it.

One more thing this file has to say plainly: these registers are almost never the whole of a piece. Satire is a stance applied to a plot genre, not a plot. Tragedy is a shape of ending. Arthouse is a set of permissions. Choose the plot genre from another file first, then come here for the stance.

## Comedy

**What the genre argues:** Comedy argues that the individual is smaller than he believes and the society is stupider than it believes, and that the collision of those two errors is survivable. It exists to ask what a person will do to be seen as something he is not, and to prove that the pretence always costs more than the truth would have. Its ending is social: the community reconstitutes itself and lets the fool back in.

**Required beats:**
1. The ordinary world established as a rigid system with one absurd rule that nobody in it questions.
2. The protagonist introduced through the comic gap — the distance between who he thinks he is and what he demonstrably is.
3. The character web assembled, each supporting character running one fixed mechanism that cannot adapt.
4. The straight man established: the person who reacts at normal size, so the audience can measure everyone else.
5. The inciting leapfrog — a small lie, mistake or ambition that requires a second one to hold it up.
6. The desire stated, and stated in a form ridiculous relative to the protagonist's actual means.
7. The plan arrives as a scam: a false identity, a cover story, a scheme with named steps.
8. The first run of the scam succeeds far too well, which is the trap.
9. A rule of the comic world demonstrated at someone's expense, so later violations register.
10. Opposition arrives from several directions at once — authority, rival, ally and the protagonist's own body.
11. An ally is recruited into the lie and must now maintain it without the skill to do so.
12. The topper sequence: one gag escalated three times, the third worse than the audience predicted.
13. A near-exposure, survived only by telling a worse lie.
14. A public set piece where the lie is tested in front of people who matter.
15. The person whose good opinion the protagonist actually wants learns part of the truth.
16. The drive turns: the danger becomes real and one beat is played with no joke in it.
17. The ultimate worst nightmare — every lie converging in one room, in public, simultaneously.
18. Exposure and humiliation, played at full size and not softened.
19. Self-revelation: the protagonist sees the gap, briefly and without a speech about it.
20. New equilibrium — a wedding, a meal, a party — with the fool seated inside the community.

**Supporting genres:** Love Story supplies the person whose opinion makes the humiliation cost something; without it beat 18 is just noise. Crime or Heist lends the scam its mechanics and its ticking clock, which is why so much farce is structurally a caper. Family Drama or Social Drama supplies the rigid system in beat 1, and is what separates comedy that means something from a sketch run long.

**Rules:** Build the character web before the first joke — comic types generate gags, gags do not generate types | Hang every joke on the plot's clothesline, so the story advances while the audience is laughing | Give the funny line's funny word the last position in the sentence | Escalate by consequence, not by volume | Let the audience reach one punchline a beat before the character does, then top it | Keep one straight man on stage so absurdity has a ruler beside it | Play the humiliation at full size — comedy that flinches at its own climax is a sitcom episode | Pay for the plan's early success with the late catastrophe it made possible

**Avoid:** The joke that stops the story, which is the genre's commonest and most seductive failure — a line so good it costs you a scene of momentum, and three of them in a row turn a film into a revue. The character who is only funny, with no want of his own, who dies on the page the moment he is asked to carry a scene. Explaining the joke, which insults the only faculty the audience brought with them. A protagonist whose lie has no cost, which makes the whole middle weightless because exposure threatens nothing. And the ending that forgives him without exposing him first — comedy's contract is humiliation then readmission, in that order, and reversing it produces sentiment.

## Satire

**What the genre argues:** Satire argues that a specific institution deserves to be despised, and it argues this by showing the institution working exactly as designed. It asks whether a person can rise inside a corrupt system without becoming its instrument, and it always answers no. Unlike comedy, satire does not readmit the fool to the community — it indicts the community for having a place for him.

**Required beats:**
1. The system shown functioning normally, competently, and to somebody's genuine benefit.
2. Its governing absurdity stated aloud by a character who believes it entirely and without irony.
3. The protagonist introduced as a person who wants to rise inside the system, not to reform it.
4. The entry: he is admitted, initiated, given a badge, a title, a desk, a code name.
5. The first moral compromise — small, plausible, and rewarded within the same scene.
6. A victim of the system appears in the background and is not commented on.
7. The rules of advancement demonstrated by example: who rose, who was destroyed, and precisely why.
8. The protagonist's first real success, achieved by the system's logic rather than by his own merit.
9. A true believer and a cynic are set against each other, and each is right about something.
10. The satirical set piece: a ceremony, a meeting, a broadcast where the absurdity is performed at full scale by people in perfect earnest.
11. Escalation — the compromise that was personal in beat 5 becomes structural.
12. An outsider names the absurdity plainly, and is disbelieved, patronised, or destroyed for it.
13. The protagonist is offered real power on condition of one irreversible act.
14. He takes it, and the audience recognises, uncomfortably, that they would have.
15. The consequence lands on someone who never consented to any of it.
16. The system absorbs the damage without embarrassment and continues.
17. New equilibrium: the protagonist elevated, hollowed, and unaware of the second half of that sentence.

**Supporting genres:** Comedy supplies the mechanism — without a working gag architecture satire is an essay with characters. Tragedy supplies beats 13 to 15 and is what stops satire curdling into smugness, because tragedy insists the protagonist is a person rather than an exhibit. Crime or Political gives the system its concrete machinery: money moving, files closing, someone being paid.

**Rules:** Name the target before you write a line — an unaimed satire is a tantrum | Show the system working, and working well, or you are attacking a strawman | Give the true believer the best speech in the piece | Make the protagonist complicit rather than observant | Keep the exaggeration at one remove from the real thing, never two | Let the audience reach the indictment; state it and you have written a pamphlet | Apply the theme to the protagonist and to the reader, never only to the enemy | Have the system survive — a satire whose institution collapses has consoled you | Choose Horatian or Juvenalian deliberately, and shift between them on purpose rather than by drift

**Avoid:** The rigged argument, which is satire's structural disease: an opposition made of idiots proves nothing, and the reader can feel the thumb on the scale from the second page. Theme applied only to others, so the piece flatters its audience for being on the right side — the most popular kind of satire and the most worthless. Exaggeration pushed past the point where it stays recognisable, which converts indictment into cartoon and lets the real institution off. The explained theme, delivered by a character who steps forward and says what the book means. And the ending where the system falls, which feels like victory and functions as anaesthetic.

## Tragedy

**What the genre argues:** Tragedy argues that a person's finest quality, applied without limit, will destroy him and take others with him. It is not about bad luck — that is melodrama — and it is not about bad people. It asks whether a human being can be great and wrong at the same time, answers yes, and then insists that the greatness is precisely what made the wrongness lethal. Its subject is authorship: the tragic hero is the one who did it.

**Required beats:**
1. The hero at his height, holding real responsibility rather than mere rank.
2. The admired virtue established in action — the quality that will kill him, shown as an asset.
3. The world's law stated, with a named penalty for breaking it.
4. The warning delivered by someone who has no power to enforce it.
5. The choice at the crossroads, made freely, for a defensible reason the audience can share.
6. The first success that confirms the choice and closes off retreat.
7. A loyal person is harmed by the choice and does not blame him for it.
8. A second chance to stop, refused for a better reason than the first refusal.
9. The hero obtains exactly the thing he wanted.
10. The fault compounds: the same error, applied now to a larger matter.
11. The mirror character — someone who faced the same crossroads and turned the other way — crosses his path.
12. The point of no return: an irreversible act against kin, oath, or his own stated principle.
13. Peripeteia. The action taken to secure the want produces its exact opposite.
14. Isolation — allies withdraw, and the last one is dismissed by the hero himself.
15. Anagnorisis: recognition, complete, in the hero's own words, and too late to act on.
16. The catastrophe falls, and it falls on people who did not choose it.
17. The hero accepts authorship — no excuse, no blaming of gods, circumstance or enemies.
18. A survivor speaks the cost, and a diminished order is restored.

**Supporting genres:** Love Story raises the price of beat 12 — the hero must destroy something he loves, not merely something he owns. Political or Crime supplies the public machinery so the fall has civic weight rather than only private grief. Family Drama supplies beat 7 and beat 16, because tragedy's real casualties are never the protagonist.

**Rules:** Give the hero a free choice at beat 5 — a hero who is trapped is a victim and you are writing melodrama | Make the fatal quality the same quality the audience admires in beat 2 | Let the warning come from someone powerless, so refusing it is plausible | Give him what he wanted at beat 9, and let it be genuinely good for a while | Put the recognition in his mouth, not the narrator's | Let the catastrophe injure the innocent | Let him accept authorship without a self-defence | Underplay the largest moment — tragedy's peak is spoken quietly or not at all

**Avoid:** Making the hero's flaw a vice rather than a virtue taken too far, which produces a cautionary tale and forfeits pity. Removing his freedom, so the machinery grinds him down and the audience feels sorry instead of implicated — Truby's line is the useful one: in melodrama fate strikes at random, in tragedy the hero perishes by his own rebellion. Explaining the recognition, at length, to a listener. Any late rescue, reprieve or intervening authority, which is the deus ex machina and it voids the whole contract. And an ending where only the hero pays: tragedy's moral claim depends on the innocent bystanders in beat 16.

   Beats 5, 8 and 12. The opponent in tragedy is the hero's earlier decision, arriving on schedule.
3. **The Load-Bearing Contradiction** (Character) `char.load_bearing_contradiction`
   Beat 2. The virtue and the fault must be one trait seen from two angles, not two traits.
4. **The Moral Line** (Arc) `arc.moral_line`
   Beat 12. Draw the line in act one so the audience knows the exact moment it is crossed.

   Why the hero is great and why that greatness is the mechanism of the disaster.

    The failure diagnostic, and the one every tragedy drafts its way toward at three in the morning.

## Melodrama

**What the genre argues:** Melodrama argues that virtue is real, that the world routinely fails to recognise it, and that the recognition — when it finally comes, publicly, from the person who owed it — is worth everything the innocent suffered to get there. Peter Brooks's term for what it is reaching for is the moral occult: a moral order that is no longer guaranteed by religion and so has to be dramatised into visibility. Where tragedy is about authorship, melodrama is about acknowledgement.

**Required beats:**
1. Virtue established inside a domestic space, demonstrated through work and care rather than stated.
2. The dependent named — the child, parent or sibling who will suffer if the innocent falls.
3. The villain, or a villainous circumstance, arrives holding legitimate power: a landlord, a law, an illness, a head of family.
4. An accusation or a misunderstanding attaches to the innocent, and the visible evidence supports it.
5. She cannot speak in her own defence, for a reason that is honourable — a promise, a shame, a person she is shielding.
6. Expulsion: she loses the home, the family, the position, the town.
7. A first refuge is found, and it is conditional on something.
8. The sacrifice — she surrenders the thing she most wants in order to protect the dependent.
9. The truth is documented somewhere and the audience is shown where: a letter, a ledger, a witness who exists.
10. Time passes. She builds a smaller life under a diminished name.
11. The villain prospers publicly and is widely admired.
12. Coincidence brings the parties into one room without recognition.
13. The dependent is threatened a second time, which is what finally forces her to break the silence.
14. Partial disclosure to one ally, who believes her, and whose belief costs him something.
15. The public scene: the accusation made in front of witnesses, and the document or witness produced.
16. Recognition — the virtue named aloud by the specific person whose acknowledgement was owed.
17. The villain's exposure, which is social rather than legal, and permanent.
18. Reunion, held one beat longer than comfort allows, and priced: something is not recovered.

**Supporting genres:** Family Drama supplies beat 1 and beat 2, and without it the innocent is a symbol rather than a person. Love Story supplies the ally of beat 14 and the thing surrendered in beat 8. Social Drama gives the villain his legitimate power — landlords, courts and asylums are better antagonists than villains, because the audience knows they exist.

**Rules:** Earn the situation before asking for the tear — the card on this is the whole genre's health | Make the silence in beat 5 honourable and specific, never mere passivity | Let the audience hold the proof the characters lack, from beat 9 onward | Route the emotion through a witness, not through the sufferer's own face | Keep the villain socially legitimate | Play the peak scenes short and the aftermaths long | Deny full restoration — reunion must be missing something | Cut every adjective in the recognition scene and see what survives

**Avoid:** Sentimentality, which in this genre means asking for a feeling the situation has not paid for — the manipulated beat, the swelling of language exactly where the writer has stopped trusting the material. Innocence with no agency, which makes the protagonist an object the plot happens to and drains eighteen beats of interest. A villain with no legitimate standing, who reduces a moral argument to a personal grudge. Coincidence used to solve rather than to complicate, which is the difference between beat 12 and a cheat. And the perfect reunion — melodrama that gives everything back has admitted the suffering was decorative.

   The gate card for this whole genre. Nothing else here works if this one is skipped.
2. **The Sympathy Beat** (Character) `char.sympathy_beat`
   Beat 1. Virtue is established by a small act of care witnessed by nobody, not by a declaration.
3. **Social Prohibition as Generator** (Subtext) `sub.social_prohibition`
   Beat 5. What she is forbidden to say is the engine; the card explains how to make silence active.
4. **33. Erroneous Judgment** (Situations) `sit.polti_33_erroneous_judgment`
   Beat 4 in its classical form. Polti's variants are worth reading before you choose your accusation.
5. **The Secret the Reader Holds** (Information) `info.audience_only_secret`
   Beat 9. The audience must hold the proof for ten beats while the characters do not.
6. **The Object That Carries It** (Emotion) `emo.object_carrier`
   The letter, the locket, the receipt. Melodrama's proof is physical and the object must be planted early.

    The second diagnostic, applied line by line to your three biggest scenes.

## Psychological and Arthouse

**What the genre argues:** This register argues that the interesting part of a life is not what happens but what a person does with what happens, and that most of that is not available to the person themselves. It refuses the promise that a life resolves. Its real subject is attention: it asks the audience to watch at a rate the plot genres never permit, and it earns that by making the watching itself productive.

**Obligations — unordered.** This genre has no fixed beat sequence, and pretending otherwise would be the first mistake. What it has is a set of things it owes, which can be discharged in any order and at any scale. Meet twelve of these and you have the genre; meet four and you have a slow film.

- A protagonist whose want is opaque to himself and legible to the audience only through behaviour.
- Ordinary routine given real screen time, so that the deviation from it can be small and still register.
- At least one scene that would be cut from any other genre, kept because it is the most truthful one.
- An event of conventional significance — death, birth, arrest — handled off-centre or offscreen.
- The interior rendered through task, object and gesture rather than through reported thought.
- A second character who has a complete life the protagonist does not have access to.
- At least one long, unbroken stretch of duration where the audience's attention is the subject.
- A contradiction in the protagonist that is never resolved or explained.
- An explanation the audience expects, deliberately withheld, with the withholding made felt.
- Something the protagonist does alone, seen by nobody in the story.
- A repetition of an earlier scene with one element changed and no comment made.
- A social world that constrains the protagonist without any character in it being an antagonist.
- The moment of change placed where the plot does not want it — early, or in the middle of something else.
- An ending that closes an image and leaves the question open.
- Silence used as content in at least one scene, long enough to be uncomfortable.
- A refusal of the consolation the material is offering.

**Supporting genres:** Family Drama is the commonest and healthiest partner, because it supplies concrete obligations for a character who has no plot. Crime or Thriller used as an under-genre — a case, a threat, a deadline running quietly beneath — is what stops arthouse dissolving; a great many of the best psychological films are a genre film with the genre turned down. Memoir lends the retrospective structure and the permission to organise by association.

**Rules:** Give the protagonist a concrete daily task and let the interiority ride on it | Establish the routine before you deviate from it | Withhold explanation, but make the audience feel the withholding | Choose scene duration deliberately, scene by scene, and write the duration into the page | Keep one external pressure running, however quiet | Let behaviour contradict statement and never adjudicate between them | Earn every long take with something that changes inside it | Close the image, not the argument | Apply the dead scene test to every scene you are keeping on instinct

**Avoid:** Confusion mistaken for depth, which is this register's endemic disease — a reader who cannot tell what is happening is not being made thoughtful, they are being made bored, and the two feel identical from inside the draft. Subtext nobody can find, which is the same failure at the line level: buried so deep it is functionally absent. Slowness with nothing inside it, where duration is imitated from films the writer admired without importing the thing that filled the duration. The arthouse ending used as an escape from having to decide what the piece means. And the total absence of external pressure, which removes the only force that was making the protagonist's behaviour informative.

   The register's main instrument. Know which rung you are on in every paragraph.
5. **Sliding the Distance Within a Scene** (POV) `pov.distance_slide`
   The same instrument, moving. This is what arthouse does instead of cutting.
6. **Scene Duration** (Time) `time.scene_duration`
   Duration is the genre's chief expressive choice. Make it a choice, on the page, per scene.
7. **Iterative Time** (Time) `time.iterative_habit`
   The routine obligation. Habitual action rendered so that one deviation carries an act's worth of weight.
8. **The Iceberg** (Subtext) `sub.iceberg_omission`
   Omission as method, with the card's conditions for the omission being felt rather than merely absent.
9. **Restricted Narration** (Information) `info.restricted_narration`
   The withheld-explanation obligation, handled as a deliberate contract rather than as vagueness.
10. **What They Notice** (Character) `char.what_they_notice`
    Selective perception is characterisation. In a film with no plot it is most of the characterisation.
11. **Sustained Low Dread** (Tension) `tens.sustained_low_dread`
    The quiet external pressure. This card is why the best slow films are not boring.
12. **The Climaxless Run** (Sequence) `seq.climaxless_run`
    A legitimate sequence shape here and a defect everywhere else. Read the conditions carefully.
13. **The Open Ending** (Endings) `end.open_ending`
    The card distinguishes an open ending from an unfinished one. That distinction is the genre's reputation.
14. **The Dead Scene Test** (Scene) `scene.dead_scene_test`
    Run it on every scene. The genre's permissions are not a licence to skip this.
15. **Confusion Mistaken for Depth** (Subtext) `sub.confusion_as_depth`
    The primary failure diagnostic. Read it before you defend an unclear passage.
16. **Subtext Nobody Can Find** (Subtext) `sub.buried_subtext`
    The second diagnostic, at line level. Hand the draft to one reader and ask them what happened.

## Surreal and Absurdist

**What the genre argues:** These are two neighbouring stances that share a shelf and should not be confused. **Surreal** argues that the unconscious has its own grammar and that dream logic reveals what waking logic conceals; it trusts the image. **Absurdist** argues that the human demand for meaning meets a universe that will not answer, and it stages that silence as routine, repetition and the collapse of communication; it distrusts language. Both refuse causality, and both live or die on the same discipline: strangeness with intention behind it.

**Obligations — unordered.** Neither stance has a beat sequence, and any list claiming one is describing a different genre wearing the costume. These are the obligations. The order is yours, and choosing it badly is one of the available failures.

- A stable rule of the impossible world, stated or demonstrated early, that the piece never breaks.
- Characters who treat the impossible as administrative, and the trivial as catastrophic.
- One anchor of complete ordinariness — a chair, a queue, a job, a sandwich — held throughout.
- A conversation in which both parties are coherent and neither is answering the other.
- Repetition that changes meaning through accumulation rather than through variation.
- An institution or a procedure applied to something it cannot possibly govern.
- A literal reading of a figurative expression, taken all the way to its consequences.
- Sensory precision: the impossible described with the specificity of a maintenance report.
- An emotional register held flat against escalating circumstances.
- A transformation with no explanation offered and none implied as forthcoming.
- A door, a threshold or a corridor that does not lead where it should, used more than once.
- The demand for meaning voiced by one character and left unanswered by everyone.
- A dread that is never named and never resolved.
- One image the piece is willing to be remembered by.
- A refusal of the closing explanation, including the sly one.

**Supporting genres:** Comedy is the usual and best partner — the absurd tradition is structurally comic even when nothing is funny. Horror supplies the dread, and the border between absurdist and cosmic horror is thin and worth crossing on purpose. Social Drama or Satire supplies the institution in the sixth obligation, and is what turns an image into an argument.

**Rules:** Fix the rule of the world and never break it — inconsistency is the only thing the reader will notice | Anchor every strange element to one ordinary one | Play the reaction flat and small | Repeat the same phrase or action until it means something else | Describe the impossible in concrete, technical nouns | Let the strangeness carry meaning rather than announcing it | Keep one emotional line running literally beneath the surreal one | Refuse the explanation at the end, including the clever one | Give the reader something to hold — a voice, a room, a smell — or they leave

**Avoid:** Randomness without intention, which is the whole genre's failure in one phrase — every strange element needs a reason even when it has no explanation, and a reader can tell the difference immediately. A character monologuing about meaninglessness, which is the writer stating the theme and calling it absurdism; the theme belongs in the stage business, not in a speech. Tonal drift, where the piece wanders out of its register because no rule was set. Explaining the joke or the image at the end, retroactively converting it into a puzzle with a solution. And the absence of any handhold — no consistent voice, no stable room, no recurring object — which produces a text nobody finishes.

**The kit (ranked):**

1. **The Rule of the World** (Openings) `open.rule_of_the_world`
   First obligation, first page. The rule is what separates this from randomness.
2. **Rule by Demonstration** (Exposition) `expo.rule_by_demonstration`
   Never explain the impossible world. Show it costing somebody something.
3. **Familiar Anchor, One Strange Word** (Exposition) `expo.familiar_anchor`
   The anchoring obligation, at sentence level. The card's ratio is the one to hold.
4. **The Literal Answer** (Comedy) `comedy.literal_answer`
   The absurdist tradition's core move: the figurative taken at its word and followed home.
5. **Refusing the Frame** (Dialogue) `dial.refusing_the_frame`
   Two coherent people, no shared premise. This is the Theatre of the Absurd's central dialogue technique.
6. **Adjacency Pair Break** (Dialogue) `dial.adjacency_break`
   The mechanical version of the same thing: question, then not-answer, systematically.
7. **Repetition and the Point Where It Turns** (Comedy) `comedy.repetition_turn`
   Accumulation obligation. The card names the exact place repetition stops being funny and starts being dread.
8. **Recurring Fragment** (Time) `time.recurring_fragment`
   The same image returning with no causal chain attaching it. Structure by rhyme instead of by cause.
9. **Corrupting the Mundane** (Tension) `tens.corrupting_the_mundane`
   The threshold, the corridor, the appliance. The domestic object is where this register gets its dread.
10. **Emotional Monotone** (Emotion) `emo.emotional_monotone`
    A defect in most genres and the correct register here. Read why before you use it.
11. **The Incantatory Register** (Voice) `voice.incantatory_register`
    Repetition at sentence level: liturgy, litany, the bureaucratic chant.
12. **The Non Sequitur** (Comedy) `comedy.non_sequitur`
    Use it sparingly and structurally. A page of these is noise; one placed right is the whole piece.
13. **Kishōtenketsu** (Structure) `struct.kishotenketsu`
    The non-conflict four-part structure. The genre's most useful macro-shape, because the turn needs no cause.
14. **Deferred Explanation** (Exposition) `expo.deferred_explanation`
    Deferred and then not delivered. The card's rules for keeping that satisfying rather than annoying.
15. **The Anti-Theme** (Theme) `theme.anti_theme`
    How a piece can argue without a thesis. Essential for the meaning obligation.
16. **Tonal Drift** (Voice) `voice.tonal_drift`
    The failure diagnostic. Surreal work drifts more easily than any other kind, because nothing corrects it.

## Horror-Comedy

**What the genre argues:** Horror-comedy is a real genre with its own obligations, not a horror film with jokes in it or a comedy with a monster. It argues that people facing annihilation remain absurd, petty and socially embarrassed right up to the moment they die, and that this is not a betrayal of the horror but the truest thing about it. Its structural claim is that laughter and fear are the same physiological release aimed at different objects, and that a story can alternate them faster than an audience can defend against either.

**Required beats:** This hybrid does have a sequence, because it rides the horror spine. The comedy sits in the register, not in the structure.

1. Ordinary life established as comically inadequate — the protagonist failing at something small and human.
2. The comic character web fixed, with its relationships and grudges, before any horror arrives.
3. The first wrong detail, played entirely as a joke, and missed by everyone including the audience.
4. A second, identical detail — now the audience sees it and the characters still do not.
5. The first real death, played straight, landing on someone the comedy had been using as a joke.
6. The tonal contract signed: one scene where the joke and the threat occupy the same shot and neither yields.
7. Denial — the group applies its ordinary social routines to an impossible situation.
8. The monster's rules demonstrated, in a way that is both funny and accurate.
9. The plan, improvised, overconfident, and assembled out of pop culture rather than sense.
10. The plan works once, which is the genre's biggest laugh and its worst mistake.
11. The competent outsider arrives and is either funnier or deader than anyone expected.
12. Escalation by proximity: the horror enters the domestic space the comedy lives in.
13. A comic character is killed on his punchline, mid-line.
14. The group fragments along exactly the comic relationships established in beat 2.
15. The protagonist has to kill or abandon someone he loves, played without a single joke in it.
16. The confrontation, in which the protagonist's useless comic skill turns out to be the one that works.
17. Survival — cheap, undignified, and owed more to luck than to mastery.
18. Coda: the world resumes with the horror domesticated into a joke, and one final image that is not funny.

**Supporting genres:** Horror is the parent, and its beat list in `genres-primal.md` should be read alongside this one — everything about rules, bounded space and the past sin still applies. Comedy supplies the character web and the escalation spiral. Buddy Love or Love Story supplies beat 15, which is the beat that decides whether the film is remembered; without a real relationship there is nothing to play straight.

**Rules:** Fix the comic character web before the first scare | Play the horror completely straight — the monster never knows it is in a comedy | Sign the tonal contract early, in one scene, so the audience knows the rules | Kill someone funny, early, and do not undercut it | Keep the monster's rules consistent and let the jokes come from the characters' misuse of them | Never let a joke rescue a character from a consequence | Reserve one sequence with no comedy at all, and place it late | End on an image that is not funny

**Avoid:** Tonal whiplash, which is the failure this genre is named for — the audience cannot tell whether to be afraid, so they choose neither, and both halves of your film are wasted. Jokes that defuse rather than accompany the threat, so no scare survives contact with the next line. A monster played for laughs, which forfeits the horror permanently the first time it happens. Comic characters who exist only to die funny, making the deaths free. And the ending that treats survival as triumph — this genre's endings are undignified, and the final image has to remember what it cost.

**The kit (ranked):**

1. **Comic Relief as a Tension Tool** (Tension) `tens.comic_relief_as_tension`
   The hybrid's founding card. Comedy as a tension instrument, not as a break from tension.
2. **Tonal Whiplash** (Voice) `voice.tonal_whiplash`
   The failure this genre lives one step away from. Read it second, not last.
3. **The Release Valve** (Tension) `tens.release_valve`
   Where the laugh goes, structurally — after the fear, never instead of it.
4. **The Threat That Obeys Rules** (Tension) `tens.threat_with_rules`
   Inherited straight from horror and non-negotiable. The comedy comes from misapplying the rules, so the rules must be firm.
5. **The Rule Demonstration** (Tension) `tens.rule_demonstration`
   Beat 8. The demonstration scene is this genre's best opportunity: funny and load-bearing at once.
6. **The Wrong-Sized Reaction** (Comedy) `comedy.wrong_reaction`
   Beats 3, 7 and 13. Under-reacting to horror is the genre's single most reliable joke.
7. **The Straight Man** (Comedy) `comedy.straight_man`
   Someone has to be correctly frightened, or the audience has no permission to be.
8. **The Wrongness Detail** (Tension) `tens.wrongness_detail`
   Beats 3 and 4. Plant it as a joke, repeat it as a threat. That double use is the hybrid in miniature.
9. **Anticipation Versus Anxiety** (Tension) `tens.anticipation_vs_anxiety`
   The card that explains why a laugh can raise tension rather than release it. Central here.
10. **Dark Comedy and Its Rules** (Comedy) `comedy.dark_comedy`
    Beat 13. Its conditions are what make killing a funny character land instead of curdling.
11. **Reversal of Expectation** (Scene) `scene.reversal_of_expectation`
    The genre's scene-level engine: set up a horror convention, deliver the mundane, then the reverse.
12. **The Safe Space Violation** (Tension) `tens.safe_space_violation`
    Beat 12. In this hybrid the safe space is also the comedy's home, so violating it costs twice.
13. **Emotion Against Type** (Emotion) `emo.emotion_against_type`
    Beat 15. The scene where the funny character is not funny, and the whole film is retroactively about it.
14. **The False Scare and Its Cost** (Tension) `tens.false_scare_cost`
    This genre runs on fake-outs and therefore accumulates this debt faster than any other. Keep the ledger.
15. **The Callback** (Endings) `end.callback`
    Beat 18. The last joke is a callback, and the last image is not a joke.
16. **Tonal Drift** (Voice) `voice.tonal_drift`
    The slow version of rank 2. Whiplash is a scene fault; drift is a draft fault. Check for both.

## Coverage

Seven form kits: Comedy, Satire, Tragedy, Melodrama, Psychological and Arthouse, Surreal and Absurdist, Horror-Comedy. Two of them — Psychological and Arthouse, Surreal and Absurdist — give unordered obligations rather than ordered beats, because they genuinely have no fixed sequence.

Horror-Comedy is the hybrid kit and is written to be read against Horror in `genres-primal.md`; it does not repeat horror's own beats. Satire is written to be read against Comedy above it. For plot genres to pair with any of these stances, see `genres-primal.md`, `genres-inquiry.md`, `genres-bond.md` and `genres-growth.md`.
