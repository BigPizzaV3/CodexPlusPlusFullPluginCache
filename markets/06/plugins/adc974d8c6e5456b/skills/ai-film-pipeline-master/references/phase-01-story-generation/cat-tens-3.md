## Pressure nobody is counting

### The Soft Deadline

**Also called:** the uncounted clock, the implied deadline, pressure with no number, the before-she-finds-out clock
**What it is:** Time pressure the audience feels precisely and cannot measure — before someone finds out, before she changes her mind, before the money runs out, before the child asks again. There is a terminus, and there is no figure on it.
**Effect on the audience:** Urgency without the thriller signature. Because the audience cannot count, they cannot reassure themselves with arithmetic, so the pressure is continuous and ambient rather than spiky — and it survives in genres where a visible clock would be ridiculous.
**Used for and where it works best:** This is what most drama actually runs on, and it is the honest answer to a sagging middle in a story that cannot carry a countdown. Three rules. **The terminus must be an event with an owner** — a person who will discover, arrive, decide or stop paying. "Before it's too late" has no owner and is not a deadline, it is a mood. **Show the gap closing once per act, through a third party**: a letter, a call not returned, a colleague who mentions the audit in passing. The audience must see it move without being handed a figure. **Never convert it into a number later** — the moment a character says "we have four days" you have `tens.countdown`, and the entire texture changes underneath the scene. Choose one and hold it.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Stage Play, Short Story | genres: Drama, Family Drama, Crime, Social Drama, Romance, Noir
**Avoid when:** The genre contract promises arithmetic. Heist, disaster and most action want a visible number, and a soft deadline there reads as a writer avoiding the work. Also avoid where there is genuinely no terminus at all: an indefinite wait is not a soft deadline, it is `tens.sustained_low_dread`, and it does a different job.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "We need to sort this out soon. Time's running out, and you know it."
> **Working:** "Your sister flies in on the fourteenth."
> "I know."
> "She'll go through that cupboard, Danny. She always does. It's the first thing she does."

**Yields to:** `tens.countdown` — where the genre contract promises arithmetic. Heist, disaster and most action want a visible number; and where there is no terminus at all this is `tens.sustained_low_dread` instead, which does a different job.  
`tens.soft_deadline`

### The Deadline Announced and Then Dropped

**Also called:** the abandoned clock, the forgotten deadline, the unenforced countdown
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A deadline stated out loud in the first act and then neither met, missed, extended nor mentioned again.
**Effect on the audience:** Worse than never having set one. The audience counted, because you told them to; when the count is not honoured they conclude — correctly — that the piece does not keep its own promises, and they stop tracking anything else it says. This is one of the commonest causes of a second half where "nothing seems to matter", which is almost always diagnosed as a pacing problem and is actually a broken contract.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere. Note the two things that look like it and are not: a deadline **visibly extended on screen** by somebody with the power to extend it is a plot beat, and a deadline the character **chooses to blow**, with consequences, is a climax. Both honour the count. Silence does not.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (most frequent in television, where a clock set in the pilot outlives the room that set it, and in features after a mid-process restructure moves the act break)
**Avoid when:** Always. The repair is mechanical and takes an hour: list every stated time figure in the draft, and write beside each one the page on which it is paid. Any figure with no page is deleted or paid. See `tens.countdown`, `tens.asserted_tension` and `end.unpaid_setup`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** p.12 — "The bank takes the house on the ninth." The house is not mentioned again for the remaining eighty pages.
> **Working:** p.12 — "The bank takes the house on the ninth." p.88 — it is the ninth, and he is three hundred miles away doing the other thing, having decided that on purpose, on page 81.

**Yields to:** `tens.countdown` — A stated clock must be visible and paid on the page.  
`tens.announced_deadline_dropped`

### The Person Who Will Ask Again

**Also called:** the human deadline, pressure with a face, the returning asker
**What it is:** The clock is a person. Somebody with standing will come back and ask the same question, and the character knows roughly when.
**Effect on the audience:** It converts abstract pressure into dread of one specific conversation, which is a far commoner lived experience than dread of a specific hour — so it lands hard on audiences who do not respond to jeopardy at all.
**Used for and where it works best:** Domestic drama, workplace, migration, debt, addiction, and any story whose antagonist should not be a villain. Four rules. **The asker must have a legitimate reason to ask** — a creditor, a doctor, a child, a spouse, an auditor; illegitimate pressure is a threat and belongs to `tens.polite_threat`. **Establish the rhythm before you use it**: show one ordinary ask so the audience learns the interval, because the second ask is the technique and it only works if they know it is the second. **Escalate the evasion, not the ask** — the ask stays the same size and the lies get worse, which is where the scene actually lives. And the strongest version by a distance: **the asker is being kind.**
**Best in:** formats: Novel, One-Hour Drama, Stage Play, Feature Film, Limited Series, Soap | genres: Family Drama, Social Drama, Workplace, Addiction, Migration, Drama
**Avoid when:** The asker has no power to act on the answer — then it is nagging rather than pressure, and the audience sides against them. Also never run two askers at once; an audience tracks one interval well and two not at all. See `anta.institutional` and `rel.the_debt`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "You have to tell her. You can't just keep putting it off for ever."
> **Working:** First Sunday: "Did you hear back from them?" — "Not yet." Second Sunday: "Did you hear back?" — "They said end of the month." Third Sunday she does not ask, and he spends the entire lunch waiting for it.

**Yields to:** `anta.institutional` — where the asker has no power to act on the answer. A person without leverage is nagging rather than pressure, and the audience sides against them; an institution needs no leverage of its own.  
`tens.human_deadline`

### The Rising Price of Waiting

**Also called:** the cost of delay, the meter running, escalating inaction, the expiring option
**What it is:** Tension built from a price that goes up while nothing happens — interest, infection, a story spreading, a body in a room, a child getting older. The character is not being chased. They are being charged.
**Effect on the audience:** It makes stillness expensive, which solves the hardest problem in the middle of a draft: the scene where the truthful dramatic move is for nobody to do anything. An audience will lean forward during inaction if, and only if, inaction has a visible price.
**Used for and where it works best:** Anywhere a countdown would be melodramatic — medical, debt, legal, family. Four rules. **Quote the price at least twice in the same unit**, so the audience does the subtraction themselves; two figures work where a warning does not. **Have somebody name the cheaper option that has already expired** — "if you'd come in last month this would have been tablets" — which is the line that does most of the work in this technique. **Give the character a real reason to wait**, or the scene is stupidity rather than pressure. **Never resolve it by having the price stop mattering**; a bill that evaporates retroactively cancels every scene it was in.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, One-Hour Drama, Feature Film, Limited Series, Stage Play, Documentary | genres: Medical, Crime, Social Drama, Drama, Addiction, Workplace
**Avoid when:** The piece is already running a countdown. A clock and a meter at once cancel each other, because an audience holds one piece of arithmetic and drops the second. Also avoid in comedy unless the rising price is itself the joke. See `anta.finite_resources` and `tens.costly_obstacle`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "Every day we wait makes this harder. You do understand that, don't you?"
> **Working:** "In March it was eleven thousand."
> "And now?"
> "Now it's nineteen. In September it's thirty-one. And in September they stop offering it."

**Yields to:** `tens.countdown` — wherever a clock is already running. An audience holds one piece of arithmetic and drops the second, so keep the clock and delete the meter.  
`tens.cost_of_waiting`

## Stakes you can see, and no room to build them

### The Consequence the Audience Can Picture

**Also called:** concrete stakes, the picturable outcome, the specific bad ending
**What it is:** Stakes that work because the audience is holding one specific image of the bad outcome — not because the piece has told them the stakes are high.
**Effect on the audience:** Fear that is portable. An audience carries an image into the next scene and into the next episode; they do not carry an assertion, which is why a story can state enormous stakes and produce nothing.
**Used for and where it works best:** Everywhere the note "raise the stakes" has been given, which is very nearly always a misdiagnosis — the stakes are usually already vast and simply unpicturable. Four rules. **Stage the bad outcome once, early, on somebody else.** The audience does not need to be told what happens to people who miss the payment; they need to have watched one. **Scale down, not up** — a city is unpicturable, the one room in it is not; losing a house is a concept, handing over a specific key is an image. **The consequence must belong to a named person** the audience has spent time with. **Test it**: ask a reader to describe the worst thing that could happen. A category ("he loses everything") means you have assertion. A picture means you have stakes.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Short Film, Limited Series, Vertical Micro-Drama | genres: Thriller, Crime, Drama, Social Drama, Survival, Family Drama
**Avoid when:** The threat's power depends on being unimaginable — cosmic horror runs on the opposite principle, and belongs to `tens.scale_dread` and `tens.refusing_to_name`. Also beware over-specifying a consequence you intend to avert: an image that precise becomes a promise, and averting it reads as a cheat. See `anta.absent_stakes` and `open.unearned_stakes`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "If we don't find her in the next twelve hours, the consequences for this family will be catastrophic."
> **Working:** In scene 4 we watched a different family clear a different flat, and the daughter carried the fish tank out to the car with the water still in it. In scene 31, nobody has to say anything at all about what happens if the money is late.

**Yields to:** `tens.scale_dread` — where the threat's power depends on being unimaginable. Cosmic horror runs on the opposite principle, and a precise image there is a domestication.  
`tens.picturable_consequence`

### The Room You Cannot Leave

**Also called:** social containment, the table you can't get up from, obligation as a cage
**What it is:** Pressure built from the social cost of leaving rather than from a locked door. The exit is physically available and unusable, because using it would announce something.
**Effect on the audience:** A specific bloodless suffocation that every audience has personally experienced, which is why it works on viewers who are entirely immune to physical jeopardy.
**Used for and where it works best:** Family dinners, board meetings, funerals, weddings, formally voluntary interviews, a car with one other person in it. Four rules. **Name the cost of leaving out loud, once and early** — someone jokes that whoever goes first is paying, or the seating plan is mentioned, or the speeches are at nine. **Give one character explicit permission to leave and have them not take it**; a cage whose key the audience can see is worse than a locked one. **Raise it by additions, not by time**: another guest, another topic, another person who does not know. **Keep the surface polite** — the moment anyone is openly hostile, the social contract breaks and the walls come down with it. Runs on `sub.power_gradient` and `tens.social_dread`. It is the social twin of `tens.closing_space`, which contracts the room physically; this one leaves the room exactly the same size.
**Best in:** formats: Stage Play, Feature Film, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Short Film, Half-Hour Comedy | genres: Family Drama, Social Drama, Comedy, Drama, Psychological, Workplace
**Avoid when:** The character genuinely has nothing to lose by walking out — then there are no walls and the scene is just people talking. Also weak in short form, where there is no time for the audience to learn what leaving would cost.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "I really should go." — "Oh, stay a bit longer." — "All right, then."
> **Working:** Her coat has been on the back of her chair, not on the hook, since she arrived. At twenty to ten the father-in-law starts on the thing about the house. At five to, somebody carries out a cake with her husband's name piped on it.

**Yields to:** `tens.micro_form_tension` — in short form, where there is no time for the audience to learn what leaving would cost.  
`tens.social_containment`

### Tension Under Thirty Seconds

**Also called:** micro-form pressure, the short-form clock, borrowed stakes — short *video*, not short prose (for flash fiction see `struct.flash_fiction`, for the prose poem `lyric.prose_poem`)
**What it is:** Tension in a piece too short to establish anything. The stakes are not built, they are borrowed from a situation the audience already carries, and the runtime is spent entirely on escalation rather than on setup.
**Effect on the audience:** Pressure inside three seconds. The cost is originality — borrowed situations are by definition familiar ones, and this is a trade rather than a trick.
**Used for and where it works best:** Vertical drama, reels, pre-roll, trailers, title sequences. Five rules. **Open on the second-worst moment, not the first** — an audience reconstructs the setup backwards far faster than you can state it forwards. **Borrow a situation with a clock already inside it**: the boarding gate, the test result, the message marked read, the parent's car pulling up; you inherit its pressure at no cost. **One threat, one question, no second strand.** **Escalate three times** — the audience needs a slope rather than a state, three is the shortest readable slope, and thirty seconds holds exactly three. **End on the question**, never the answer — `tens.suspended_ending`.
**Best in:** formats: Vertical Micro-Drama, Reels/Shorts, Trailer, Advertising, Short Film, Web Series | genres: Thriller, Drama, Horror, Crime, Romance, Comedy
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2028-03. The three-second window and the escalation count follow platform feed behaviour, which has changed twice since 2020; the borrowing principle underneath it is durable.
**Avoid when:** The situation has no cultural shorthand. A borrowed clock the audience does not own is worse than no clock at all, because the piece then spends its whole runtime explaining and arrives nowhere. See `tens.first_five_seconds` and `expo.short_form_frontload`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** 0–8s establish the office, the colleague and the relationship. 9–20s the conflict. 21–30s the threat.
> **Working:** 0–2s a hand deleting a message before it sends. 3s the door handle turns. 8s — "Who were you texting?" 15s the phone, face down, buzzes. 22s it buzzes again. 29s a hand reaches for it, and it is not hers.

**Yields to:** `expo.short_form_frontload` — where the situation has no cultural shorthand. A borrowed clock the audience does not own is worse than no clock, so the teaching has to come first or the piece arrives nowhere.  
`tens.micro_form_tension`

## Writing for a reader under stress

### The Instruction Read Under Stress

**Also called:** emergency instruction, the safety card, the crisis message, writing for a collapsed reader
**What it is:** Instruction written for someone whose reading comprehension has dropped — narrowed attention, no working memory, no tolerance for a subordinate clause — which is the state of every reader who actually needs it.
**Effect on the audience:** They do it, or they do not. This is the one place in the database where the measure is behavioural rather than felt: not whether the reader understood, but whether they acted.
**Used for and where it works best:** Safety cards, emergency alerts, evacuation notices, medication instructions, outage and incident messages, the crisis section of a corporate script — and any drama that reproduces one. Seven rules, and they are meant literally. **Action first, reason second, or no reason at all** — a stressed reader stops after the first clause. **One instruction per sentence, one sentence per line.** **Second person, imperative, active voice.** **No negation carrying critical meaning**: "do not remain in the building" fails under stress, "leave the building" does not. **Write at eighth-grade level or below**, which is the plain-language standard, and note that studied emergency messaging routinely lands above thirteenth grade. **Repeat the single most important instruction verbatim at the end** — verbatim, because a paraphrase is read as a second, different instruction. **Give one specific thing to do**; the specific action is what restores a sense of control, and the vague assurance is what destroys it.
**Best in:** formats: PSA, UX Writing, Corporate, Explainer, Voiceover Script, Educational | genres: Medical, Educational, Advocacy, Journalism, Disaster, Survival
**Avoid when:** The reader is calm and the task is complex. This format strips the *why*, and without the why a calm reader cannot adapt the instruction to a situation you did not anticipate — that is `rhythm.instruction_step` and `expo.explainer_scaffold`, which are the unhurried forms.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "In the unlikely event that an evacuation becomes necessary, occupants should make their way in an orderly fashion to their nearest designated assembly point, avoiding where possible the use of lifts or escalators."
> **Working:**
> "Leave the building now.
> Use the stairs.
> Go to the car park behind Block C.
> Do not go back for anything.
> Leave the building now."

**Yields to:** `rhythm.instruction_step` and `expo.explainer_scaffold` — where the reader is calm and the task is complex. This format strips the *why*, and without it a calm reader cannot adapt the instruction to a situation you did not anticipate.  
`tens.instruction_under_stress`

### The Warning Notice

**Also called:** the caution, the hazard line, the compelled warning, the safety label
**What it is:** A warning written to be obeyed rather than to satisfy whoever required it. The compelled caution is usually drafted by somebody protecting an organisation, which is exactly why it is usually ignored.
**Effect on the audience:** The difference between a reader who stops and a reader whose eye slides off. Compliance rises with three specific things and with almost nothing else.
**Used for and where it works best:** Product copy, packaging, signage, in-app cautions, medical leaflets, the line before a procedure, and the warning inside a drama that must be believed. Five rules, and the structure is not negotiable — it comes straight from the safety-labelling standards. **Three parts, in this order, grouped together: the hazard, the consequence, the avoidance.** What it is, what it will do to you, what to do instead. Drop the consequence and the warning becomes a rule, and rules get negotiated. **Be explicit about the consequence**: "may cause injury" is a rule, "can take a finger off" is a warning. **Match the signal word to the real severity** — the standard hierarchy reserves the strongest word for what *will* kill and the next for what *may*, and over-grading is the fastest route to `tens.warning_fatigue`. **Put it where the decision is made**, not where it is convenient to print. **One hazard per notice.**
**Best in:** formats: UX Writing, PSA, Product Video, Corporate, Explainer, Educational | genres: Medical, Educational, Advocacy, Workplace, Science, Journalism
**Avoid when:** The real purpose is liability and you have no authority over the wording. The honest craft move there is to add a readable instruction beside the compelled text, not to rewrite the compelled text. Note too that in drama a formally perfect warning is a signal that nobody will heed it — see `tens.threat_with_rules`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "CAUTION: Improper use may result in injury. Read all instructions before operating."
> **Working:** "WARNING — The blade keeps turning for four seconds after you let go. It will cut through a glove. Count to five before you reach in."

**Yields to:** `tens.instruction_under_stress` — where the wording is compelled and you have no authority over it. The honest move is to add a readable instruction beside the compelled text rather than to rewrite the compelled text.  
`tens.warning_notice`

### Warning Fatigue

**Also called:** alert fatigue, over-warning, habituation, the ignored alarm, crying wolf
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** Warning so often, so loudly, or about so little that the audience stops reading warnings at all — including the one that matters.
**Effect on the audience:** Measurable and severe. Habituation to repeated warnings is a documented decline in response rather than an impression: clinicians override the large majority of the interaction alerts they are shown, and public emergency-alert research finds people opting out of an entire channel rather than filtering it. The audience does not become annoyed. They become absent, and you cannot tell the difference from inside the system.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere. The same mechanism is why a story that threatens in every scene stops being frightening — `tens.threat_never_acts` and `tens.false_scare_cost` are the dramatic forms of this defect, and they fail for exactly this reason.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (worst in product, medical and platform writing, where every team adds its own caution and nobody owns the total; and in horror serials, where a threat is re-promised weekly and never spends)
**Avoid when:** Always. The repair is subtraction and grading rather than rewording: count the warnings one user meets in a single session, rank them by real severity, and delete or demote everything below the top tier. A warning that has never once been the reason somebody changed their behaviour is costing you the ones that were. See `tens.warning_notice`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** Six modal dialogs in one flow, each headed "Warning", five of them reversible.
> **Working:** One dialog in the flow: "This deletes the 4,200 photos in this album. There is no undo." Four of the others became inline text. The fifth became an undo button.

**Yields to:** `tens.warning_notice` — Grade by real severity; keep the one that matters.  
`tens.warning_fatigue`

## Coverage

The **Urgency** entry's missing card, the soft deadline, is `tens.soft_deadline`, with its named failure `tens.announced_deadline_dropped` and its two relatives `tens.human_deadline` and `tens.cost_of_waiting` — between them these are the three shapes uncounted pressure actually takes, and no route in the file could previously reach any of them. The **Instruction** entry's two missing cards, instruction read under stress and the warning notice, are `tens.instruction_under_stress` and `tens.warning_notice`, with `tens.warning_fatigue` as the failure that destroys both.

**What the research showed that the brief did not anticipate.** Three things.

First, **the shrinking room is two cards, and one of them already exists.** `tens.closing_space` in Part 1 owns spatial contraction, and owns it well. What was genuinely missing is the social version, where the room does not change size at all and the exit is unusable for reasons of obligation. That is written here as `tens.social_containment`, and the card says explicitly which half it is, so the pair reads correctly from either direction.

Second, **the warning notice and the instruction under stress are not variants of one card.** The safety-label literature gives a fixed three-part structure — hazard, consequence, avoidance — which a stressed-reader instruction must actively break, because it front-loads the action and drops the reason. Written as one card they contradict each other on their first rule.

Third, **warning fatigue is the same defect as a threat that never acts.** The dramatic and the practical version have one mechanism, and once that is visible, `tens.threat_never_acts` and `tens.false_scare_cost` stop being two unrelated horror notes and become instances of a measurable phenomenon. The card records the link in both directions.

One card carries a review date: `tens.micro_form_tension`. The other nine are durable.

Named failures in this file: `tens.announced_deadline_dropped`, `tens.warning_fatigue`.
