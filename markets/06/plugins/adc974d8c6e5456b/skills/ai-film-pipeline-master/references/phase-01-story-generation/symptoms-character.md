# Character complaints

Part of the symptom router. Open this file when the complaint is about character. Each complaint below is followed by the cards that repair it, best first; `LOOKUP.md` says which file holds each card.

---

### "The protagonist is passive"

The most common note in the trade, and it almost never means what it says. A passive-seeming protagonist is usually an active one whose decisions were never shown.

1. The decisions are missing, not the character — add the sequel unit
   `scene.reaction_dilemma_decision` · `scene.scene_and_sequel`
2. Check the structural cause: they may still be in reaction mode past the midpoint
   `struct.four_act_two_a` · `struct.four_act_midpoint` · `struct.four_act_two_b`
3. Give them a want in each scene, not just in the story
   `scene.give_a_want`

### "I don't care about this character"

1. Attachment is built in the weighing, not in the acting
   `scene.reaction_dilemma_decision`
2. Give them a stated limit early, casually, in a scene about something else
   `arc.moral_line`
3. Give them something false they believe and cannot see
   `arc.the_lie` · `arc.the_ghost`

### "The change at the end feels unearned"

1. There is no cost. Name what choosing the truth takes from them
   `arc.cost_of_truth`
2. The want and the need are not in conflict, so the climax is an arrival rather than a choice
   `arc.the_want` · `arc.the_need`
3. Check you are running the arc you think you are
   `arc.positive_change` · `arc.fall` · `arc.corruption` · `arc.disillusionment` · `arc.flat`
4. Build the climax so the Want is still on the table — one signature away — and refusing it is a visible forfeiture. If the Want has already been lost by the time he chooses, there is no choice, only an acceptance
   `arc.the_choice`
5. And nothing after the climax demonstrates it. Hand him the first-act situation again, small and undramatic, and let him handle it differently without anyone remarking on it
   `arc.proof_scene` · `end.mirror_ending`

### "The character explains their own psychology"

1. The moment a character can state the Lie, it stops being one
   `arc.the_lie` · `dial.on_the_nose`
2. Let someone else name it, wrongly, and let the character reject it
   `arc.the_need`
3. Externalise it into a character further down the same road
   `arc.mirror_character`

### "The antagonist is flat"

1. Give them the same want as the protagonist, so only one can have it
   `arc.shared_want_opponent` · `struct.truby_07_opponent`
2. Write their case as if you agreed with it
   `char.antagonist_case`
3. Build them to attack this protagonist's specific weakness, not a general one
   `char.tailored_opponent`
4. Let them be right about something
   `dial.refusing_the_frame` · `dial.concession_that_wins`
5. Give them a plan of their own rather than a set of reactions
   `struct.truby_11_opponent_plan`

### "The introduction doesn't land"

1. Introduce on an action that decides something, not on description
   `char.defining_action_entrance`
2. Or enter against the type the audience expects
   `char.entrance_against_type`
3. Establish competence before vulnerability
   `char.competence_first`
4. Let them be talked about before they arrive
   `char.reputation_before_entrance`
5. Give one early beat that buys sympathy without asking for it
   `char.sympathy_beat`

### "The character is flat / one-note"

1. Give them one contradiction the whole character rests on
   `char.load_bearing_contradiction`
2. Name the one rule they break, and when
   `char.the_exception`
3. Give them a zone where they are genuinely bad at something
   `char.incompetence_zone`
4. Charge their competence — skill that costs nothing is decoration
   `char.competence_cost`
5. Write the list of things they would never do
   `char.never_list` · `arc.moral_line`

### "I can't tell the characters apart"

1. Build the ensemble as a grid of differences, not a set of individuals
   `char.ensemble_grid`
2. Give each a physical signature and a stress tell
   `char.physical_signature` · `char.stress_tell`
3. Give each a different thing they notice in the same room
   `char.what_they_notice`
4. Give every supporting character their own want
   `char.supporting_character_want`
5. Names are design, not labels
   `char.name_as_design`
6. If they are distinguishable in the description and not in the mouth, the fix is a procedure rather than a technique: assign each principal six *countable* speech parameters before drafting — mean sentence length, contraction rate, question rate, whether they finish sentences — so that no two share more than two values
   `char.idiolect_sheet`

### "He hardly speaks and it reads as me having nothing for him"

The difference between a quiet character and an underwritten one is whether the quantity was decided.

1. Set the line count as a parameter and hold it across the whole piece — a fixed, deliberately small allocation, so quietness is a property of the person rather than an accident of who got the good scenes. The rule that makes it work is that he must be *present* in scenes he says nothing in, doing something the reader can watch
   `char.silence_as_trait`
2. In a group scene, who is silent is a staging decision and it changes every line around it
   `dial.who_is_silent`
3. If he is quiet because he is withholding something specific, that is not a trait, it is subtext, and it is built differently
   `sub.silence_as_content`

### "Everybody in this swears the same way"

1. Swearing is not a property of the piece but of each person in it — how, when, how much, and in front of whom. Set it per character and it carries class, trade, age, control and the state they are currently in for free
   `char.profanity_budget`
2. The instrument is the break: the man who never swears and does, once, is worth more than the man who always does
   `dial.register_shift`

### "The want is vague"

1. Make the desire physical — something that can be held, signed, entered or lost
   `char.desire_made_physical` · `arc.the_want`
2. State the lie in one sentence you never put in a character's mouth
   `char.lie_in_one_line` · `arc.the_lie`
3. Convert the wound into a rule the character lives by
   `char.wound_as_rule` · `arc.the_ghost`
4. Those two are the only things worth harvesting from the standard development instrument, and the card exists to say where to stop: fill it in for the protagonist and the opponent, take the rule and the sentence, and close it. It is diagnostic and single-cause by construction, it generates no plot, and a cast in which every behaviour has a documented origin reads as a hospital rather than a world
   `char.wound_questionnaire`

### "There's too much backstory"

1. Spend it — every piece of history must buy a present-tense behaviour
   `char.backstory_economy`
2. Give them one secret and let its pressure do the work
   `char.the_secret`
3. Show who they are when nobody is watching
   `char.alone_behaviour` · `char.carried_object`

### "The relationships feel generic"

1. Each character is a different person with each other character
   `char.relationship_self`
2. Write the scene as a status transaction and mark who gains
   `char.status_transaction` · `char.status_flip`

### "They want each other and the story wants something else"

That is not a problem, it is the engine, and most drafts have it the wrong way round — the romance is decoration bolted to the spine instead of a second thing the same person is holding.

1. Give the character a desire for a *person* that is genuinely separate from the story goal, and make it capable of losing. If they can have both the person and the goal, you have written a reward rather than a want
   `char.desire_second_want`
2. The incompatibility must be practical rather than thematic, so it can be staged: the job is in another city, the person is on the other side of the case, the family will not survive the marriage
3. Have the choice made once, early, and wrongly, so the second half is spent on the consequence
   `arc.the_want` · `rel.relationship_want`
4. In a tight thriller or a short there is room for one spine. Cut it rather than shrink it

### "They fancy each other and the scene is two people saying so"

1. Courtship is mechanics, not declaration. Every approach needs a *pretext* — the returned book, the invented errand, the question about work — and the pretext is the characterisation, because it shows what this person thinks is permissible
   `char.courtship_behaviour`
2. Write what they are offering. A courtship is a proposal about what life with this person would be like, and that is a thing an audience can weigh
3. Where the culture forbids a direct approach — which is most of history and much of the present — the whole exchange runs through third parties and objects
   `sub.high_context_indirection` · `sub.erotic_code`
4. If instead the script keeps asserting that somebody is irresistible and staging only other people finding them so, the missing card is the skill itself, which is *attention* and never lines: the seducer notices one true thing nobody has said to that person in a year, and says it without asking for anything
   `char.seduction_competence` · `char.informed_attribute`
5. Never write the approach as courtship where the story is about coercion or a power gradient. The mechanics are identical and the framing launders it

### "She's there at the end as his reward"

1. That is `char.prize_love_interest`. Three tests, in order: does she want something he is not, does she ever say no to him and hold it, and does she have one scene with somebody else in which he is not the subject. Two failures out of three and the diagnosis is confirmed
2. The repair is two cards applied together — a want of her own, and the removal of the pure-reaction default
   `char.supporting_character_want` · `char.only_reacts`
3. The structural version is stronger: give her a want that his victory actively costs, so the ending has to be negotiated rather than awarded
4. The tell in a draft is the reconciliation she is never asked about — he changes, he arrives, she is simply there. The honest exception is the fable and fairy-tale register, where the marriage is a formal closing device the audience has consented to
   `theme.fable_contract`

### "The protagonist is likeable but dull"

1. Interesting beats likeable. Specificity is the currency, not warmth
   `char.likeable_vs_interesting`
2. If they exist for the audience to stand in, they have no character of their own
   `char.audience_surrogate_trap`
3. If the fix is not landing, the structural cause may be the passive-protagonist pattern
   `char.passive_protagonist_fix`

### "I don't know whose story this is" (character side)

1. Run the protagonist test before rewriting anything
   `char.protagonist_test`
2. A reluctant protagonist still has to choose; a protagonist who is wrong is not a passive one
   `char.reluctant_protagonist` · `char.protagonist_who_is_wrong`
3. An unlikeable lead is held by competence and by wanting, not by warmth
   `char.unlikeable_lead`

### "The character has no interior life"

1. Establish what they think is normal — everything else measures against it
   `char.normal_baseline` · `char.theory_of_others`
2. Give them a superstition and a sense of what they deserve
   `char.superstition` · `char.deserving_setting`
3. For faith and community characters, write the belief from inside it
   `char.writing_the_believer` · `char.community_built`

### "The character's job is just a label"

1. A trade shapes perception and syntax before it shapes the plot
   `char.occupational_perception` · `char.trade_vocabulary`
2. Money is characterisation — what they cannot afford, and who pays
   `char.money_specifics` · `char.cannot_afford` · `char.who_pays_most`

### "The rich character is rich in the way a poor writer imagines it"

1. That is `char.wealth_as_objects` — the watch, the car, the marque of the kitchen, the label in the coat — and the diagnostic is one pass: delete every brand name from the character's scenes and see whether anything about their position survives. If nothing does, the wealth was never written, the objects were
2. It reads as the writer's research rather than the character's life. Readers who know those objects notice the errors; readers who do not are being handed a price tag and asked to be impressed, and they resent it
3. The repair is the **omitted calculation** — write what this person never has to work out. They do not check the price before ordering, do not convert the currency, do not ask what the appointment will cost, do not count the days to the payment date. One omitted calculation in a scene where somebody else performs it is worth a page of description
   `char.inherited_ease`
4. Then split inherited ease from earned ease, because under identical circumstances they move in opposite directions. Inherited is *incurious*: it does not know what things cost, undervalues what it has, is often shabby on purpose and generous carelessly. Earned is *vigilant*: it knows every price exactly, overvalues what it has, is correct rather than shabby, and is generous in ways that can be seen. Put them in one room and the inherited one is rude to a waiter without noticing while the earned one is gracious and watches whether it registered
5. Give the inherited character one thing they *do* count, because a person with no anxiety at all is not a character — and it is never money. And do not make ease equal cruelty; the accurate version is that it simply does not notice, which is also harder for the audience to dismiss
6. The absence is a comparative instrument, so it needs somebody in the scene performing the calculation. With only one class in the story there is nothing to measure against
   `sub.consumption_tell`
7. The narrow places where naming the object is correct: satire, where the brand is the joke; lifestyle and advertising work, where the object is the subject; and a character whose whole personality is the performance of having — in which case the list belongs in *their* mouth rather than in the narration

### "She's had money for twenty years and I can't find what's left of where she started"

1. The residue — the behaviours that did not change when the circumstances did — is the most reliable evidence of where a person actually came from, and it does in one line of action what a flashback does in a scene
   `char.class_tell_survives_money`
2. It lives in three places and almost nowhere else. *Food and waste*: what they will not throw away, what they take from a hotel breakfast, whether they can leave wine in a glass. *Institutions*: how they speak to a doctor, a police officer, a bank, since deference and aggression are both learned early and neither is unlearned. *Contingency*: what they keep in case, what they will not do without a written appointment, whether they reach an airport two hours early or forty minutes
3. Pick **one** and repeat it three times across the piece, unremarked. Two tells is a portrait of a class; three is a sketch show
4. Class movement is asymmetric, and that asymmetry is what makes it playable. Acquired ease is a skill and can be performed well; the early habits are not skills and cannot be suppressed under stress — so write the acquired register as the surface and let the residue appear exactly where the character has stopped monitoring herself
   `char.stress_tell` · `char.normal_baseline`
5. An unused residue is a quirk, and a quirk is `char.trait_not_character`. If the class origin is not in play anywhere in the story, do not plant one

### "The rank is doing all the characterisation"

1. Write the two things at once — the behaviour the rank requires and the person underneath — and find the scenes in what the rank *forbids this particular person*: the sergeant who may not be liked, the surgeon who may not say she does not know, the foreman who may not be first to leave
   `char.rank_and_person`
2. Give them one subordinate they cannot manage and one superior they manage effortlessly. That is a portrait in two relationships
3. Promote or demote them once, on the page. Nothing reveals a person faster than what they keep and what they drop when the position changes
4. The failure this prevents is rank written as personality — the boss is bossy, the junior is timid — which decides the scene before anybody speaks
   `char.status_transaction`

### "He's forty and his family still treats him as the one who fixes things"

1. Because they assigned him the job in childhood and he is still doing it in rooms that have nothing to do with them. Build the character from the role — the fixer, the blamed one, the one who is not trouble, the one who makes everybody laugh — and play it as still running
   `char.family_role`
2. Allocate the roles so no two siblings share one, and make sure at least one character is visibly no longer suited to theirs
3. The turn nobody sees coming is the role being *reassigned*, in one scene. And the family's strongest instrument is the request addressed to the job rather than to the person, which is why it cannot be refused
4. Birth order as an ongoing dynamic is a different card
   `rel.sibling_order`
5. Keep the therapy register out of the dialogue. A character who names his own role out loud converts a structure into a speech
   `sub.behaviour_contradicts`

### "He left the army ten years ago and it doesn't show"

1. Build him out of the group that formed him — army, gang, ship, ward, seminary, care system, boarding school — and write the unit's *grammar* rather than its rituals: how they say no, who eats first, what counts as an insult, what it is unforgivable to do. Grammar transfers out of the institution and rituals do not
   `char.unit_member`
2. Give him one habit that made perfect sense inside and is faintly alarming outside
3. Decide the price of leaving. A unit that cost nothing to leave was never a unit
4. Two people from the same unit should behave alike in exactly one specific way and differ everywhere else — that is how the formation is proved without flattening either of them
   `rel.institutional_bond`
5. The failure mode is the institution as costume: the slang, the tattoo, the nickname, and a man who behaves like everybody else the moment the plot starts

### "The character has cancer and the script is about cancer"

The master move for every bodily condition — illness, disability, injury, pain, addiction, pregnancy, age — and the one that separates craft from etiquette. Start here before any of the complaints below it.

1. Write it as three lists rather than as an adjective: what they cannot do, what they can do only at a price, and what the doing costs somebody else. The test is that the constraint must generate scenes you would not otherwise have written — if the condition can be deleted and every scene still plays, you wrote a label
   `char.condition_as_constraint`
2. Then write the admin. The appointment, the form, the pharmacy, the broken lift: that is where the time actually goes, and time is the thing you are dramatising
3. For anything chronic, dramatise the *allocation* rather than the symptom — a finite capacity spent before the day starts, and the trades made to protect what matters. Build one good day early so the bad ones have a baseline, and let the character be wrong about their own budget at least once
   `char.management_day`
4. Where the body is a source of pressure rather than a fact about the person, the tension-side card is the one you want
   `tens.unreliable_body`

### "He's in a wheelchair and I've written him as brave"

1. That is `char.pity_lever`, and two questions find it. Who in the scene is being changed — if the disabled or ill character exists so that a non-disabled one can learn something, the lever is in the room. And delete the condition: if the want goes with it, or the want *was* the condition, there was never a person there
2. The repair is the ordinary machinery restored — a want, a lie, a contradiction, a bad decision — with the condition constraining it rather than replacing it
   `char.condition_as_constraint`
3. Then reverse the default the audience arrived with: build the character around what they know that nobody else in the cast knows — the routes, the timings, which staff are useful, how the system actually behaves — and put the obstacle in the environment rather than in the body. The step, the lift out of order, the taxi firm that lies, all three of which he predicted
   `char.disability_expertise`
4. The inspiration half of the same fault lands as the same insult from the other side: expertise inflated into compensation, the blind character with impossible hearing. The card names it
5. The legitimate neighbour, where the feeling is genuinely the subject, is first-person testimony — the person speaking for themselves about their own life
   `emo.testimony_emotion`

### "The condition is in the character description and nowhere else"

1. That is `char.costless_condition`, the mirror of the pity lever and the commoner of the two in commercial drafts. Two-minute audit: search the draft for the condition, and if it appears only in the description and one sympathetic scene, it is decoration. Then take the three hardest physical or logistical moments in the story and ask what it does to each — "nothing" means it is switched off exactly where it would have been most productive
2. The repair is not suffering, it is *cost*: minutes, money, help asked for, a thing done a different way
   `char.condition_as_constraint`
3. Damage persists. An injury on page 40 constrains page 70, and the healing continuity error — stabbed on Monday, climbing rope on Wednesday — is the form this fault takes in action work
4. The honest neighbour is a well-managed condition in a world built to accommodate it; the difference is that the accommodation is visible and somebody arranged it

### "She's pregnant and it changed nothing about the plot"

1. Written properly it is a moving constraint with a terminus nobody can negotiate, and the constraint set has to change every act — the first trimester's problem is concealment and nausea, the second's is disclosure and money, the third's is logistics and fear
   `char.pregnancy_clock`
2. Play the social change hardest. Pregnancy converts a woman into public property, and everybody in the scene acquires an opinion, a hand and a piece of advice
3. Give her a want that is not the baby, or you have written a condition and no character. The same clock runs in reverse where she does not want it, and there the scarce resource is time to decide
4. Do not reach for it as a third-act stakes injection — it is the most transparent of them, and the audience reads a writer topping up rather than a life happening

### "The addiction is all remorse and no day"

1. Build it out of the daily operational load instead: what it costs, where it comes from, who must not find out, and how much of the day goes on arranging it. Give the habit a number and a calendar — an addiction is first of all an expense with a deadline
   `char.addiction_logistics` · `char.money_specifics`
2. Maintaining the cover story is labour, and that makes it the same engine as a secret
   `char.the_secret`
3. Put the tell in a competence: the thing they used to do well that now takes two attempts. And let the habit win an argument early and honestly, because if it delivers nothing, nobody would have one
4. Keep the relapse a decision inside a scene rather than a montage
   `char.relapse_beat`

### "The character under pressure isn't convincing"

1. Decide what breaks first, and build the ladder to it
   `char.what_breaks_first` · `char.pressure_ladder`
2. Let the competence fail, specifically
   `char.competence_fails` · `char.panic_behaviour`
3. Show them alone and frightened
   `char.frightened_alone` · `char.hands_test`
4. If the problem is a specific decision the reader will not accept, the missing element is usually physical rather than psychological. Establish the depletion — no sleep, no food, cold, pain, fourteen hours standing — two scenes before you cash it, degrade *one* faculty rather than the person, and write it as the abandoned sentence and the repeated question, never as yawning
   `char.degraded_decision`
5. The line the audience will not cross: tiredness excuses a bad decision and never a stupid one, and the difference is whether the character's own logic survives

### "The change doesn't convince"

1. Some characters cannot change, and that must be the argument rather than an omission
   `char.cannot_change` · `char.unearned_change`
2. Change that happens offstage needs evidence on return
   `char.offstage_change`
3. And the relapse beat is what makes a change believable
   `char.relapse_beat`
4. But only the short one, placed before the final choice and overruled inside the same scene. A relapse after the change has been proved is `arc.padding_relapse`, which is a different complaint and is held separately below

### "I can't decide whether he changes at the end"

Not a taste question. Each arc delivers a different verdict on the same truth, so the arc is read off the claim rather than chosen.

1. Take the controlling idea, decide what verdict the ending must deliver, and read the arc off it
   `theme.arc_carries_claim` · `theme.controlling_idea`
2. Know what a flat arc costs you: the burden of proof moves to the world, so it needs a much stronger opposition system than a change arc does
   `arc.flat` · `anta.three_levels`
3. A negative arc still argues positively — a fall proves the value by showing the cost of its absence, which is why tragedy is not nihilism
   `arc.fall` · `arc.corruption`

### "He doesn't change and I don't know whether that's a problem"

Four different things wear that look and they are not interchangeable. Decide which one you are in before repairing anything, because three of them are shapes and only one is a fault.

1. He holds the Truth already and the world is what moves. Legitimate, and it carries one requirement writers skip: at least one supporting character must complete a full change, or a steadfast lead reads as a smug one
   `arc.flat`
2. He is not wrong about anything — he lacks knowledge, skill or nerve, and the story supplies it. Nothing is unlearned; something is added. Warm, and thin for anyone over about twelve unless you find the Lie under the incapacity, which is almost always "I am not the kind of person who does this"
   `arc.growth_no_lie`
3. Nothing in the situation can be fixed — the illness, the verdict, the exile — and the whole movement is in how he holds the same facts at the end. Acceptance played directly is inert; give him something small and adjacent he *can* change and let him fight for that instead
   `arc.acceptance`
4. He is the same person and has arrived at a verdict he could not have reached at the start. The arc is the shift in *which question he is answering* — from "which is safer" to "which can I live with" — and both options need a named advocate in the cast, neither of them a fool
   `arc.decision_arc` · `char.protagonist_who_is_wrong`

### "He changes and I can't point at the scene where it happened"

Because the change is in your notes and in the pitch rather than on the page. Two instruments, and the order matters — locate the scenes first, then read them.

1. The arc has four load-bearing moments, each fastened to a plot beat rather than floated between them: the Lie shown working, the Lie first seriously challenged, the Lie used and rewarded, the Lie abandoned or re-embraced. Name all four by page number. If you cannot, you have a theme rather than an arc
   `arc.turn_placement`
2. Same card, second rule, and the one more often broken: no arc beat may sit in a scene with no external consequence. An interior turn with nothing visible won or lost plays as a mood change and the audience will not file it
3. Then run the four-scene read as its own pass. It is a procedure, not a repair, and it is what tells you the arc lives in the bible rather than in the script
   `arc.arc_audit`
4. The commonest finding is that knowing and doing were collapsed into one beat. They are two: the insight changes nothing by itself, and the choice taken after it is the change
   `arc.self_revelation` · `arc.the_choice`
5. The second commonest is that the scene you are looking for is the one after the climax, and it does not exist yet. It is usually about four lines long
   `arc.proof_scene`

### "I don't know whether my character has an arc at all"

Both answers are procedures rather than repairs — things you do to a draft, in a fixed order — and neither of them writes a scene for you.

1. Before outlining, and before every rewrite, write the one-line test: *X believes A; by the end, at the cost of B, X acts as though C*. A and C must be incompatible or there is no arc; B must be a loss that can be filmed, not "his innocence"; and the sentence may not contain *learns*, *realises*, *grows*, *discovers* or *finally*, all five of which are where the missing mechanism hides
   `arc.arc_statement`
2. On a finished draft, find four specific scenes and read only those, consecutively, with nothing between them
   `arc.arc_audit`
3. The belief half on its own, written down before any of it is staged
   `char.lie_in_one_line`
4. Keep the sentence out of the pitch and out of the script bible. It is a working test, and read aloud in a room it sounds mechanical; left where a director or an actor finds it, it hands them the conclusion

### "The lie never really gets tested"

Not the same as a missing arc. The Lie, the Ghost and the Need are all specified — usually at length — and the plot never once applies force to any of them, so the story happens next to the arc rather than through it.

1. That is `arc.unattacked_lie`, and it is the most common structural fault in developed drafts by writers who have read the theory. The complaint it arrives as is "I didn't care about him", which sends people off to add sympathy beats and backstory, neither of which is the problem
2. One question settles it: which scene would be *easy* for this character if they did not hold the Lie. If the answer is none, the plot does not touch the arc
3. The cause is nearly always an opponent built from a genre template, who cannot press on this weakness except by accident
   `char.tailored_opponent`
4. Then place the earliest real test deliberately, at or just before the first act turn, and put it in the mouth of someone the character cannot dismiss — not a stranger, not an enemy, because both are easy to discount
   `arc.first_challenge`

### "The audience just thinks he's being stupid"

1. They have never seen the flaw pay. Give it one deliberate, visible success, witnessed by somebody whose opinion he values, with nobody in the scene signalling disapproval — and do it exactly once, because two rewarded wins and the audience concludes the story agrees with the Lie
   `arc.lie_rewarded`
2. The other half of the same defect is a weak rationalisation. When the Lie is first challenged the explaining-away must be *good*, or the character is merely foolish and the investment ends there. The same card holds the placement rule and this one
   `arc.first_challenge`
3. In kids' work and fable the contract runs the other way and the wrong behaviour must visibly fail, so neither move applies
   `theme.fable_contract`

### "He could back out at any moment and the second half knows it"

1. Nothing has been closed behind him. Make it an *act* rather than a decision — something performed in the world with a witness or a record: a signature, a confession, a wire transfer, a name on a form. A resolve can be revised overnight
   `arc.point_of_no_return`
2. He must not understand at the time that it was the point of no return. Let him find the door shut two scenes later, when he tries to go back
3. And he may never say so. A character who announces there is no going back has converted a structural fact into a line of dialogue, and the audience sees the writer standing behind him
4. Placement moves with the arc: near the second act turn in a positive one, and much later, at the third refusal, in a fall
   `struct.stc_break_two` · `arc.fall`

### "Someone tells him the truth and that's the revelation"

1. That is `arc.told_revelation` — the arc closed on the strength of having been told. The audience credits the change to the speaker, demotes the protagonist to a passenger in his own story, and leaves with the theme already stated, so the ending has nothing left to demonstrate
2. The repair is mechanical and reliable: keep the speech, move it to the middle of the story, and have him *reject* it. Then let the same insight arrive later through an action of his own, with a cost attached — the insight is not wrong, its delivery system is
   `arc.self_revelation`
3. Then prove it in an ordinary moment afterwards, where nobody present knows there was a decision
   `arc.proof_scene`
4. A second repair, useful in a rewrite: give the speech to someone who is right about the what and wrong about the why, so he has to correct them and discovers it while arguing
5. The honest exceptions are kids' work below about seven, where an adult naming the lesson is part of the contract, and therapy-set drama where the naming is the subject and is resisted
   `theme.explained_theme` · `char.mouthpiece`

### "The plot fixes his problem for him"

1. That is `arc.plot_solved_arc`: the antagonist dies, the money arrives, the illness resolves, the letter is intercepted — and the choice the arc was built toward never has to be made. The audience rarely names it; they report that the ending happened too easily and then quietly stop trusting the earlier stakes
2. It is subtler than `end.deus_ex_machina` and commoner, because the convenient event is usually well planted and the writer defends it as set up. Planting is not the issue; agency is
3. The test: delete the event and ask whether he would still have had to choose. If he would, put the event *before* the choice so it raises the price instead of removing it
   `arc.the_choice`
4. The strongest single repair is to let the event happen and require the choice anyway — the antagonist dies and he must still decide whether to say what he did

### "Everything peaks in the middle and the last act is admin"

1. That is `arc.midpoint_peak`, and it usually turns up in a draft whose midpoint was rewritten to be stronger. The action can be escalating steeply and the film will still feel as though it is coasting, because the question the audience was tracking was answered forty minutes ago
2. Either demote the midpoint change to a *partial* one — he grasps the Truth intellectually and immediately proves he cannot yet act on it, which puts the beat back to doing its proper job
   `struct.stc_midpoint`
3. Or keep it and supply the second half's real question: he has changed, and must now find out what the change costs. That is a different story and it needs a new want by page 60
   `arc.cost_of_truth`
4. What never works is a relapse inserted to reopen the question
   `arc.padding_relapse`

### "He keeps relapsing and it's starting to feel like filler"

1. That is `arc.padding_relapse` if it arrives *after* the change has been proved, runs for a sequence or more, and is resolved by the character deciding again what he already decided
2. The distinction from the legitimate beat is precise: the real one is short, comes *before* the final choice, and is overruled inside the same scene at a cost
   `char.relapse_beat`
3. The test is removal. Take it out and see whether the ending still works; if it does, it was runtime, and the usual root cause is a script fifteen pages short and the only reversal you know you can stage
4. The honest long versions are tragedy and addiction narrative, where the relapse is the ending rather than an obstacle, and recovery drama where the repetitions differ from each other in something more than severity

### "Two of them need the same scene to change in"

1. Stage the clash rather than playing both at full weight. An audience tracks one turn per scene and will take whichever arrives with the louder action, so the second arc is the one they remember as having "just sort of ended". Three solutions in order of preference: stagger the secondary beat one scene earlier, subordinate it so the supporting character's revelation *causes* the protagonist's choice, or split the scene across a cut
   `arc.collision`
2. Look for it at outline stage. It is cheap to solve on a card and expensive to solve in a draft
3. The most common real instance is a climax where the protagonist chooses and the antagonist is also redeemed. That needs its own scene, usually the one before
   `anta.antagonist_arc`
4. Left unsolved to the end of a film it presents as a different complaint altogether
   `end.too_many_endings`

### "The supporting character's arc is eating the film"

1. It has a budget and you are over it: three scenes — the position stated, the position tested, the position changed — where the protagonist gets twelve, with the middle one sharing a scene with the main arc so the two are welded rather than parallel
   `arc.supporting_arc`
2. Give one to at most two characters. A third makes the piece an ensemble whether you intended one or not, and it should then be built as one
   `arc.group_arc`
3. A want is one line and every functional role gets one; an arc is three scenes. Handing an arc to the waiter is the failure the want card warns about
   `char.supporting_character_want` · `char.only_reacts`
4. And if it resolves *after* the protagonist's climax, that is what makes a film feel as though it does not know it has finished. Under seventy minutes, a supporting arc is a deleted scene being written in advance

### "Two characters are going opposite ways and it reads as a diagram"

1. Check the three requirements before touching anything else: a starting condition the audience can see is identical, the same opportunity offered to both at least once, and a crossing point that is a single scene both are in — the moment they are briefly level
   `arc.double_arc`
2. The failure is symmetry. Mapping the two beat for beat gives the audience something they can read by the midpoint, after which they stop watching and start waiting. And after the crossing, deliberately cut the screen time of the one moving downward, because an audience that watches a descent in equal detail begins to prefer it
3. Under ninety minutes, or with a cast above six, one of the two becomes a summary. The lighter versions carry one full arc instead of two
   `theme.counter_example_character` · `arc.mirror_character`

### "The change belongs to the crew and nobody in it moves"

1. Then the unit of change is the group, which is a shape rather than a fault. Name the group's Lie as something the group *says* — a motto, a rule, a joke they all repeat — so the audience can hear it change
   `arc.group_arc`
2. Give exactly one member a full personal arc as the group's proxy, and stage the turn as collective action, never a vote. The failure mode is change by acclamation: one speech, everybody nods, and the audience counts the nods
3. A group arc laid over an undifferentiated group is a mood. Build the differences first
   `char.ensemble_grid`
4. And do not bolt one onto a piece that has a clear protagonist. That is where the note "the ending belongs to everyone and no one" comes from

### "The thing that changes is the company, not a person"

Not the same complaint as the one above, and the difference decides the whole build. A crew is a group of people who can be counted and named; an institution is a shared operating belief that outlives whoever currently holds it. If your subject is a hospital, a regiment, a parish, a newspaper or a family firm, you are in this one.

1. The institution begins holding a belief it says out loud about itself, meets pressure it cannot answer on its own terms, and ends transformed or dead — while the people inside it may not move at all
   `arc.institutional_arc`
2. The test that tells you which card you are on: if one person runs the place, this is not an institutional arc. The moment the change has an author it collapses into an ordinary character arc with a large office attached, and the entire value of the shape is that nobody chose it
   `arc.group_arc`
3. Do not run it as a subplot. It is slow and made of small evidential beats, and at subplot scale it reads as background texture rather than as movement
4. The evidence is procedural rather than emotional — a form that gets a new box, a rule that stops being enforced, a phrase that enters the building's vocabulary

### "The narrator is the one who changes and I only tracked the character"

1. Then the arc is in the telling, and it is visible in the prose itself: the events are fixed, and what moves is the teller's relationship to them
   `arc.narrator_arc`
2. Check first that you are not running two arcs of the same shape at two altitudes. If the narrated character already changes the same way, that is redundancy the reader feels as slowness, and the narrator's version always wins because it is closer
3. It also asks the reader to attend to the telling, which is the wrong instruction in cool, tightly plotted genre work — there the reader has been trained to watch the events
4. Do not confuse it with a narrator who cannot be trusted about himself. That is a property of the telling and it does not move; this one does, and the complaint for it is separate
   `pov.split_narrator` · `pov.retrospective_distance`

### "The lead changes every few episodes and the show still has to move"

1. Then the arc belongs to the *position* rather than to the person — the detective on this beat, the wife in this house, the night nurse — and successive occupants each serve one stretch of a curve none of them completes
   `arc.role_arc`
2. The precondition: the role must deform whoever holds it. A position that leaves its occupant unchanged has no arc, only a rota
3. The cost is affection. Continuity of person is what builds it, and a form that replaces its lead spends its emotional budget on re-establishment — so do not use this where the audience is being asked to love somebody

### "He is only ever described by other people"

1. That is a shape, not a gap: the change exists as an assembly of witness accounts, and the arc the audience builds is an artefact of *who testifies in what order*
   `arc.reported_arc`
2. Which means the order of testimony is the structure. Sequence the witnesses by what each one makes the previous account mean
3. It guarantees distance, and that is the mechanism rather than a side effect — so if the climax needs the audience inside him, this form will have to break its own rule to get there and the break reads as a cheat
4. Weakest on a subject the audience already knows from elsewhere, where testimony is received as commentary rather than as construction

### "The arc stops one scene before the change"

Two different things look like this, and only one of them is unfinished.

1. If the piece runs the whole machinery — the Lie shown working, the pressure, the collapse of the old position — and then ends with the character standing at the change and the change unwritten, that is a deliberate shape and the audience is meant to be left holding it
   `arc.unfinished_arc`
2. It is off the table where the contract promises a landing: romance, kids' work, redemption, most genre, anything commissioned as uplift. And in series television it only survives at a season end — across an episode break it is not withholding, it is a cliffhanger, and it will be judged as one
3. If instead the character dies, leaves or is removed partway through, the completion transfers: somebody else carries it out, or it is left as a debt the survivors owe
   `arc.interrupted_arc`
4. The disqualifying case for that second one: if the removed character existed to motivate somebody else, an arc invented in order to be interrupted is worse than no arc, because it advertises the intention. And never do it twice in one piece
5. Neither of these is the fault where the change simply was not written. That is a separate complaint
   `arc.arc_audit`

### "He does everything a changed man does and I want it to be a lie"

1. Give him every visible marker of a completed arc — the apology, the humility, the new routine, the proof scene — and build the piece so the audience believes it first and discovers it second
   `arc.false_arc`
2. The structural requirement it cannot work without: one character who was present for the original behaviour and is still present at the end. Without that witness the reveal is an assertion, and the audience will conclude you changed your mind rather than that he was lying
3. Think hard before using it in work aimed at people living the same pattern. It is a claim that change is not available, which is a serious thing to say by accident
   `theme.arc_carries_claim`

### "The cast is too big"

1. There is a limit, and it is lower than most drafts assume
   `char.cast_size_limit`
2. Combine two characters before cutting one
   `char.combine_characters`
3. Know which one exists to be lost, and which one the audience will miss
   `char.exists_to_be_lost` · `char.the_missed_one`

### "They were in episode two and nobody remembers them"

1. The design happens at the **first** appearance, not at the return. Plant one retrievable hook — one, not three — and make it doable rather than descriptive: the man who counted the crates twice, the woman who would not sit down. Physical description is not retrievable; behaviour is
   `char.half_remembered_return`
2. Return them performing the same hook *before* they are named. The audience should recognise the behaviour a beat before they recover the name, and that half-beat gap is the whole pleasure — a reader who retrieves a name unaided feels competent, and the same reader handed a reminder feels managed
3. Change exactly one thing about them and let it carry the elapsed time. A returning character who is identical is a prop; one who is unrecognisable is a new character with an old name
   `char.behavioural_continuity`
4. Give the return a reason in the plot and not only in the structure. An audience forgives a coincidence of place far more readily than a coincidence of timing
5. Do not prop it with a reminder line. If the character has to be reintroduced in dialogue the hook failed, and the fix belongs back at the first appearance
   `expo.recap_sequence`
6. Where the audience genuinely cannot be expected to hold the plant — short form, a very large cast, or a gap of more than a season in a weekly show — reinstall them through a third party instead of pretending the memory is there
   `info.recap_without_recap`

### "Nobody can pronounce my characters' names and I won't change them"

You do not have to change them. You have to decide how they are spelled, once, and treat that as characterisation rather than as copy-editing.

1. Choose one romanisation system for the whole project and write it into the bible. Scholarly systems are unambiguous and unreadable aloud; popular transcription is readable and ambiguous, which is why one name can have a hundred English spellings
   `char.transliteration_choice`
2. For a screenplay, choose the spelling that gets an actor closest to the sound on a *cold read*, and put the pronunciation in a one-line note rather than in a glossary
3. Never mix within a system — the article and the clan name are not interchangeable, and a script that mixes them announces itself in the first page
4. Then make variation deliberate when you use it. A man whose name is spelled one way on his passport and another on his shop sign has a history, and it is a better history than a paragraph
5. Keep the total count of unfamiliar proper nouns low, whatever system you pick
   `expo.proper_noun_budget` · `char.name_as_design`

### "A reader from that world says the names are wrong"

1. That is `char.decorative_exotic_name` — names chosen for how foreign or impressive they look rather than for whether they come from the world of the story. It matters more than it sounds, because readers from that culture stop reading the story and start auditing the writer, and a wrong name is evidence that the rest was not checked either
2. Three checks over the cast list, before the first draft. Could a real person of that community, generation and faith be called this. Can an actor say it cold, once, correctly. And does the list read as one society rather than as a menu — one Arabic name, one Norse, one invented, all in the same village, is the tell
3. The repair is a real name pool for the exact place and decade, then the differentiation pass on top of it
   `char.name_as_design`
4. On the fantasy side the same fault is the apostrophe as a spice: five invented names, none meaning anything, and no rule that would let a sixth be generated. The legitimate neighbour is a built language whose names obey their own phonology

### "Everybody in this addresses everybody else the same way"

1. Establish which system of titles the world runs on — professional, religious, age-based, teknonymic, inherited — and be complete about it, because one correctly used title places a character's age, class, region, faith and era at once
   `char.honorific_system`
2. Then characterise by *deviation*: the person who insists on a title they have not earned, the one who refuses one they have, the young man who uses the honorific to a man he is about to rob
3. Where the society runs on family terms for people who are not family — uncle, aunt, brother, mother — treat the term as a *claim* rather than a courtesy, and let the granting and the withholding of it carry the status. Dropping a kinship term for a bare first name is a rupture everyone present hears, in any language
   `char.kinship_address`
4. Never gloss either of them in the mouth. A character who explains what the word means is talking to the audience; translate it in the subtitle or leave it
   `expo.heritage_specificity` · `dial.untranslated_line`
5. Verify the usage for that exact community and period. An honorific from the wrong denomination, region or century is the error native speakers spot first and forgive least, and getting the garments right while getting the titles wrong is the standing signature of research done from pictures

### "He has two names and the script only ever uses one"

1. Put both into live circulation and decide *who is still entitled to the old one* — a mother, a first employer, a police file. That list is the character's real map of relationships
   `char.changed_name`
2. Make the change a decision with a reason and a price, not a shrug. The man who became Joe at twenty-three because a foreman could not be bothered has a different injury from the woman who chose the new name herself
3. Stage the collision exactly once, in a room where both parties are present, and never let the character deliver the history — the name does it
4. Which name is used in a given scene is the scene's subtext instrument
   `sub.address_form`
5. The failure mode is the reader being told the real name in chapter one and never hearing it again, which spends the best card in the deck on an announcement

### "Writing across a culture I am not from"

1. Specificity, not representation — one particular person, not a category
   `char.specificity_not_representation` · `char.inside_outside_culture`
2. For diaspora characters, the two places are both present at once
   `char.diaspora_character`
3. Narrow the claim to what you can actually know — the encounter and the system, not the interior life of the community
   `theme.from_outside`
4. If it is your own community, membership buys the hard claim and does not remove the second audience: insiders read an argument within the family, outsiders read evidence about it
   `theme.from_inside` · `sub.dual_audience`

### "The community in my story is there to teach the protagonist something"

1. The named failure, and the tell is always the same — wisdom and no interests, dignity and no arguments
   `theme.culture_as_lesson`
2. Two tests, both of which must pass: is there a scene where they disagree with each other about something internal, with no outsider present; and do they want something the protagonist's arrival makes harder
   `theme.culture_as_lesson`
3. The repair is a want of their own that predates the protagonist and continues after them
   `theme.from_outside` · `char.specificity_not_representation`

### "He can't decide this on his own and the script treats it as his decision"

The story he holds belongs to a family, a village, a congregation or a people, so his testimony is not his alone to give. `char.speaks_for_others` is largely a **prohibition** card — much of its content is what may not be written — so open it before the scene rather than after.

1. Write who has to be asked, by name and relation, and write what asking costs. The obligation is never abstract: it is a specific uncle, a specific priest, a woman in Chicago who has not spoken to him since 2009
   `char.speaks_for_others`
2. Give him an opinion of his own and make it differ from the community's, or you have written `char.mouthpiece` with better manners. The drama is a person who wants to tell you and may not
3. Write the withdrawal as well as the grant. Collective consent is revocable in a way individual consent is not, because the community that gave it can change its mind through people who were never in the room, and a character who knows that is watchful in a way that reads on the page
4. Let the refusal be reasoned rather than mystical. Somebody who says *that is not for outsiders* and can say exactly who was harmed the last time it was told is a person; one who says it and cannot is decoration
5. The two failure modes sit on either side: the community written as unanimous, and the community written only as a cage to escape
   `char.inside_outside_culture` · `char.community_built`
6. And if the obligation can only appear as a line of dialogue explaining that he must consult his elders, cut it and write a different constraint. A named obligation with no visible cost is exoticism, and it is the version outsiders write
7. The conversation itself, on the film side, is a separate complaint in the commissioned section
   `scene.reconsent_scene`

### "She's fluent and funny in her own language and flat in mine"

Audiences read halting speech as limited intelligence. Unless you correct that default once, everything the audience sees of her is a reduction they will mistake for the person.

1. The structural requirement, without which the card does not work: show her at full size at least once, **early**, in her own language and fully subtitled — one scene where she is the fastest person in the room. Everything afterwards is read against it
   `char.smaller_in_this_language`
2. Never write her second-language speech as comic, and never as broken grammar transcribed for texture. Adult second-language speakers are usually precise and short rather than mangled, and the vocabulary they reach for is a level formal
   `dial.second_language_rhythm` · `voice.second_language_voice`
3. Write what she cannot say rather than what she says wrong: the joke abandoned mid-sentence, the correction she chooses not to make, the exact word she knows exists and does not have
4. Give the reduction a witness who has heard the full-size version — a child, a colleague — because their embarrassment carries what the scene cannot state
5. The two-place condition itself, the scene conducted in the second language, and the line deliberately withheld from translation
   `char.diaspora_character` · `scene.second_language_scene` · `scene.untranslated_line`
6. If the runtime cannot hold the full-size scene, the technique inverts: you have written a character who is simply less than everyone around her, which is the stereotype the card exists to defeat. Give the reduction to somebody the audience already knows well from elsewhere instead

### "The character's faith is a costume"

1. Write the believer as someone who is right about something — not sincere, not sympathetic, right
   `char.writing_the_believer`
2. Then decide deliberately which side of the line the piece stands on: what it may argue about — the institution, the practice, the history — and what its audience has placed outside argument
   `theme.sacred_material`
3. For the register itself
   `voice.liturgical_register`

### "The character has to work in a very short or very long form"

1. Ten seconds is enough for a person if the first action decides something
   `char.ten_second_person` · `char.vertical_archetype`
2. Across eighty episodes, some characters must be protected from development
   `char.long_series_character` · `char.protected_static_role`

### "The character is a device"

1. A mouthpiece for the writer's position
   `char.mouthpiece`
2. One who only reacts
   `char.only_reacts`
3. A trait presented as a character
   `char.trait_not_character`
4. An attribute the story asserts and never shows
   `char.informed_attribute`

### "The gods in this are just powerful people"

1. Two things make a god playable, and neither is power. A *jurisdiction* — what they are the god of, and therefore what they may not touch — and a *lack*, the one thing their power cannot obtain: attention, grief, being remembered, being contradicted
   `char.god_character`
2. Write their scale in time rather than in force. A being who has seen the city three times reads as divine; one who throws lightning reads as a superhero
3. Give them one small bad habit, because personality descends from appetite
4. The failure mode is the committee of gods — five deities with one speech register and no jurisdictions, quarrelling like a board of directors
5. Where you are dramatising a living faith rather than a mythology, the obligations are different and they are held elsewhere
   `theme.sacred_material` · `char.writing_the_believer`

### "Why can the rabbit drive a car and the bear hibernate?"

1. Because the world has no species logic, and it is fixed once, before any character is designed. Decide the predation question first — it silently determines whether the world can have jeopardy at all
   `char.species_logic`
2. Give each species one capability and one cost, and cast so the abilities do not overlap: if the bird can fly, nobody else may, or flight stops being a solution
3. Keep the species trait working *against* the character's want. The owl who cannot stay awake is a character; the wise owl is furniture
4. Establish the rule by demonstration in the first episode, never by a line of explanation, and then hold it across the whole season, because a preschool audience is on its eleventh viewing and knows
   `expo.rule_by_demonstration`
5. For realistic animals this is the wrong card and the subject is ecology
   `char.animal_character`

### "The house is described beautifully and does nothing"

1. Build it as a character sheet rather than as description. Give the place a want stated as a tendency — the house wants to be empty, the town wants nothing to change, the river wants its old course — and then obey it, so it behaves the same way in scene 4 and scene 40
   `char.personified_place`
2. Assign it a body: the two or three physical facts it always asserts, the door that does not close, the room that is always cold
3. The moment it reads as an agent rather than as weather is the moment it treats two characters differently
4. Do not give it a voice unless the form asks for one — narration by a place is a separate instrument
   `voice.voice_of_a_place`
5. If what you actually have is a system with rules, that is the institution card, not this one
   `anta.institutional`

### "I can't tell the twins apart on the page"

1. Differentiate on *tempo and tactics*, never on personality — tempo survives a costume change and reads in silhouette. One interrupts and one waits; one arrives early and one arrives late
   `char.twins`
2. Give them different relationships to the same third person. That is where the difference becomes dramatic instead of decorative
3. Decide early whether the audience is ever confused, and if so, exactly twice and on purpose. Then break the pair: the useful scene is always the one where they are apart and each is a slightly worse version of themselves
4. The failure is the opposites build — the wild one and the careful one — which is a single character cut in half, and the audience recombines them inside twenty minutes
5. If the second figure is not a sibling but the life the protagonist might have had, it is a different card with a harder requirement
   `char.the_double`

### "He's undercover and the cover costs him nothing"

1. Write the *labour*: the rehearsed biography, the second phone, the accent maintained while exhausted, the relative who must never visit. Cover is work, and the work is the character
   `char.assumed_identity`
2. Fix the leak. Decide the one thing the cover cannot survive and put it in the room early, then schedule the fraying so the audience sees it before any character does
3. Give him something real inside the cover — a friendship, a competence, a place he is happier — because the documented hazard of long cover is not exposure but attachment
4. The failure mode is the character who lies fluently, is never nearly caught, is never tempted by the life, and drops the identity at the end without residue: nothing was risked and the strand was a delay
5. Do not run it in a piece too short to show maintenance, where a disguise is a costume change and the audience simply waits for it to fall off

### "The antagonist turns out to be his double and it explains everything"

1. That is `char.evil_twin`. Two tests. Ask what the double believes and why they are right about something — if the answers are "nothing" and "they are not", you have an inverted copy rather than a character, which is the antagonist's case never having been written
   `char.antagonist_case`
2. Then ask what the protagonist learns about himself that only this person could teach him. If any other antagonist could teach it, the duplication is decorative
3. The two honest repairs: a double built on the *same want* and a different method, so the hero is tempted rather than merely threatened; or an opponent built out of this specific hero's lie
   `char.the_double` · `char.tailored_opponent`
4. The worst version is the third-act twin, introduced after the midpoint to explain an earlier inconsistency, which retroactively cancels every scene the audience had been reading carefully
5. Two real exceptions: declared genre play, and the doubled performance as a craft object where one actor playing two is the point. Both still require the second character to want something the first does not

### "The child / elderly character sounds wrong"

1. Write from that age's own logic and stakes, not from an adult's memory of it
   `char.age_specific_logic`

### "I'm writing about real people"

1. Composite, displace, and know what a name change does and does not cure
   `char.real_person_composite`
2. Note: that card states plainly that nothing in this database is legal advice
3. Where the real person is the *opposition*, work from the record — the zone that fails is the mixture: recognisable enough to identify, invented enough to defame, plausible enough to be believed
   `anta.real_person_opposition`
4. If the piece claims the person *changed*, that is not a design decision but an assertion about a life. It has to be found in the material, stated at the strength the evidence supports, and left incomplete wherever the evidence is
   `arc.real_person_arc`
5. The named failure is the third act nobody had — a reckoning that did not occur, a reconciliation that was not made, a moral failure smoothed into a stage somebody moved through — reached for because the material refused to produce one. Compressing *how* a person got somewhere is a convention of the form; inventing that they arrived is not. Its mirror is the same offence: flattening someone who did change into someone who could not, because the film prefers them tragic
   `arc.imposed_arc`
6. And do not start one on a subject who is alive, close and still inside the change. An arc needs an end position, and a person mid-trial, mid-recovery or mid-grief has not got one — restructure around a question instead of a curve
7. Where the person is single, living, named and cannot be merged with anybody, the narrower card carries three working rules: write only what you can source, because negative characterisation you can produce evidence for is both defensible and better writing — *he was forty minutes late to his mother's funeral* against *he was a selfish man*; give them their own case at its strongest, so that they could say "that is not why I did it, but that is what I did"; and decide what you will not cut *before* you speak to them, because the conversation is not the place to discover your own limit
   `char.living_subject_reader`
8. The failure that shadows it is `char.softened_for_the_reader` — the portrait written as though the subject were already reading over your shoulder, hedged and pre-excused into having no edges. The test is countable: find every sentence about them containing a concessive clause — *though of course*, *to be fair*, *in his defence*, *it was a different time* — and count them. One or two placed deliberately is a live portrait; eight, clustered on one incident, marks the incident you do not want to have to defend. Second test: hand the passage to somebody who does not know the person and ask what the subject did
9. The repair is not to become harsher. Replace each concession with the evidence for it, which either survives — and then it belonged there — or does not, and then you have found the sentence you were avoiding
10. The genuine exception is a duty of care rather than a fear: a vulnerable contributor, a child, a person endangered by the detail. The honest form of that is to withhold the material and say in the text that you have, not to keep it and blunt it

### "I can hear the writer casting the part"

1. Write the line that would make an actor want it — not the line that describes the character
   `char.casting_line`
2. If there is a real attachment, that is a lever rather than a problem: take the performer's demonstrated instrument — the pause, the speed, the flatness, the stillness — and give them scenes that need it, while writing the role so it still stands up if they fall out, because attachments collapse and a script welded to one body is unsellable
   `char.write_for_known_actor`
3. Writing *against* a known performer is the stronger move and it is paid for with behaviour, not with an announcement: one early scene where the familiar register appears and is then withheld
4. Never write the performer's known catchphrase or signature moment. It reads as fan service and it is the note a good actor gives back first
5. The failure is dialogue written in the actor's voice rather than the character's, which is the uniformity fault with a more famous author
   `dial.writer_voice_uniformity`

### "The actor isn't an actor"

`char.written_for_a_non_actor` is a **prohibition** card — most of its content is what may not be written — so read it before you draft the part rather than after, because every repair in it is structural and none is a polish.

1. What you may not write. No subtext that requires a performance: a non-actor can *say* a line and cannot *hold* a second meaning underneath it, so the second meaning has to live in the situation or in another character's face. No line whose sense depends on which word is stressed — write the emphasis as word order. No interiority that needs a reaction shot, because a trained actor can think visibly and a first-timer looks like somebody waiting. And never a lie for them to tell, since performing a false surface is precisely the skill they do not have
   `char.written_for_a_non_actor`
2. What works instead: give them a task, because doing is the one thing a non-actor does better than a professional; write in their own register and ideally from their own words, since a line they would never say arrives as recitation; keep every speech under three sentences; and put the difficult beat on a professional so the non-actor can answer it in four words
   `char.hands_test` · `char.idiolect_sheet`
3. Verify by having them read it aloud once, cold. The lines they smooth out on their own are the ones to keep
4. The role carrying the story's deception, its reversal, or a change of mind that must be legible in one take needs a trained actor. The honest move is to restructure so the non-actor holds the part of the story that is true and present
5. Do not write the whole cast this way out of loyalty to the style. That produces a film in which nobody can escalate
6. The inverse problem — a performer attached and the part bending toward them — is the complaint above
   `char.write_for_known_actor`

### "The woman I cast for her face is saying the film's argument"

1. That is `char.coached_authenticity` — casting a real person from a community for the authority their presence carries, then writing them the sentence the film needs said, so the authenticity the casting bought is spent on a line that person would never have produced. It is `char.mouthpiece` with a real person's authority attached, which makes it worse rather than milder
2. The effect is the reverse of the intention. Viewers from that community hear the seam instantly, because they know what the register should be and the line is one register up; everyone else registers a performance that has gone slightly wooden and cannot say why
3. The test is quick. Take every line a non-professional or community cast member speaks and ask who wrote it: if the sentence is doing a job in the *film's* argument rather than in the character's afternoon, it is this
4. The repair is not to cut the person, it is to cut the sentence and give them an action that makes the same point. Where a thesis genuinely has to be stated, give it to a character the audience is free to disbelieve
5. The one honest neighbour is a person speaking their own words, gathered in advance and given back to them verbatim, which is a different technique with a different name. If you wrote the sentence, it is not that
   `scene.transcript_built`

### "The part keeps being turned down and nobody says why"

1. Learn how the role is read before it reaches the actor. Count the scenes, and the pages between first and last appearance — eleven scenes spread over ninety pages is a schedule problem and will be declined for reasons nobody mentions
   `char.agent_read`
2. Give the role at least one scene where the character changes tactics on camera. That is what an actor reads for, and a part with one note is a part with no offer
3. Make the character the engine of two scenes rather than the recipient of six. A supporting role that is hard to cast is almost never underwritten in length; it is underwritten in agency
   `char.only_reacts` · `char.casting_line`
4. What does not work is the big speech added as bait. Actors read it as bait, and a monologue is not the same as a part

### "We've recast and the audience has to accept it"

1. Separate the behaviour from the performance. The tactics, prohibitions and relationships are the character and must be reproduced exactly; the timing, physical business and vocal music belonged to the previous actor and must be released, because a new performer doing an impression is worse than a new performer
   `char.recasting`
2. Give the new actor one scene early with high behavioural density and no emotional demand — the character doing three characteristic things while the plot does nothing. That is what lets an audience re-anchor
3. Decide the acknowledgement question once and hold it: silent replacement is standard and works, in-fiction explanation is expensive, and the comic acknowledgement is a one-off that spends the audience's belief
4. Do not over-write the first episode. Three references to how different they seem tells the audience to look at the thing you wanted them to stop looking at
5. Where the character is being retired rather than replaced, the card is a different one
   `end.retiring_a_character`

### "I know everything about her and none of it is in the script"

1. That is `char.character_dossier`, and the test is a ratio: count the pages of development against the pages of draft, and past about one to ten the documentation has become the project. The second test is per fact — name the scene it changes. A fact that changes no scene is knowledge about a document
2. Three procedures survive that audit, and they are procedures rather than repairs. The **interview**, run aloud, at speed, for twenty minutes, asking only about behaviour and objects — what is in the drawer, what did you do on the last day of that job, who did you not ring — and asking questions the character has a reason to dodge, because the evasions are the whole yield. Harvest exactly three things: one line of speech you would not have written, one refusal, one physical fact. Everything else is compost
   `char.character_interview`
3. The **want/need sheet**, one page, a row per principal and two columns, both specific and physical — and the need written as the thing they would have to give up the want to get. Fill it for the antagonist too. If any principal can have both columns at the end, the sheet has just told you the ending is unearned
   `char.want_need_sheet` · `arc.the_want` · `arc.the_need`
4. The **audition scene**, which is the only development exercise that produces actual writing: put the character in a situation outside the story's world and register — the crime lord at a parents' evening, the grieving widow returning a faulty kettle — and read for two things only, whether you knew what they would do, and whether the lines could belong to anyone else in the cast. It is also the fastest fix for a character who is fine in their own scenes and vanishes in everybody else's
   `char.audition_scene`
5. Do not keep it. The audition scene is usually charming, has nothing to do with the story, and survives four drafts before somebody with no affection for it asks what it is doing there
6. The genuine exception to the whole diagnosis is a multi-writer production, where documentation is infrastructure rather than avoidance — the distinguishing feature being that somebody other than the author will read it
   `char.bible_entry`
