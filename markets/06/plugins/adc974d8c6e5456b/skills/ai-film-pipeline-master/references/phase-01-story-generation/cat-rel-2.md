## Distance, Absence and the Barrier

### The Reunion After Long Separation

**Also called:** the return, years later, the homecoming meeting
**What it is:** The scene where two people who have been apart for years meet again, written as a collision between two obsolete models — each arrives holding a version of the other that stopped updating on the day they parted.
**Effect on the audience:** A very particular ache: they can see both wrong models at once, and they watch each person discover, line by line, that the person in front of them has been living a life without them in it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Migration stories, prison releases, estranged families, war returns, the second half of any story with a gap in it. The craft rule: never let the reunion be about the reunion. Give the scene a mundane practical task — a suitcase, a form, a meal that needs cooking, a lift from the station — and play the entire reconnection inside the logistics, because two people who have not spoken in nine years cannot bear to look at each other with nothing in their hands. Second rule, from the migration research: the returning person has been an absent presence, and the household reorganised itself around the hole. Show the reorganisation before the return, so the audience knows exactly which chair no longer exists. Third: have each of them get one fact about the other wrong out loud, and have the correction be small and devastating — a job they no longer do, a name they no longer use.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Stage Play, Short Story, Memoir | genres: Drama, Family Drama, Migration, Literary, War, Romance
**Avoid when:** the audience has not experienced the separation as a cost. If the gap was a title card, the reunion is two strangers being polite, and no amount of underscoring will convert information into feeling.
**Example:**
> "Is that everything?"
> "That's everything."
> "It's small."
> "You said not to bring much."
> "I said that about the flat. I meant — the flat was smaller then." She takes the bag. It is much lighter than she braced for, and she nearly swings it into the wall.

**Yields to:** `rel.bond_across_a_time_jump` — The gap was never paid for.  
`rel.reunion_after_separation`

### The Correspondence Relationship

**Also called:** the epistolary bond, the texting relationship, the long-distance thread
**What it is:** A relationship conducted entirely through a channel — letters, email, text, voice notes, phone — where the writer never gets the other person's face, and every gap in the exchange is readable as a message.
**Effect on the audience:** They become a forensic reader. With no body language available, they start weighing sentence length, punctuation and reply delay exactly as the characters do, which makes a three-word message an event.
**Used for and where it works best:** Migration and diaspora stories, wartime, prison, long-distance romance, any bond that must survive across a border. The craft rule: the channel must have a cost or a limit — postage, a censor, a phone card, one bar of signal, a shared family handset — because an unlimited channel produces unlimited talk and unlimited talk has no shape. Second rule, the one most drafts miss: **the silence is the plot.** Design the gaps first — who stops replying, for how long, and what the other one does in the gap — then write the messages around them. Third: each person must write in a different register, or the thread reads as one voice arguing with itself; see `voice.the_letter_voice` and `dial.voice_note`. Never let a message be a scene summary — a letter is an argument the writer is making about themselves.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Serialised Fiction, Short Story, Limited Series, Audio Drama, Stage Play | genres: Romance, Drama, Migration, Literary, War, Coming of Age
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2028-09. The letter half is permanent; the messaging half is not. Read receipts, typing indicators, disappearing messages and voice notes are all load-bearing in current drafts and all platform-specific, and a story whose central turn is "she saw it and didn't reply" is pinned to a UI that changes.
**Avoid when:** the pair needs a physical scene at the climax and you have not built one. A correspondence story that ends with a meeting has to pay for that meeting with an entire structure; if they simply meet and it is fine, you have spent the whole book on an obstacle you then waved away.
**Example:**
> 14 March. "Tell me one ordinary thing. Not the news. An ordinary thing."
> 16 March. "The man downstairs has started singing again."
> 2 June. "Is he still singing?"
> 2 June. "Who"
> 2 June. "The man downstairs."
> 2 June. "I moved in April. I told you I moved."

**Yields to:** `rel.reunion_after_separation` — The climax must be physical.  
`rel.correspondence_bond`

### The Bond With the Dead

**Also called:** the continuing bond, the ongoing relationship with the deceased, the absent party
**What it is:** A relationship that keeps running after one party dies — the living person still consults, argues with, performs for and hides from someone who cannot answer, and the bond continues to change.
**Effect on the audience:** They stop reading grief as a subtraction and start reading it as a live relationship with one silent participant, which is both truer and far more dramatic than mourning.
**Used for and where it works best:** Anywhere loss is the subject, and heritage and testimony work above all. The craft rule comes out of the continuing-bonds model in bereavement research: the dead person is not a memory, they are a **standing opinion**. Give the living character a specific thing the dead one would have said, in the dead one's own syntax, and let the character act on it, resent it, or get it deliberately wrong. Second rule: the bond must move. Write four positions — the dead person as authority, as accuser, as someone the character argues back to, and finally as someone who can be disagreed with and kept — and stage one scene at each. Third: route it through an object or a task, not a graveside speech. A coat that still gets taken to the dry cleaner's is a relationship; a monologue at a headstone is a eulogy. Distinct from `emo.grief`, which is the feeling; this is the bond, and a bond has terms.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Stage Play, Short Story, Memoir, Heritage Documentary | genres: Drama, Family Drama, Literary, Heritage, Faith, Testimony
**Avoid when:** you are using the dead to settle a living argument. If the deceased always turns out to have been right, they are not a relationship, they are the author with an unfair advantage, and the audience will resent being overruled by someone who cannot be cross-examined.
**Example:**
> "He'd have said leave it."
> "He'd have said leave it because he never once in his life rang anybody first."
> "He'd still have said it."
> "I know." She rings. It takes her the whole length of the corridor to do it, and she is arguing the entire way with a man who has been dead fourteen months.

**Yields to:** `emo.grief` — You want the feeling, not a live bond.  
`rel.bond_with_the_dead`

### The Relationship Across a Language

**Also called:** the language barrier bond, the bilingual pair, love in a second tongue
**What it is:** Two people who do not share a first language and must build a relationship out of a reduced vocabulary, a third language, gesture, or an interpreter — so the bond is made of what can be said, not what is meant.
**Effect on the audience:** Enormous subtext for free. Every exchange is visibly missing something, and the audience supplies the missing part, which makes them feel complicit in the intimacy.
**Used for and where it works best:** Migration, war, medical and legal settings, heritage work, any romance across a border. The craft rules: give each person a different competence profile, not a shared level — one understands far more than they can say, and that asymmetry is the whole engine, because the person who understands is always ahead and always silent. Second: reduced language is not childish language. A fluent adult in a second tongue is precise, formal and slightly archaic, never cute; write them with short clean declaratives and no idiom rather than with broken grammar, and avoid `dial.phonetic_accent_spelling` entirely. Third: when a third party interprets, the interpreter has loyalties — the research on interpreter-mediated consultations is blunt that trust in the interpreter, not in the other speaker, is what the relationship actually runs on. `dial.interpreter_scene` is the scene-level move, `dial.untranslated_line` the line-level one; this is the sustained bond.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Stage Play, Short Story, Documentary | genres: Migration, Drama, Romance, War, Literary, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** the plot needs precise verbal information to pass between exactly these two people. Route it through a document or a third party instead, or you will spend a page manufacturing comprehension and lose the barrier you built.
**Example:**
> "You are angry."
> "I'm not angry."
> "You are angry. I know this word. I do not know the other words, but I know this word."
> "...Yes."
> "Good. Now say the other words. I will not understand, but say them."

**Yields to:** `rel.bond_through_a_go_between` — Precise information must pass between them.  
`rel.across_a_language`

### The Pair Separated by Class

**Also called:** the cross-class bond, the money gap, upstairs and downstairs
**What it is:** Two people whose relationship is continuously mediated by unequal money, education and entitlement — where the gap shows up not in argument but in small automatic behaviours neither of them chose.
**Effect on the audience:** A recognition they usually cannot name, because class in fiction is far more often felt than stated. Done well it produces a low, permanent unease that no reconciliation scene can clear.
**Used for and where it works best:** British and American social drama, romance, workplace stories, family sagas where one sibling left. The craft rules: write class as **reflex, not opinion** — who asks the price, who sends food back, who assumes a room can be booked, who knows what a form is for, who apologises to staff. Second: give the lower-status person competence the higher-status one lacks and visibly needs, or the pair becomes charity and the story becomes condescension. Third, and this is where most drafts fail: the wealthier character must not be written as the one with the problem. The cross-class scenes that land hardest are the ones where the richer person is kind, generous and completely unable to see the cost of their own generosity. Pairs naturally with `char.money_specifics` and with `rel.group_hierarchy_display` when the gap is staged in a room.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Limited Series, Stage Play, One-Hour Drama, Short Story | genres: Social Drama, Drama, Romance, Literary, Coming of Age, Family Drama
**Avoid when:** the story's argument is that love transcends circumstance. It cannot, and audiences in a cost-of-living decade know it cannot; the ending will read as a fantasy you did not signal you were writing.
**Example:**
> "Just get a cab, I'll send you the money."
> "It's fine, the bus goes right past."
> "It's forty minutes."
> "It's forty minutes on the bus or it's forty minutes of you knowing you paid for it. I'll take the bus."

### The Relationship That Only Works in One Place

**Also called:** the location-bound bond, the holiday friendship, the pair who cannot travel
**What it is:** A relationship that is fully alive inside one specific setting — a ward, a shift, a boat, a village, a summer — and which cannot be moved, so the story's real threat is not conflict but relocation.
**Effect on the audience:** Anticipatory grief. From the moment they understand the rule, every scene in the place is shadowed by the certainty that the place will end.
**Used for and where it works best:** Holiday stories, hospital and military settings, school years, seasonal work, anything with a fixed term. The craft rule: prove the rule once, early and cheaply, by taking the pair one street outside the boundary and letting them run out of things to say within four lines. That single failed excursion tells the audience everything and costs you half a page. Second rule: neither character may name the rule; the moment someone says "we're only like this here," the device becomes a theme and stops being a dread. Third: end inside the place, not outside it. A last scene in a car park is an epilogue about a relationship that has already died offstage.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Short Story, Stage Play, Audio Drama | genres: Drama, Romance, Coming of Age, Literary, Medical, War
**Avoid when:** the pair is your through-line for a long series. A location-bound bond has a fixed expiry, and a returning show will eventually have to either move it and break it, or never leave the room.
**Example:**
> Inside, on the ward, three in the morning: forty minutes of the worst and funniest conversation either of them has had all year.
> Outside, in the car park, in daylight: "So. You're off, then." / "I'm off." / "Right." / "Right." / Eleven seconds. Then a handshake, which neither of them has ever done.

**Yields to:** `rel.professional_pair` — Pair must carry a long-running series.  
`rel.location_bound_bond`

### The Bond Across a Time Jump

**Also called:** the relationship after the gap, the skipped years, the two-timeline pair
**What it is:** Carrying a specific relationship across a deliberate jump of months or years, so the audience is handed the new state of the bond and has to reconstruct the missing middle from evidence.
**Effect on the audience:** Active reading. They arrive on the far side of the jump and immediately start auditing — who is standing where, who uses which name, what is now normal — and the reconstruction is more involving than the omitted scenes would have been.
**Used for and where it works best:** Season breaks, second halves, sagas, any story that needs the cost of time without the expense of dramatising it. The craft rule: land the jump on a **changed rule, not a changed fact.** Do not open the new timeline on "they are divorced now"; open it on one of them automatically doing something the old version never did — knocking before entering, using the full first name, taking the second-best chair. Second rule: leave exactly one thing unchanged and put it in the first scene after the jump. The single surviving habit is what makes the audience feel the passage of time rather than be told about it. Use with `time.time_jump` and `time.ellipsis`; the difference is that those handle the story's clock and this handles what the clock did to two specific people.
**Best in:** formats: Series, Limited Series, Novel, Feature Film, Serialised Fiction, One-Hour Drama | genres: Drama, Family Drama, Crime, Literary, Science Fiction, Historical
**Avoid when:** the relationship's most important change happened inside the gap. You cannot skip the turn and then report it; the audience will feel robbed of the one scene the whole line was built toward.
**Example:**
> Before: he lets himself in, shouts from the hall, opens the fridge.
> After (four years): he knocks. She opens it. "You don't have to knock." / "I know." He does not come in until she stands aside. But he still opens the fridge, and she still says nothing about it.

**Yields to:** `rel.relationship_arc` — The key change happened inside the gap.  
`rel.bond_across_a_time_jump`

## Care, Obligation and the Third Party

### The Caretaker and the Cared-For

**Also called:** the carer bond, nursing the beloved, the dependency pair
**What it is:** A relationship reorganised around one person's body or mind needing another person's daily labour, in which love and resentment are carried by the same tasks.
**Effect on the audience:** Unglamorous recognition. This is the relationship most readers have lived and most fiction gets wrong, so specificity here buys more trust than almost any other material.
**Used for and where it works best:** Illness stories, ageing parents, disability narratives, war injury, addiction. The craft rules, drawn from care-work writing: **write the exact labour** — not "she looked after him" but the crushed pills, the second sheet, the appointment letter, the sixteen minutes on hold. Second: name who controls each decision, and be specific, because the real drama of care is autonomy, not effort — the cared-for person keeps their own goals, refuses things, and is allowed to be wrong. Third: the person receiving care must give something back that is not gratitude, or you have written a saint and a burden instead of two people. Fourth: let the arrangement change through a choice someone makes, not through the illness progressing — an illness is a plot, a decision is a story. `rel.parent_and_adult_child` is the usual precursor; the turn between them is the moment the chore changes hands.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Stage Play, Limited Series, Memoir, Short Story | genres: Drama, Family Drama, Medical, Literary, Social Drama, Addiction
**Avoid when:** you intend the cared-for person as a lesson for the carer. Using a disabled or dying character as somebody else's arc is the oldest failure in this material and is legible instantly to anyone who has been either party.
**Example:**
> "I can do the buttons."
> "I know you can do the buttons."
> "Then why are you standing there?"
> "Because last time you did the buttons we missed the bus and you didn't speak to me till Tuesday."
> "Then stand there quietly."
> She stands there quietly. It takes four minutes. They make the bus.

### The Pair With a Shared Child

**Also called:** co-parents, the permanent ex, the custody bond
**What it is:** Two people whose romantic or family relationship has ended but whose logistical relationship cannot, because a third person requires them to keep negotiating for years.
**Effect on the audience:** Low-grade, endlessly renewable tension. Every handover is a scene, and none of them can be walked out of.
**Used for and where it works best:** Contemporary drama, series above all, because it generates conflict weekly without new plot. The craft rule: play every scene through **scheduling, money and objects** — the returned coat, the fifteen minutes late, the £40, the school form that needs two signatures. Never let them discuss the marriage; let them discuss the Thursday. Second rule: the child must want something of their own, or they become a prop passed between two adults, which is both bad craft and the thing the story is supposedly criticising. Third: give them one shared pleasure that survives — a joke about the child, a shared exasperation at a teacher — because a co-parent pair with nothing left is just an argument. This is `rel.locked_pair` with a specific and very durable lock — but the lock binds them to the logistics, not to each other, so a reunion is still a choice the lock does not make for them, which is why this pair can carry romantic hope where `rel.locked_pair` cannot.
**Best in:** formats: Series, One-Hour Drama, Feature Film, Novel, Half-Hour Comedy, Stage Play | genres: Family Drama, Drama, Comedy, Social Drama, Romantic Comedy, Crime
**Avoid when:** you want the audience to hope they reunite and you have also written them as competent co-parents with nothing outside the arrangement. Functional co-parenting resolves the logistical bond, and audiences stop wishing for the marriage once the arrangement works; a reunion has to be built as a new, freely chosen relationship outside the child, the schedule and the custody lock.
**Example:**
> "She needs the reading folder back by Monday."
> "It's in the blue bag."
> "It's not in the blue bag."
> "Then it's at yours."
> "It's not at mine, because I packed the blue bag, because I always pack the blue bag."
> A pause. "It's in my car." / "Right." / "I'll drop it." / "Don't come in." / "I wasn't going to come in."

### The Institutional Relationship

**Also called:** the person and the organisation, loyalty to the firm, the bond with the army
**What it is:** A relationship between one person and an institution — employer, church, regiment, party, club, family firm — written with the institution as a full participant that promises, demands, rewards and betrays.
**Effect on the audience:** They recognise a bond most fiction refuses to name as a bond, and they feel the specific horror of being left by something that was never capable of loving them.
**Used for and where it works best:** Workplace drama, faith stories, military and police work, political fiction, heritage material about a vanished trade or institution. The craft rules: give the institution a **voice with a register** — the email, the form letter, the liturgy, the standing order — and let that register do what dialogue does; `voice.institutional_voice` and `voice.bureaucratic_register` are the tools. Second: personify it in exactly one human who both represents it and is subject to it, so the protagonist can have a scene with the institution that is also a scene with a person. Third: the institution must have given something real, early. An organisation that was always cold produces a grievance; one that genuinely raised, fed and named someone produces a tragedy. `anta.institutional` handles the institution as opponent; this handles it as an attachment.
**Best in:** formats: Limited Series, Novel, Feature Film, Stage Play, One-Hour Drama, Long-Form Non-Fiction | genres: Workplace, Political, Faith, War, Social Drama, Drama
**Avoid when:** the piece has a single named villain who runs the place. The moment the institution has an author, it stops being an institution and becomes a boss, and the particular dread of facelessness is gone.
**Example:**
> "Thirty-one years."
> "I know, and that is genuinely reflected in the package."
> "I built the Selby line."
> "And that's in the letter. Have you read the letter? Because there's a paragraph about Selby, and I wrote it, and I meant it."
> "Who signed it?"
> He turns it over. Nobody signed it. It has a printed signature and a reference number.

**Yields to:** `anta.institutional` — The institution opposes rather than attaches.  
`rel.institutional_bond`

### The Parasocial Bond

**Also called:** the one-sided attachment, the fan and the figure, the stranger who raised me
**What it is:** A relationship one person experiences fully and the other does not know exists — with a performer, a streamer, an author, a public figure, a dead relative they never met — which nonetheless shapes decisions, language and identity.
**Effect on the audience:** Sharp discomfort, because almost everyone reading has one, and fiction that names it accurately is read as an accusation before it is read as a story.
**Used for and where it works best:** Contemporary drama, satire, horror, coming of age, anything about attention economies. The craft rule: make the bond **functional, not pathetic.** Horton and Wohl's original point, and the current research, is that these attachments often work — they reduce loneliness and lead to real friendships — so write what it genuinely supplies first, and only then the cost. A story that opens on ridicule has nowhere to go. Second rule: the asymmetry must be dramatised once, physically, in a meeting — the figure is warm, professional, and does not retain the encounter, and that is worse than cruelty. Third: give the figure their own small, ordinary want inside the scene (to get to the car, to not be photographed eating), because a public figure written only as a surface is `char.mouthpiece` in a wig.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Novel, Short Story, Web Series, Stage Play | genres: Drama, Satire, Psychological, Coming of Age, Horror, Social Drama
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2028-09. The structure is permanent, the surface is not. Streaming, superchats, DMs, AI companions and algorithmic "day in my life" feeds each change what the fan can do, how close they can get, and whether the figure could plausibly reply — and a plot turning on whether a message was seen depends entirely on the platform of the year.
**Avoid when:** the story needs the figure to reciprocate for the ending to work. The instant the bond becomes mutual it is an ordinary relationship, and everything the parasocial frame bought you is spent.
**Example:**
> "I've been listening since the Leeds episode. The one about your dad."
> "Oh, thank you, that's — thank you." He is already signing. He has a warm, entirely genuine smile that he has now given four hundred times today.
> "It got me through the year."
> "That's really lovely to hear." Beat. "Sorry — was it to Dan, or Daniel?"

**Yields to:** `rel.asymmetric_investment` — The figure has to reciprocate.  
`rel.parasocial_bond`

### The Relationship Conducted Through a Third Person

**Also called:** the go-between, the messenger bond, talking through the child
**What it is:** Two people who will not or cannot address each other directly, so every exchange passes through a third party — a child, an interpreter, a lawyer, a mutual friend, an assistant — who edits it on the way.
**Effect on the audience:** They read three relationships in one exchange, and they watch the go-between's editing choices become the actual plot.
**Used for and where it works best:** Feuding families, divorce, hostage and negotiation stories, migration, courtly and high-context settings. The craft rule: the go-between must have their own stake and must **change the message** — softening, sharpening, omitting — and the audience must see both versions, or the device is a delivery mechanism rather than a relationship. Second rule: the go-between pays the cost. Being the channel is exhausting and corrupting, and the interpreter research is clear that the trust in the room lands on the middle person, not on either principal. Third: the turn is always the same and always works — the go-between refuses, and the two principals are left with no channel at all. Adjacent: `dial.talking_to_the_room` and `dial.spoken_about_present` for the scene-level version, where the middle person is standing right there.
**Best in:** formats: Stage Play, Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Short Story, Audio Drama | genres: Family Drama, Drama, Migration, Crime, Political, Literary
**Avoid when:** the two principals have a direct scene early and it goes fine. One functional direct exchange destroys the premise retroactively; the audience will spend the rest of the story asking why they do not simply talk.
**Example:**
> "Tell your mother the boiler man comes Thursday."
> "Mum, Dad says the boiler man's Thursday."
> "Tell him I know, because I booked him."
> "She says thanks."
> "Tell her I said thanks for what?"
> "He says that's great." Nobody has said one true sentence and the boiler will be fixed.

`rel.bond_through_a_go_between`

### The Non-Human Member

**Also called:** the animal in the cast, the machine companion, the fifth member with no lines
**What it is:** A dog, a horse, a robot, an AI, a ship, a house — given a genuine position in the relationship map, with its own rules and a relationship to more than one human.
**Effect on the audience:** Disproportionate attachment, and a licence to show human characters behaving in ways they would never permit themselves in front of another person.
**Used for and where it works best:** Family stories, science fiction, kids and animation, survival, heritage work about working animals. The craft rules: give it **constraints, not thoughts.** A non-human member is compelling because of what it cannot do — cannot lie, cannot leave, cannot understand a specific category of thing — and the moment you grant it full interiority it becomes an ordinary character in a costume. Second: it must have a different relationship with each human, not one relationship with the household; who feeds it and who it actually follows should be two different people, and that discrepancy is a free scene. Third, the discipline that separates good from cheap: it must be able to cost somebody something. A creature that only supplies comfort is furniture that breathes. `pov.nonhuman_pov` is the separate choice of narrating from inside it; this card is about its place in the group.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Animation Feature, Series, Picture Book, Game Narrative | genres: Family Drama, Science Fiction, Adventure, Kids, Survival, Drama
**Avoid when:** you intend to kill it for an emotional beat you have not otherwise earned. Audiences now read the animal's death as a manoeuvre, and being caught manoeuvring costs more than the scene gains — see `emo.manipulated_beat`.
**Example:**
> "He likes you best."
> "He doesn't like me best. I feed him."
> "No — watch." She opens the back door. The dog looks at the door, looks at the brother, and sits down beside the brother.
> "He's old. He doesn't want to go out."
> "He went out with me twice this morning."

**Yields to:** `pov.nonhuman_pov` — Narrating from inside the non-human.

## Beginnings, Ruptures and Repairs

### The First Meeting That Is Not a Meet-Cute

**Also called:** the introduction scene, the non-cute meet, the bad first impression
**What it is:** The scene where two people who will matter to each other meet, built so that the meeting establishes the relationship's permanent problem rather than its charm.
**Effect on the audience:** They leave the scene holding a question rather than a mood, which is what makes them follow a pair rather than merely approve of them.
**Used for and where it works best:** Every relationship line, romance included. The craft rule: put the **spark and the block in the same beat.** Whatever draws them must be identical to whatever will destroy them — she is drawn to how certain he is, and his certainty is the thing that will end it — so the meeting scene and the breakup scene are the same event seen twice. Second rule: neither person is available for a relationship in the scene. Give each of them a prior errand they are trying to finish, and play the whole meeting as an interruption to both. Third: no third party may frame the meeting as significant; the second a friend says "well, *she* was nice," you have told the audience the answer and the line goes slack. See `open.the_meet` for the opening-position version and `char.defining_action_entrance` for what each of them should be caught doing.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Series, Short Story, Stage Play, Half-Hour Comedy | genres: Romance, Drama, Romantic Comedy, Literary, Crime, Buddy
**Avoid when:** the relationship's interest is its history. A story about two people who have known each other for thirty years should not stop to dramatise 1994; use `rel.founding_event` and keep it in dispute.
**Example:**
> "You're in my seat."
> "There's forty seats."
> "You're in the one I'm in on Tuesdays."
> "...It's Wednesday."
> "I know what day it is." She does not move. He does not move. He is, eventually, the one who laughs, and that is the exact thing she will not be able to stand in eleven months' time.

**Yields to:** `rel.founding_event` — The interest is the history, not the meeting.  
`rel.first_meeting`

### The Architecture of the Breakup Scene

**Also called:** the rupture, the ending scene, the split
**What it is:** The scene in which a relationship ends, structured as a sequence of tactics rather than a statement — because nobody ends a relationship in one move, and the shape of the failing is the content.
**Effect on the audience:** They experience it as an event rather than an announcement, and they feel the specific vertigo of watching someone try four things that do not work.
**Used for and where it works best:** Romance, family drama, partnerships, any story with a third-act separation. The craft rules: **the person ending it must not know how**, and must arrive with a prepared line that does not survive contact — say the prepared line early and let it fail, then watch them improvise. Second: the two of them must want different scenes. One wants it finished; the other wants an explanation, or an argument, or one more hour. That mismatch of objectives, not the disagreement about the relationship, is what generates the scene. Third: end on logistics, always. Keys, a dog, a booking, a lift home — the drop from the enormous to the administrative is what makes the audience cry, not the declaration. Fourth: give it a physical interruption partway through, so neither of them gets a clean run. This is `scene.value_turn` executed on the bond, and `scene.button` matters more here than almost anywhere.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Stage Play, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Short Story | genres: Romance, Drama, Family Drama, Literary, Melodrama, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** the relationship's end is meant to be ambiguous or gradual. A staged breakup scene declares a result; if you want the thing to rot instead, write three scenes of ordinary business getting slightly worse and never hold the meeting.
**Example:**
> "I think we both know this isn't working." (the prepared line)
> "No."
> "...What?"
> "I said no. I don't know that. You know that. Don't do the 'we'."
> "Fine. I know it."
> "Since when?"
> "Since — does it matter?"
> "It's the only thing that matters. Everything after it is just admin." Later, at the door: "Is Thursday still all right for the car?" "Thursday's fine."

**Yields to:** `rel.relationship_arc` — The end must be gradual and unannounced.  
`rel.breakup_architecture`

### The Reconciliation on New Terms

**Also called:** the repair that costs, conditional reconciliation, reconciliation without resolution
**What it is:** A repair scene in which the relationship resumes on explicitly worse or different conditions — reduced access, supervised contact, a subject permanently closed — rather than returning to the pre-rupture state.
**Effect on the audience:** Relief with a splinter in it. They get the reunion they wanted and immediately understand what it cost, which is the only version of forgiveness adult audiences believe.
**Used for and where it works best:** Family drama, late seasons, any story where the rupture was real enough that a full restoration would be a lie. The craft rules: **name the new terms out loud and make them concrete.** Not "we'll try" but "you can come on Sundays, and you don't come in the house." A vague repair is an ambiguous scene the audience will misfile as a happy ending. Second: reconciliation is a heightening, not a defusing — the terms must be humiliating for one of them to accept and difficult for the other to offer, so the scene has a struggle in it. Third: stage it in two contacts, not one. The first is logistical and constrained, the emotional confrontation comes later, after a trigger — one-scene repair is the failure named at the end of this file. Fourth: the new terms exist to be violated. Write the agreement so a later scene can break it.
**Best in:** formats: Limited Series, Novel, Feature Film, Series, Stage Play, Memoir | genres: Family Drama, Drama, Crime, Literary, Redemption, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** the genre contract promises full restoration. Comedy and much romance owe the audience the old terms back; imposing conditions there reads as a different film arriving in the last ten minutes.
**Example:**
> "You can come Sundays."
> "Sundays."
> "Sundays, and you ring first, and you don't bring her."
> "That's not —"
> "Those are the terms. I've thought about them for a year. You get about nine seconds."
> "...Sundays."

**Yields to:** `rel.instant_reconciliation` — Comedy or kids owe full restoration.  
`rel.reconciliation_on_new_terms`

### The Pair Who Are Wrong About What They Are

**Also called:** the misnamed relationship, the mislabelled bond, the two definitions
**What it is:** Two people running the same relationship under different names — he thinks they are friends, she thinks they are colleagues; she thinks it is a mentorship, he thinks it is a partnership — and neither has checked.
**Effect on the audience:** Sustained dramatic irony that requires no secret and no lie. The audience holds both definitions and can see every exchange landing wrong in both directions.
**Used for and where it works best:** Workplace stories, unrequited friendship, family business, any long bond nobody has ever had to define out loud. The craft rules: each person's definition must produce a **different set of obligations**, and you dramatise the mismatch by having each of them do something their own definition requires and the other's does not — he cancels a holiday because that is what friends do; she does not thank him, because colleagues get compensated, not thanked. Second: never let a third party diagnose it. The audience must assemble the gap. Third: the reveal is not a confession, it is a category word said casually — "my mate Tom" heard by a man who has been calling her his business partner for six years — and the scene is entirely the other person's face. Related: `sub.want_statement_gap`, and `rel.asymmetric_investment` for the version where both people know.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Series, Stage Play, Short Story, Half-Hour Comedy | genres: Workplace, Drama, Comedy, Literary, Romance, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** the mislabelling is the entire plot and the story is long. Two people being wrong about each other sustains a feature or a novella; over a season it becomes a running gag about people who never speak.
**Example:**
> "This is Tom — Tom does my accounts."
> He shakes the hand. He says, "That's right, I do her accounts."
> In the car, forty minutes later: "Did you want me to say something else?"
> "No."
> "Because you can say something else."
> "There isn't anything else. You do my accounts."

**Yields to:** `rel.asymmetric_investment` — Both people know the imbalance.  
`rel.misnamed_relationship`

### The Public Version of the Relationship

**Also called:** the performed bond, the two accounts, what we tell people
**What it is:** The pair maintains an official story about itself — how they met, how they are doing, whose idea it was — which the audience can compare against the private evidence, and which each of them polices.
**Effect on the audience:** They become the only witness with both versions, and the small maintenance work of the public story (a correction, a laugh in the wrong place) becomes unbearable to watch.
**Used for and where it works best:** Marriages, family businesses, political and public couples, any bond that gets asked about at parties. The craft rules: write the public account **once, fluently, as a performance both are good at** — the anecdote with the timing worked out, the two of them handing lines to each other. Only then show the private version. Second: break it by damage, not by confession. One of them fumbles a beat of the anecdote, or adds a detail that is true, and the other repairs it in front of everyone; the repair is the violation. Third: decide who owns the story. Usually one person authored it and the other has agreed to perform it, and the story ends when the performer stops. Pairs hard with `rel.unspoken_agreement`, and `scene.public_scene` is the frame.
**Best in:** formats: Stage Play, Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Series, Short Story | genres: Drama, Family Drama, Political, Literary, Satire, Romance
**Avoid when:** the audience has not been given the private version first, or has been given it too completely. This device runs on a gap; no gap is a dinner party, a total gap is a lie plot.
**Example:**
> "Tell them the thing about the ferry."
> "You tell it better."
> "I don't, he tells it better." (He tells it. It is very funny. They have done this a hundred times.)
> Later, to someone else, alone: "It wasn't a ferry, it was the airport, and I wasn't late, she was. It's funnier the other way round."

`rel.public_face`

## Three or More

### The Third Wheel

**Also called:** the spare, the odd one out, the friend who came too
**What it is:** A trio in which two members have a bond the third cannot enter, written from the position of the excluded one — who is present, tolerated, and quietly doing the arithmetic.
**Effect on the audience:** Physical discomfort. This is one of the few structures that can make a reader want to leave a room, and it needs no villain to do it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Coming of age, friendship groups, in-laws, new partners entering an old pair. The craft rule: the pair must not be unkind. The whole effect depends on the two of them being generous, inclusive and entirely unable to stop referencing things that happened before the third person arrived. Second rule: give the third person **one small, real advantage** — they are funnier, or they drive, or they are the only one with money — so they are not simply a victim and can make a bid to change the arrangement. Third: the scene shape is always the same — four beats of inclusion, then one shared reference that nobody explains, then the third person asks what it means, and the explanation is worse than the exclusion. Distinct from `rel.triangle`, which requires three live edges; here the third edge is the missing thing.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Series, Short Story, Stage Play, Half-Hour Comedy | genres: Coming of Age, Drama, Comedy, Literary, Romance, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** the third person is the protagonist's chosen viewpoint for a whole novel. The position is static by design; sustained at length it produces self-pity rather than pressure unless the third person eventually acts.
**Example:**
> "You're going to love this place. Do they still do the thing?"
> "They stopped doing the thing."
> "They stopped doing the thing?"
> "After Marcus."
> A beat. Then, kindly, to the third: "Sorry — you'd have to have been there."
> "No, go on, what happened with Marcus?"
> "Honestly, it's not even a story."

**Yields to:** `rel.triangle` — The excluded position must sustain a long piece.  
`rel.third_wheel`

### Jealousy as a Three-Person Structure

**Also called:** mimetic desire, triangular desire, the rival who taught you to want
**What it is:** Building jealousy as a triangle in which desire runs from the subject through a **model** to the object — the character wants the thing because a particular rival wants it, so the rival, not the prize, is the real object of attention.
**Effect on the audience:** They watch someone destroy their own life over something they can see the character does not actually want, and it is entirely convincing, because they have done it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Literary fiction, family sagas, workplace and status stories, tragedy, anywhere ambition and envy drive the plot. The craft rule from Girard's *Deceit, Desire and the Novel*: desire is borrowed, so **establish the model before the object.** Show the character admiring or measuring themselves against the rival for at least one scene before the prize is introduced, and the jealousy will read as inevitable rather than convenient. Second rule, the diagnostic: if the object were removed and the character would still be watching the rival, you have the structure; if they would go home, you have ordinary competition. Third: the give-away behaviour is imitation — the jealous character starts adopting the rival's vocabulary, clothes, opinions, exactly while claiming to despise them. Related classical situations: `sit.polti_32_mistaken_jealousy` and `sit.polti_24_rivalry_superior_inferior`; `emo.envy` covers the feeling, this covers the machinery.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Stage Play, Limited Series, Short Story, One-Hour Drama | genres: Literary, Tragedy, Drama, Psychological, Family Drama, Workplace
**Avoid when:** you need the audience to share the jealous character's valuation of the prize. This structure tells them the prize is arbitrary, which is fatal to a story where the object must genuinely matter.
**Example:**
> "You don't even like the flat."
> "I like the flat."
> "You called it a corridor with ambitions. You said that to me, in that room, about that flat."
> "That was before."
> "Before what?"
> "...Before I knew he'd offered on it."

**Yields to:** `arc.shared_want_opponent` — The prize must genuinely matter.  
`rel.mimetic_jealousy`

### The Group That Expels a Member

**Also called:** the ostracism, casting out, the vote
**What it is:** The scene or sequence in which a group removes one of its own, written as a group decision with a mechanism — who raises it, who stays silent, who is made to deliver it.
**Effect on the audience:** Primal dread, then an unexpected relief that implicates them. Expulsion scenes work because the reader catches themselves agreeing with the group.
**Used for and where it works best:** Crews, villages, congregations, workplaces, school stories, any closed system. The craft rules, and the social-psychology research is unusually useful here: groups expel for exactly two reasons — **norm violation** and **ineptitude** — and the two produce completely different scenes. Norm violation gets a trial; ineptitude gets a quiet reassignment nobody names. Pick one and commit. Second: someone must be against it and lose, or the group is `rel.everyone_agrees` with a casualty. Third: the expelled person must be given a chance to leave with dignity and must refuse it, because the refusal is what makes it terrible. Fourth: show the group immediately afterwards, functioning better. That is the cruellest and truest beat available, and almost nobody writes it.
**Best in:** formats: Limited Series, Novel, Feature Film, Stage Play, Series, Short Story | genres: Drama, Crime, Social Drama, Faith, War, Thriller
**Avoid when:** the expelled member returns within the same act. Expulsion is one of the few irreversible group events; spending it and then undoing it teaches the audience that nothing in this group has consequences.
**Example:**
> "Nobody's saying you have to go."
> "You've moved the meeting."
> "We moved it to Wednesdays. There was a thing on Tuesdays."
> "What thing?"
> Nobody answers. Someone starts stacking chairs that do not need stacking.

`rel.expulsion`

### The Succession Fight

**Also called:** the inheritance war, who gets it, the heir problem
**What it is:** A structure in which several people compete to replace one aging holder of power, and where the incumbent's refusal to decide is itself the engine that keeps them all in place.
**Effect on the audience:** Endlessly renewable tension with no external threat required — and a bitter comedy, because the contenders keep performing for an audience of one who is never going to be satisfied.
**Used for and where it works best:** Family firms, dynasties, criminal organisations, churches, political parties, any heritage story about a throne. The craft rules: the incumbent must **withhold the decision deliberately**, because an heir who is named stops competing and the machine stops. Second: each contender must be genuinely qualified in a different way — the loyal one, the capable one, the one who is most like the incumbent — so the audience can argue about it too. Third: give the succession an object, not a title. A ring, a chair, a set of keys, a signature that has to be witnessed; abstract power produces abstract scenes. Fourth: at least one contender must sincerely not want it and be pushed, or every scene plays at the same pitch. Runs on `rel.shifting_coalition` for the alliance layer and `rel.sibling_order` where the contenders are family.
**Best in:** formats: Series, Limited Series, Novel, Feature Film, Stage Play, One-Hour Drama | genres: Family Drama, Political, Crime, Historical, Drama, Satire
**Avoid when:** the incumbent is dead at the start. That is an inheritance dispute — a different, flatter structure, because the thing everybody actually wants is the dead person's approval, and it can no longer be withheld or granted.
**Example:**
> "You've never once said which of us it is."
> "Haven't I?"
> "No."
> "Well." He picks up the glass. "It's whichever of you asks me that question last."

**Yields to:** `rel.bond_with_the_dead` — The incumbent is dead at the start.  
`rel.succession_fight`

### The Ensemble That Loses Its Leader

**Also called:** the removed centre, life after the lead, the vacancy
**What it is:** Handling the departure of the character the group was organised around — by death, exit or recasting — so that the remaining relationships have to re-form rather than simply carry on.
**Effect on the audience:** Genuine instability, because the audience's own orientation device has been removed and they must re-learn the room alongside the characters.
**Used for and where it works best:** Long-running series, sagas, war stories, any ensemble that outlives a cast member. The craft rules: **do not replace the function, redistribute it.** The leader was doing four jobs — deciding, absorbing blame, being funny, holding two enemies apart — and the strong version hands each job to a different person, badly. Second: the vacancy must be visible in the first scene afterwards, structurally rather than verbally; somebody sits in the chair and it is wrong, or nobody does and the meeting does not start. Third: name the thing that now has no owner. When the leader goes, one relationship in the group was entirely mediated by them, and that pair must now deal with each other directly for the first time — that is the scene the departure was for. Fourth: give the group one visible improvement. The rot in this material is treating the leader as irreplaceable, which flatters the departed and starves the survivors.
**Best in:** formats: Series, Limited Series, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Half-Hour Comedy, Serialised Fiction | genres: Drama, Workplace, Crime, War, Comedy, Family Drama
**Avoid when:** the leader is the story's subject rather than its centre of gravity. Some pieces simply end when that person does, and continuing produces a sequel about people the audience was never following.
**Example:**
> "Who's chairing?"
> "You are."
> "I'm not chairing."
> "Somebody's chairing. It's ten past."
> Priya pulls the folder over, opens it at the wrong page, and starts anyway. Marcus, who has not spoken directly to Priya in two years, says, "It's the blue tab." It is the first useful thing either has said to the other since the funeral.

`rel.leader_removed`

## The Ensemble on the Page

### The Ensemble Pilot

**Also called:** introducing eight people, the cast-launch problem, the first-episode load
**What it is:** The specific craft of a first episode or first chapter that must establish a large cast, solved by refusing to introduce them as individuals and introducing them as **pairs and factions** instead.
**Effect on the audience:** They retain the cast. Readers cannot hold eight names in an hour, but they can hold three relationships and a conflict, and the names attach themselves to those.
**Used for and where it works best:** Television Pilots, first chapters of big novels, ensemble features, launch episodes of audio drama. The craft rules, and the pilot literature is united on the first: **you do not have to introduce everyone.** Launch three fully, sketch three, and hold two back for episode two. Second: introduce by relationship, not by person — the audience should meet "the two who are not speaking" before they meet Marcus and Priya, because a pair with a problem is one unit of memory and two people are two. Third: give every character introduced in the first ten minutes a single, physical, defining action, not a description; `char.defining_action_entrance` and `char.ten_second_person` are the tools. Fourth: stage one scene with the whole cast present but only three speaking, so the audience sees the shape of the group before being asked to learn it. Feed the load-spreading with `expo.pilot_load_split`, and audit the result with `rel.pair_matrix`.
**Best in:** formats: Television Pilot, Series, Novel, Limited Series, Audio Drama, Game Narrative | genres: Drama, Workplace, Crime, Fantasy, Science Fiction, Ensemble
**Avoid when:** the piece is a closed story under about ninety minutes. Below that, a large cast introduced properly leaves no time for anything to happen to them; combine characters instead — see `char.combine_characters`.
**Example:**
> Page 3 gives the reader three units, not eight names.
> "The two who won't share a car." / "The one everybody apologises to." / "The three who were already here at six."
> Marcus and Priya are introduced as the first unit. Their names arrive on page 5, by which time the reader already wants them.

**Yields to:** `char.combine_characters` — Closed story under about ninety minutes.  
`rel.ensemble_pilot`

### The Reunion Ensemble

**Also called:** the reunion play, the funeral weekend, everyone back in the house
**What it is:** A structure that gathers an old group in one place for a bounded occasion — a funeral, a wedding, an anniversary, a sale of the house — so that every dormant relationship has to be reactivated at once.
**Effect on the audience:** High density with no exposition. Every pairing arrives pre-loaded, and the audience gets the pleasure of decoding a history they were not present for.
**Used for and where it works best:** Stage plays above all, features, limited series, literary novels. The craft rules: the occasion must have a **deadline and a task** — the house sells Monday, the service is at eleven, the will is read at four — because a reunion without a clock is a party. Second: assign each returning member a current-life problem they have brought with them, so the group is not merely excavating the past; the past is what they use as a weapon in a present argument. Third: one person never left, and they are the protagonist, because they are the only one for whom the location is not nostalgia. Fourth: do not reunite everybody at once. Stagger the arrivals across the first third, and let each arrival re-sort the room. Use `rel.pair_off_the_room` for the texture and `rel.shifting_coalition` for the movement.
**Best in:** formats: Stage Play, Feature Film, Limited Series, Novel, Short Story, Audio Drama | genres: Family Drama, Drama, Literary, Comedy, Social Drama, Mystery
**Avoid when:** the group's shared history has to be explained rather than referenced. If the audience needs a briefing to follow the room, the form is wrong; reunion structures run on overheard fragments, not on catch-up.
**Example:**
> "You came."
> "I said I'd come."
> "You said that in 2009."
> "I came in 2009."
> "You came to the church car park in 2009."
> From the kitchen, not looking up: "He's here now, isn't he. Somebody do the potatoes."

**Yields to:** `rel.outsider_in_the_group` — The history must be explained, not referenced.  
`rel.reunion_ensemble`

## Named Failures

### The Admiration Chorus

**Also called:** the group that exists to be impressed, the fan club cast, the entourage problem
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** An ensemble whose members have no relationships with each other — only with the protagonist — and whose function in every scene is to witness, endorse and be surprised by them.
**Effect on the audience:** They stop believing the protagonist. Praise from characters with no independent existence reads as the author praising their own creation, and it actively reduces the lead's stature rather than raising it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Never deliberately. Detect it with the cell audit: take the protagonist out of the ensemble grid and see how many pairs still have anything between them. If the grid collapses, you have it. Second test — count how many scenes in the story do not contain the lead. If the answer is none, the supporting cast does not exist when unobserved.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** always. It is most common in first novels, in superhero and fantasy material, and in anything written from a single admiring point of view. The real failure mode is not vanity — it is that the writer built the cast from the protagonist outward, assigning each person a function relative to the lead, and never went back to connect them sideways. Three fixes: give two supporting characters a conflict the protagonist does not know about; write one scene the protagonist is not in and keep it; and let the most loyal member be right about something the lead is wrong about, in front of the others. See `rel.pair_matrix`, `char.supporting_character_want` and `char.audience_surrogate_trap`.
**Example:**
> BEFORE: "How did you even know?" / "I didn't. He did. He always does." / "He's unbelievable."
> AFTER: "How did you even know?" / "He didn't. He guessed, and we've got about four hours before anyone finds out he guessed, so can everybody stop looking at him and start on the boxes."

**Yields to:** `rel.ensemble_as_system` — Connect the cast sideways, not to the lead.  
`rel.admiration_chorus`

### The Rupture With No Aftermath

**Also called:** the consequence-free row, the reset scene, the argument that never happened
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A major relationship break — a betrayal, a said-out-loud cruelty, an expulsion — after which the next scene shows the pair behaving exactly as before, with no altered permissions, no changed logistics and no residue.
**Effect on the audience:** They downgrade every future conflict. Once a rupture has been shown to cost nothing, the audience correctly concludes that nothing in this story is load-bearing, and they stop flinching.
**Used for and where it works best:** Never deliberately. Detect it by covering the rupture scene and reading the two scenes either side of it back to back; if they join seamlessly, the rupture is decorative. Second test: list what one character is now allowed to do that they were not allowed to do before, and what they have lost permission for. An empty list is the diagnosis.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** always, and most damagingly in long series, where it is usually caused by production reality rather than by choice — the pair must keep working together next week, so the writers quietly restore the old terms. The real failure mode is that the writer treated the argument as the payoff. It is not; the payoff is the fortnight afterwards. Three fixes: change one piece of logistics permanently (who drives, who is told first, who has the key); remove one topic from the pair's available conversation and never let them have it again; and let a third party notice the change before either of them does.
**Example:**
> BEFORE: Episode 4 ends, "You've never once put me first." Episode 5 opens, "Coffee?" / "Please."
> AFTER: Episode 5 opens with him making one coffee, for himself, and her seeing him do it. Neither says anything. He does not make her one for another three episodes.

**Yields to:** `rel.reconciliation_on_new_terms` — The rupture must change the terms.  
`rel.rupture_without_aftermath`

### The One-Scene Reconciliation

**Also called:** the instant repair, the apology that works, forgiveness on demand
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A repair that arrives immediately after the rupture, in which an apology is offered, accepted, and the relationship is restored inside a single scene — usually because the plot needs the pair functional again.
**Effect on the audience:** They feel the machinery. Real repair is slow, partial and humiliating, and audiences know it from their own lives, so a clean one-scene forgiveness reads as the author rescuing themselves from a problem they created.
**Used for and where it works best:** Never deliberately in drama. The genuine exceptions are contractual: episodic comedy that resets by design, preschool and kids' stories where the repair **is** the teaching, and short-form where there is no room. Outside those, detect it by measuring the distance: count the pages or minutes between the rupture and the repair, then ask what the injured party did in that gap. If the answer is "waited," you have it.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** always in long-form drama. The real failure mode is scheduling, not sentiment — the writer has a plot beat in three pages that requires these two to cooperate, so the relationship is repaired to serve it. Four fixes, in descending order: put a plot obligation between the rupture and the repair so they must cooperate while still broken, which is far better material than either scene; make the first contact purely logistical and defer the emotional confrontation until a later trigger; have the apology be accepted and not work, so the terms change rather than restore — see `rel.reconciliation_on_new_terms`; or let the injured party forgive and the offender refuse to be forgiven.
**Example:**
> BEFORE: "I'm sorry." / "I know. Come here." (rupture to repair: forty seconds)
> AFTER: "I'm sorry."
> "All right."
> "Are we all right?"
> "I said all right. I didn't say we're all right." She picks up the other end of the wardrobe. They carry it down two flights, and that is the whole of the reconciliation for now.

**Yields to:** `rel.reconciliation_on_new_terms` — Explicitly named as the repair.  
`rel.instant_reconciliation`
