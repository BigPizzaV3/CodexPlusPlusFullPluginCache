# Exposition and world complaints

Part of the symptom router. Open this file when the complaint is about exposition and world. Each complaint below is followed by the cards that repair it, best first; `LOOKUP.md` says which file holds each card.

---

### "The world isn't landing"

1. Know more than you show, and show the tip
   `expo.iceberg_principle` · `expo.implication_worldbuilding`
2. Demonstrate the rule instead of explaining it, and show what it costs
   `expo.rule_by_demonstration` · `expo.rule_cost_demo` · `expo.object_tells_rule`
3. Let the place carry it
   `expo.environmental_detail` · `expo.diegetic_text` · `expo.offscreen_world`
4. For heritage and historical work, specificity is the whole credibility budget
   `expo.heritage_specificity`

### "There's an infodump"

1. Name it, then apply the deletion test
   `expo.lore_dump` · `expo.deletion_test`
2. If a character could reply "I know", cut the line
   `expo.as_you_know` · `dial.known_information`
3. Move it downstream to the point of use
   `expo.relocate_downstream` · `expo.deferred_explanation`
4. Or absorb it into an argument that was happening anyway
   `expo.absorb_into_argument` · `expo.exposition_under_pressure`
5. Give the listener a reason to resist hearing it
   `expo.resistant_listener`
6. If the block survives all that, there are three mechanical repairs, in order of how much they cost you. Hand it to the listener — they ask in pieces and each answer reaches only as far as the question
   `expo.statement_into_question`
7. Or cut it in three and attach each part to a different character's need in a different scene
   `expo.split_across_three_scenes`
8. Or delete it and put one concrete object in its place
   `expo.trade_paragraph_for_image`

### "Too many invented names and terms"

1. There is a budget, and you are over it
   `expo.proper_noun_budget` · `expo.proper_noun_avalanche` · `open.overload_open`
2. One new term at a time, anchored to something familiar
   `expo.coined_term_in_context` · `expo.familiar_anchor` · `expo.jargon_threshold`
3. A glossary is a symptom, not a solution
   `expo.glossary_crutch` · `expo.optional_appendix`

### "The reader needs to know this before the story starts"

1. Usually they do not
   `expo.history_prologue` · `open.backstory_open`
2. Ration it against need
   `expo.need_to_know_ladder`
3. Use a newcomer, or a competent character walking through it
   `expo.newcomer_eye` · `expo.competent_walkthrough`

### "Technical or procedural material is killing the pace"

1. Procedural exposition has its own rules
   `expo.procedural_exposition`
2. In non-fiction, the narration carries a contract
   `expo.doc_narration_contract` · `expo.explainer_scaffold`
3. In short-form, front-load it and move
   `expo.short_form_frontload`


### "Episode two starts and nobody remembers what happened in episode one"

Three different jobs that look like one, and doing the wrong one is what makes a season open badly.

1. The first two minutes should restate where everyone is and what is outstanding, inside a scene that is visibly doing something else
   `expo.re_entry_exposition`
2. A cut-together recap is the blunter tool, and it carries only the facts *this* episode will use
   `expo.recap_sequence`
3. The failure to watch for: a recap that includes a shot whose only purpose is this episode's turn, so the audience knows the shape before it starts
   `expo.spoiler_recap`
4. At the start of a new season the job is different again — a light re-delivery of the core rules serving three audiences at once, those who watched a year ago, those who binged last week, and those starting here
   `expo.season_two_reteach`
5. And decide the inter-episode interval as a convention rather than fresh each time; audiences learn it and predict with it
   `time.serial_clock`

### "The reader put it down for a week and came back lost"

Nobody writes a recap for a novel, and a novelist's gaps are unknown, unequal, and there are roughly forty of them. **Know the decay order, because it is not the one you would guess.** Names go first and go fast — a proper name is an arbitrary label, which is why a reader who has forgotten *Marek* has not forgotten the brother-in-law with the van. Then logistical state, then causality. Emotional state survives almost everything. So restore the name first, the position second, the stakes last — and never restore the feeling, which is already there and reads as instruction the moment you touch it.

1. Re-entry is carried by concrete anchors, not by summary. Three inside the first hundred words, one of each kind: a name welded to an object or a job, a physical position, and one line reprised verbatim so the reader *recognises* rather than learns. Recognition is free; learning costs a paragraph
   `expo.book_re_entry`
2. The limit is grammatical rather than quantitative. The reminder rides in a subordinate clause, an appositive or a possessive and never becomes the main clause. Test it by deleting the reminder: if the sentence still has a job, the reminder was riding; if the sentence collapses, it was a recap wearing a clause
   `expo.as_you_know`
3. For a character gone longer than about forty pages, stop reminding and reintroduce — and that work is done at the first appearance, not at the return
   `char.half_remembered_return`
4. The anxious version destroys the asset it is insuring: a writer who re-identifies every character at every chapter head trains the reader to skim chapter openings, which is exactly where the hooks are hidden
   `open.chapter_opening` · `rhythm.section_break_scale`
5. The audiobook is the hard case and needs a different text. Every anchor audible rather than visual, names repeating more often than a printed page would tolerate, attribution before the line rather than after, and no pronoun reaching back further than one sentence. Load-bearing facts get two touches in two different kinds of channel. If you only get one text for both, write for the ear
   `rhythm.listener_no_rewind` · `expo.two_touch_rule`
6. For reinstalling a fact through a third party, and for a plant used as maintenance
   `info.recap_without_recap` · `info.buried_plant`
7. The screen cards sit next door and all assume a scheduled gap whose length the writer knows, which is exactly what a novelist does not have
   `expo.re_entry_exposition` · `expo.recap_sequence` · `expo.season_two_reteach` · `info.between_episode_memory`
8. The instruction case inverts the sorting rule — an instruction reader keeps the concept and loses the state, a novel reader keeps the feeling and loses the name
   `expo.lapsed_learner_re_entry`
9. Serialised fiction inverts the etiquette instead: the gap is a week by design and re-entry is a convention readers expect. But the convention is *overlap* rather than summary — end the instalment one beat earlier than you want to and open the next on that same beat from the other side of the room
10. A dramatis personae at the front is a confession that the prose failed, not a repair for it. The real diagnostic is a reader told to read the manuscript only on weekday mornings, marking every place they turned back

### "The pilot is all setup"

1. Split the world information deliberately: the minimum episode one must carry, and the much larger remainder distributed across the season
   `expo.pilot_load_split`

### "The character who explains things isn't a character"

1. That is `expo.exposition_fairy` — a companion, a mentor, an AI with no want of their own, no capacity to be wrong and nothing to lose
2. The cheapest repair is to reverse the direction of the scene: the person who needs to know asks, in pieces
   `expo.statement_into_question`
3. If the explainer must stay, give the facts somewhere to be taken rather than given — every fact as the reaction half of a beat
   `beat.exposition_beat`

### "I've explained the rule four times"

1. That is `expo.over_taught_rule`: demonstrated, restated, demonstrated again, referred to once more, and only then used
2. The actual requirement is smaller than the fear — twice, in two different *kinds* of channel, separated by at least one intervening beat
   `expo.two_touch_rule`
3. To find out whether even that is needed, read it aloud once at performance speed to someone who does not know the story, and ask them to tell it back
   `expo.first_hearing_test`

### "The ending needs a rule I never set up"

1. That is `expo.retroactive_rule`, and audiences catch it every time
2. The related breach: a piece establishes how another language is rendered and then abandons the convention for one scene's convenience
   `expo.translation_convention_breach`

### "The flashback tells me what I already knew"

1. That is `expo.redundant_flashback` — a scene dramatising what the audience already inferred
2. If the material genuinely has to land, trade the whole passage for one concrete image instead
   `expo.trade_paragraph_for_image`

### "The rules of the monster arrive as a lecture"

1. Teach them on a casualty: someone breaks the rule and pays for it, before the protagonist is ever at risk
   `expo.horror_rule_victim`
2. In comedy the same load rides in the *setup* of a joke, so the audience takes delivery while leaning forward for the punchline
   `expo.joke_carries_the_fact`
3. In a game or a sport, explain only at the moment the rule is first invoked, tied to the incident just watched
   `expo.game_rule_commentary`
4. For a young audience, three channels inside one segment — said once, shown once without words, then asked for, with the answer left to the child
   `expo.kids_repetition_ladder`

### "Everyone already knows this story and I'm still setting it up"

1. Drop the setup the source spent chapters on and enter where the informed audience is already sitting
   `expo.assumed_canon`

### "The narration is a neutral voice reading facts"

1. Give the voice a position in the story — a participant, a survivor, a later self — so every fact arrives coloured by an interest
   `expo.character_voiceover`
2. In factual work, lay the claim and its evidence in the same three seconds: the interviewee's sentence over footage of the thing described
   `expo.interview_cutaway`
3. Move names, dates and quantities to the screen and free the narration to do what text cannot
   `expo.onscreen_graphics`
4. Or put the context at a boundary, in nobody's voice — before the film, above the chapter, under a scene change
   `expo.framing_text_furniture`
5. Or hand the world over as in-world documents and let the reader assemble it
   `expo.epistolary_delivery`

### "I want an epigraph and I can't tell if it's earning its place"

The page's own furniture — epigraph, part title, section heading, dateline, standfirst — set in a different type from the prose because it is not in the prose's voice. Four different jobs, and confusing them is where most of the damage happens.

1. The card holds the rule for each piece
   `expo.page_furniture`
2. **The epigraph must be cashed or broken on purpose.** If you cannot finish the sentence "this epigraph is answered on page ___", cut it. The strongest modern use is one the book contradicts — quote the official history, then spend three hundred pages disproving it. And write your own wherever you can, because an invented epigraph cannot be borrowed authority
3. **A part title is a lens the reader holds over forty pages.** Name it after the thing they will only later understand was at stake — a noun out of the world, never an abstraction. The test: change it and see whether the chapters under it read differently. "LOSS" on a part is a theme label at forty-eight point
   `theme.explained_theme`
4. **A section heading marks the beginning of an idea, never the middle of one.** Put them where the argument turns rather than at a fixed interval, and make heading and text each stand alone — a first sentence that leans on the heading is a paragraph written with a hole in it
5. **A dateline only pays where it is native.** In reportage it is a claim about where the reporter stood. In fiction use it only where exact coordinates matter to the plot, and then ignore it: the scene beneath it must still open on a detail that could belong to no other place
   `time.passage_marker` · `time.datestamp_crutch`
6. **A standfirst promises and must be paid inside the first third.** Write it after the piece and out of the piece, and never write one the article cannot meet
7. The honest failure is that furniture gets used to supply what the prose failed to establish, and the diagnostic is quick and brutal: **delete every piece of furniture and read the pages.** Whatever becomes unreadable is what the prose was never carrying — fix it there, then restore only what you still actively want. Anything you cannot bring yourself to put back was never doing anything
   `expo.deletion_test`
8. Use none at all in a single-strand novel in one place and one continuous time, which is most novels. None in short fiction, where a section heading inside twelve pages reads as a memo. None in a first-person voice close enough that a second typeface breaks it. And never stack *kinds* at one boundary — part title, epigraph, dateline, chapter one is four throat-clearings before a word of story
9. The screen's version of the same instrument, where the constraint is legibility in seconds and the audience cannot go back
   `expo.framing_text_furniture`
10. What comes after the last page, and text that exists inside the world rather than outside it
   `expo.back_matter_craft` · `expo.diegetic_text`

### "The tutorial is a layer bolted over the opening"

1. Write a scene in which somebody has an in-world reason to explain, demonstrate or demand the action, so the tutorial *is* the opening
   `inter.diegetic_tutorial`
2. The stronger version removes the explaining entirely: build the first space so the only available action is the mechanic, and progress is impossible until they perform it
   `expo.tutorial_by_level_design`
