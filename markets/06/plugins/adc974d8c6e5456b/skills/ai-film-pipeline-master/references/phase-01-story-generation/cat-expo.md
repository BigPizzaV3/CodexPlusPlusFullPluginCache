## The Information Budget

### Name the Fact Before the Scene

**Also called:** the exposition selector, one fact at a time, what will they do with it
**What it is:** The card at the head of the `expo.` shelf. It does not tell you how to deliver anything. It makes you write down **the single fact** first — one sentence, one fact, no conjunctions — and then asks the three questions the shelf's own pair graph asks over and over: **what will the audience have to do with this fact, out of whose mouth is it allowed to come, and is this the first time they have met it.** Each answer returns a named shortlist. The axes were not invented: they were read off the shelf's `Yields to:` conditions and its `Avoid when:` boundaries, and the conditions that repeated became the questions. Every card cited here is cited by id.
**Effect on the audience:** None. This card never reaches the page or the screen. It exists so that the card that does reach it was chosen against the fact, the speaker and the clock, rather than reached for because it is the one the writer likes.
**Used for and where it works best:** Answer the three questions **in order, out loud, in one sentence each, with the fact written down in front of you.** If the fact will not fit in one sentence it is two facts and this runs twice — the shelf's commonest defect is a writer choosing one delivery for a block. The first answer removes about two-thirds of the shelf; the second and third leave a shortlist of three to six. If you cannot answer question one, you do not have an exposition problem, you have an outline problem, and nothing here will fix it. Where two answers fight over the same beat, that is two deliveries, and the second one is on the axis-three list.
**Best in:** formats: all | genres: all
**Avoid when:** **The withholding is the point.** If the audience is meant not to know, you are on the wrong shelf — that is `info.`, and this map will hand you a delivery card for a thing you are not delivering. **Also avoid it on a draft that already exists.** This card chooses; it does not diagnose. It is forbidden from naming a single named failure, so it cannot tell you what went wrong — the diagnosis is always in the `Avoid when:` of the card you actually used, and it is reached from there, never from here. And **distrust axis two whenever your honest answer is its third value.** More than a third of the reachable shelf lives under *the piece speaking in its own voice*, which is not a channel so much as the absence of one: choose it and this map is down to two questions and doing two-thirds of the work it does on a scene with people in it.
**Prompt:** `[Selector — produces no prompt. It returns card ids. The chosen card carries the prompt.]`

**The axes**

**Axis 1 — What will the audience have to DO with this fact?**
Asked first, because it is the boundary the shelf states most often and the only one that sorts every region of it. The conditions are stated as consequences, not as feelings: *"where precision matters to the plot outcome"* (`expo.implication_worldbuilding`), *"where the term is a plot-critical mechanism with a precise definition"* (`expo.coined_term_in_context`), *"where the fact is load-bearing and must be held precisely"* (`expo.joke_carries_the_fact`), *"where the omitted information is load-bearing for the plot's logic"* (`expo.iceberg_principle`), *"Reader will never perform the process"* (`expo.numbered_step_list`), *"Fiction; reader will not reproduce it"* (`expo.worked_example`), *"where the book will be read to a child rather than by one"* (`expo.decodability_spec`), *"where the audience has already bought and is trying to learn the thing"* (`expo.demo_as_scene`), *"in fiction with no factual claim"* (`expo.back_matter_craft`), *"Non-fiction making factual claims"* (`expo.optional_appendix`), *"Fiction, where showing beats naming"* (`expo.mechanism_statement`).

**Axis 2 — Out of whose mouth, and is anyone's mouth involved at all?**
The channel boundary, and the shelf states it as an incapacity rather than a preference: *"a room can carry a state and cannot state a law of inheritance"* (`expo.environmental_detail`), *"the rule is abstract, statistical or legal — no single stageable instance"* (`expo.rule_by_demonstration`), *"where the audience cannot be assumed to read"* (`expo.diegetic_text`), *"Format cannot hold document reading time"* (`expo.epistolary_delivery`), *"where the fact is a number, a date or a name"* (`expo.trade_paragraph_for_image`), *"for anything emotional — a super under a grieving interviewee moves the eyes off the face"* (`expo.onscreen_graphics`), *"Piece needs a fixed, neutral remit"* (`expo.character_voiceover`), *"Medium is the printed page, not the screen"* (`expo.framing_text_furniture`), *"anything the insiders would find too obvious to mention"* (`expo.newcomer_eye`). Its fourth value is the honest one: **nobody yet.**

**Axis 3 — Is this the first time they meet it, or are you handing it back?**
Asked last, and asked at all because it is the only axis that reaches the re-delivery and re-entry region, which the other two cannot see. *"The gap is a season, not a week"* (`expo.re_entry_exposition`), *"where the show is episodic or comic"* (`expo.recap_sequence`), *"Premiere opens on a cliffhanger payoff"* (`expo.season_two_reteach`), *"Character gone longer than about forty pages"* (`expo.book_re_entry`), *"The returning audience is a story audience"* (`expo.lapsed_learner_re_entry`), *"where the format permits re-reading"* (`expo.two_touch_rule`), *"where the adaptation reinterprets the premise — a changed rule must be established from zero"* (`expo.assumed_canon`).

**The default, and the one against it**

**The default is `expo.need_to_know_ladder` holding the budget with `expo.rule_by_demonstration` carrying the delivery — and it is measured, not asserted.** `expo.rule_by_demonstration` is the most-yielded-to card on the shelf: five cards that are not themselves failures send a reader to it — `expo.implication_worldbuilding` (*"precision matters to the plot outcome"*), `expo.explainer_scaffold` (*"the material is narrative rather than explanatory"*), `expo.game_rule_commentary` (*"the audience must anticipate the rule before it is broken"*), `expo.assumed_canon` (*"the adaptation reinterprets the premise"*) and `expo.mechanism_statement` (*"Fiction, where showing beats naming"*) — and a sixth edge reaches it from a named failure this map may not list. `expo.need_to_know_ladder` is next with three live, from `expo.iceberg_principle`, `expo.history_prologue` and `expo.deferred_explanation`, plus one more from a failure. Nothing else on the shelf is named more than three times. **The two are not rivals and that is the point: the shelf falls back to the ladder when the answer is *you delivered too much, too early*, and to demonstration when the answer is *you said it instead of staging it*.** Two different wrong answers, two different fallbacks, and every serious piece needs both.

**The one against it is `expo.skippable_by_design`.** Nothing routes to it; it is the target of no `Yields to:` field anywhere on the shelf; and it is the only card here that hands the decision to the audience. Every other card on this shelf assumes the writer owns the moment of delivery and is choosing how to spend it — the budget cards ration it, the channel cards dress it, the re-entry cards repeat it, and all of them take for granted that the audience will be there when it happens. `expo.skippable_by_design` starts from the opposite premise: **the audience may refuse this, so build the piece so that refusing it is safe, and put the irreducible minimum in something short that cannot be refused.** That inverts the whole shelf, and it is written for games because games are where the audience's refusal is visible — but nothing in the card is about games, and the one thing short-form and print most badly lack is a designed floor beneath the part the audience will skip. Its only stated cost is in its own `Avoid when:` — a piece whose whole proposition is its text has designed against itself. Its near cousin against the grain is `expo.object_tells_rule`, likewise the target of nothing, and likewise the only card where the delivery is carried by somebody *reacting* to a thing rather than by anyone delivering anything.

### The Iceberg Principle

**Also called:** theory of omission, the one-eighth rule, Hemingway's iceberg
**What it is:** You build far more world than you deliver, and put roughly an eighth of it on the page — the visible part reads as solid because the invisible part is actually there.
**Effect on the audience:** The world feels larger than the story. Details carry weight the audience cannot account for, which reads as depth rather than decoration.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any invented or unfamiliar world. The craft rule that makes it work is the hard one: omission only functions if you *did the work*. A detail left out of a world you never built reads as a hole. Test it by asking any secondary character a question the story never asks — if you can answer in one specific sentence, the iceberg is real.
**Best in:** formats: novel, feature, series, documentary | genres: fantasy, science fiction, historical, crime
**Avoid when:** the omitted information is load-bearing for the plot's logic. Iceberging a rule the climax depends on is not restraint, it is a cheat — the audience feels the ending was decided off-page. The real failure mode: writers use "iceberg" as a permission slip for vagueness they never resolved.
**Example:**
> Explained: "The Ninth Concord had ended thirty years ago, after the Salt Wars, when the northern houses surrendered their water rights to the Assembly."
> Iceberged: "He still wouldn't drink from a northern well. Thirty years, and he still wouldn't."

**Yields to:** `expo.need_to_know_ladder` — where the omitted information is load-bearing for the plot's logic. Iceberging a rule the climax depends on is not restraint, it is a cheat, and the ladder is how that rule gets delivered without a dump.  
`expo.iceberg_principle`

### Need-to-Know Ladder

**Also called:** just-in-time exposition, the information staircase
**What it is:** Every fact is delivered at the last moment the audience can still use it, and not one scene earlier.
**Effect on the audience:** Information arrives when it is already interesting, because a question is open. Nothing has to be memorised in advance.
**Used for and where it works best:** Dense worlds and procedural plots where the raw fact count is high. The craft rule: for each fact, name the scene where the audience *uses* it, then place the delivery in the scene immediately before. If you cannot name a use scene, the fact is not exposition — it is trivia, and it comes out. Related to `info.plant_and_payoff`, but stricter: plant-and-payoff tolerates distance, the ladder deliberately shortens it.
**Best in:** formats: feature, series, thriller novel, game | genres: heist, science fiction, legal, medical, spy
**Avoid when:** the fact must feel *pre-existing* rather than convenient. Deliver the rule of a trap one beat before the trap and the audience smells the machinery. For those, move it far upstream and bury it — that is `info.buried_plant`, not this.
**Example:**
> Not: chapter two explains that the vault doors run on the city's power grid.
> Instead, chapter nine, crossing the plaza: "Streetlights died a block behind them. She counted. Nine seconds later the lights ahead came on. 'The grid's on a loop,' she said. 'So is the vault.'"

**Yields to:** `info.buried_plant` — where the fact must feel *pre-existing* rather than convenient. Deliver the rule of a trap one beat before the trap and the audience smells the machinery; move it far upstream and bury it instead.  
`expo.need_to_know_ladder`

### The Proper Noun Budget

**Also called:** name rationing, the three-name rule
**What it is:** A hard cap on how many invented names — people, places, factions, objects — the audience meets per unit of story, usually no more than three new ones in an opening chapter or opening five minutes.
**Effect on the audience:** They retain names instead of skimming past them. Retained names make the next scene legible; skipped names compound into fog.
**Used for and where it works best:** Fantasy, science fiction, historical and any ensemble piece. The craft rule: a proper noun earns its place only if the audience will need to *recognise it again*. A name used once should be a description instead — "the man from the coast," not "Tessarin of Vell." Budget hardest in the opening, where the audience has no scaffold to hang names on.
**Best in:** formats: novel, feature, series pilot, short film | genres: fantasy, science fiction, historical epic, political drama
**Avoid when:** the crowding *is* the effect. A bureaucratic nightmare, a war room, a genealogy meant to overwhelm — there, an avalanche of names is the experience. Do it knowingly and for less than a page, or you have written `expo.proper_noun_avalanche` by accident.
**Example:**
> Over budget: "Kerath of the Hulun sept sent word to Dravaine, who had held the Mereth Gate since the Sundering of Oss."
> On budget: "A man from the river clans sent word to the gate. The gate had held for sixty years. It was called Mereth."

`expo.proper_noun_budget`

### Worldbuilding by Implication

**Also called:** the implied hinterland, negative-space worldbuilding
**What it is:** One specific concrete detail implies an entire system the text never describes — a tax stamp implies a state, a ration card implies a scarcity, a scar implies a law.
**Effect on the audience:** They do the construction. A world the audience assembled themselves is a world they believe, because they cannot separate it from their own inference.
**Used for and where it works best:** Anywhere the system is too large to explain — economies, religions, bureaucracies, histories. The craft rule: choose the artefact at the *far end* of the system, the consumer-level object, not the institution. Don't describe the temple hierarchy; show a woman counting coins to buy the cheaper incense. Works with `open.rule_of_the_world` when the implication opens the piece.
**Best in:** formats: novel, feature, documentary, short film, reel | genres: science fiction, fantasy, historical, dystopia, heritage
**Avoid when:** precision matters to the plot outcome. If the audience must know the succession law exactly, implying it produces three readings and two of them break the ending. Implication is for texture and pressure, not for the rule the climax turns on.
**Example:**
> Explained: "Under the occupation, all printing required a state licence stamped on every page."
> Implied: "The pamphlet had no stamp in the corner. He read it standing up, near a window, ready to eat it."

**Yields to:** `expo.rule_by_demonstration` — where precision matters to the plot outcome. If the audience must know the succession law exactly, implying it produces three readings and two of them break the ending.  
`expo.implication_worldbuilding`

## Delivery by Demonstration

### Rule by Demonstration

**Also called:** show the law, mechanic-in-action, the demonstration scene
**What it is:** A rule of the world is established by staging one clean instance of it working, rather than by anyone stating it.
**Effect on the audience:** They learn the rule as an observation, which they trust, instead of as a claim, which they have to take on faith.
**Used for and where it works best:** Magic systems, technologies, social codes, institutional procedure — anything with a cause and an effect. The craft rule: the demonstration must be *unambiguous and single-variable*. One rule per demonstration, with cause and effect in the same shot or the same paragraph, and nothing else new competing for attention. Two rules in one demonstration and the audience learns neither.
**Best in:** formats: feature, series, novel, game, animation | genres: fantasy, science fiction, action, sport, heist
**Avoid when:** the rule is abstract, statistical or legal — "inheritance passes through the mother's line" has no single stageable instance, and forcing one produces a contrived scene. Say those plainly through a character who has a reason to say them; see `dial.exposition_correction`.
**Example:**
> Stated: "Iron blocks their magic."
> Demonstrated: "She reached for the latch and stopped an inch short, the way you stop at a hot stove. He looked at the latch. Iron. He put his hand flat on it, and nothing happened to him at all."

**Yields to:** `dial.exposition_correction` — where the rule is abstract, statistical or legal. "Inheritance passes through the mother's line" has no single stageable instance; say it plainly through a character who has a reason to say it.  
`expo.rule_by_demonstration`

### Show the Rule's Price

**Also called:** cost demonstration, the tax scene
**What it is:** The second demonstration of a rule shows what it costs to use, immediately after the first showed what it does.
**Effect on the audience:** The rule becomes dramatic instead of decorative. A power with a price generates suspense every time it becomes available.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any invented capability — magic, tech, a talent, an alliance, a drug. The craft rule: deliver the price on a *minor* character or a low-stakes use, so the audience has already learned the tax before the protagonist faces paying it. Establishing the cost in the same scene where the hero must pay it makes the rule feel invented on the spot.
**Best in:** formats: feature, series, novel, game | genres: fantasy, science fiction, horror, superhero, thriller
**Avoid when:** the capability is meant to feel free and the *absence* of cost is the point — comedy, wish-fulfilment, some children's work. Taxing a joke kills it.
**Example:**
> "He healed the boy's arm in under a minute. Then he sat down on the step and didn't get up again until dark, and when he did, his hair had gone the colour of the bandage."

`expo.rule_cost_demo`

### The Competent-Character Walkthrough

**Also called:** the orientation tour, the induction scene, the briefing walk
**What it is:** An expert who belongs in the world moves through it doing their job, and the audience learns the world from the order in which the expert touches things.
**Effect on the audience:** The world reads as functional rather than described. Competence is also charismatic, so the exposition carries pleasure.
**Used for and where it works best:** Institutions, ships, hospitals, workshops, kitchens, excavation sites. The craft rule: the expert must be *doing the job, not narrating it*. Attach a task with a deadline — the tour is the route to the task, never the purpose of the scene. Give the walker a want (`scene.give_a_want`) and the exposition becomes a by-product. If the expert stops to explain for the audience's benefit, you have slid into `dial.known_information`.
**Best in:** formats: feature, series, documentary, novel, game | genres: procedural, science fiction, workplace drama, war, heritage
**Avoid when:** the world's strangeness should feel threatening. Competence reassures; a walkthrough by someone who knows what everything is tells the audience there is nothing to fear. For dread, use the newcomer instead.
**Example:**
> "She signed the board, took the middle torch off the rack — never the end one, the end one's for the inspector — and went down. Forty rungs. At thirty she started counting out loud, because that is what you do when the air is going to be bad."

**Yields to:** `expo.newcomer_eye` — where the world's strangeness should feel threatening. Competence reassures: a walkthrough by someone who knows what everything is tells the audience there is nothing to fear.  
`expo.competent_walkthrough`

### The Newcomer's Eye

**Also called:** fish out of water, the audience surrogate, the initiate
**What it is:** A character who does not know the world arrives in it, and the questions the audience would ask become that character's questions.
**Effect on the audience:** Permission to be confused. Their ignorance is represented on screen, so being told things does not feel condescending.
**Used for and where it works best:** Maximum-strangeness worlds where almost nothing can be assumed. The craft rule: the newcomer must *want* something from the world, not merely observe it, or the device collapses into a guided tour. And they must be wrong at least once — a newcomer whose questions are always the right questions is a microphone stand, not a character.
**Best in:** formats: feature, series pilot, novel, animation | genres: fantasy, science fiction, coming of age, crime, migration
**Avoid when:** the piece is about people already inside the world and the newcomer has to be invented to carry information. That character will always feel bolted on. It also cannot deliver anything the insiders would find too obvious to mention — for those, use environmental delivery.
**Example:**
> Surrogate as microphone: "So the Guild controls the water?"
> Surrogate as character: "I paid the man at the pump." Everyone at the table stopped eating. "You paid him. With money."

**Yields to:** `expo.competent_walkthrough` — where the piece is about people already inside the world and the newcomer would have to be invented; and `expo.environmental_detail` for anything the insiders would find too obvious to mention.  
`expo.newcomer_eye`

### Exposition Under Pressure

**Also called:** exposition on the run, the compressed briefing
**What it is:** Necessary information is delivered while something urgent is happening, so the audience receives it in a state of attention they did not have to be asked for.
**Effect on the audience:** The facts land as stakes rather than as background, and the scene never stops moving.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any block of unavoidable hard information — a plan, a diagnosis, a history that genuinely must be stated. The craft rule: the pressure must be able to *interrupt* the exposition. If the speaker can finish every sentence, the pressure is decorative. Cut the last third of the explanation with an event and let the audience carry the unfinished sentence forward. The dialogue-level cousins are `dial.exposition_accusation` and `dial.expert_explains_badly`; this card is the scene-design layer under them.
**Best in:** formats: feature, series, thriller novel, game | genres: thriller, action, medical, war, heist
**Avoid when:** the information is emotionally heavy and needs stillness. Delivering a death, a confession or a diagnosis over a chase cheapens both. Pressure is for mechanism, not for grief.
**Example:**
> "Third door — no, third — it's a sequence lock, four digits, and it resets every ninety seconds from the hour, so if we're not through by—" The corridor lights went out. "Now. If we're not through now."

**Yields to:** `expo.resistant_listener` — The scene needs heat rather than speed.  
`expo.exposition_under_pressure`

## Terminology, Jargon and Apparatus

### Coined Term in Context

**Also called:** context-clue naming, the unglossed word, learn-by-use
**What it is:** An invented word is used correctly in a sentence whose surrounding meaning is clear, and never defined.
**Effect on the audience:** They absorb the word the way they absorbed words as children — from placement — and feel fluent rather than tutored.
**Used for and where it works best:** All invented vocabulary. The craft rule: give each new term three unglossed uses in *different* grammatical positions before it carries any weight — once as an object, once as something a character does, once as something a character feels about. By the third the meaning is fixed and no definition was ever needed. Never define a coined word in the sentence that introduces it; the appositive comma ("a serrin, a kind of debt-priest, entered") is the tell of a writer who does not trust the reader.
**Best in:** formats: novel, series, feature, game | genres: science fiction, fantasy, historical, crime, military
**Avoid when:** the term is a plot-critical mechanism with a precise definition. A rule the ending depends on cannot be left to inference. Also avoid stacking: two unglossed coinages in one sentence produce noise, not fluency.
**Example:**
> Glossed: "She checked the chrono, a wrist-mounted timepiece."
> In context: "She checked the chrono. Four minutes. She checked it again, which did not help, and the numbers did not care."

**Yields to:** `expo.jargon_threshold` — where the term is a plot-critical mechanism with a precise definition. A rule the ending depends on cannot be left to inference, so the term gets taught rather than absorbed.  
`expo.coined_term_in_context`

### The Jargon Threshold

**Also called:** how much to teach, the working-vocabulary limit
**What it is:** A deliberate decision about which technical terms the audience will be taught and which will simply wash over them as authentic noise.
**Effect on the audience:** Correctly set, the world sounds real and stays followable. The audience understands the terms that matter and lets the rest pass without anxiety.
**Used for and where it works best:** Procedurals, military, medical, legal, aviation, any craft. The craft rule: teach only the terms the audience must *use to follow a decision*. Everything else stays untranslated on purpose — and the sign the threshold is set right is that every taught term recurs at least three times across the piece, while no untaught one is ever needed to make a scene make sense.
**Best in:** formats: series, feature, documentary, novel | genres: medical, legal, military, crime, sport
**Avoid when:** writing for an audience of practitioners, who will find the taught terms patronising, or for young children, where unexplained noise reads as failure rather than texture.
**Example:**
> "Pressure's dropping — get me two units O-neg and a level-one infuser."
> The audience never learns what a level-one infuser is. They learn that it is fast, and that fast is what is needed.

**Yields to:** `expo.kids_repetition_ladder` — for young children, where unexplained noise reads as failure rather than texture and a word is taught by return rather than by threshold.  
`expo.jargon_threshold`

### Familiar Anchor, One Strange Word

**Also called:** the one-weird-thing rule, ordinary sentence with a strange noun
**What it is:** A sentence built from entirely ordinary syntax, ordinary verbs and ordinary emotion, carrying exactly one unfamiliar element.
**Effect on the audience:** The strange element becomes vivid because everything around it is stable. Comprehension costs almost nothing.
**Used for and where it works best:** First contact with any invented world, and the opening pages above all. The craft rule: the anchor must be a *bodily or domestic* familiarity — hunger, cold, a queue, a bill, a bad knee. Strangeness anchored to another abstraction just doubles the abstraction.
**Best in:** formats: novel, short story, feature, animation, reel | genres: science fiction, fantasy, magical realism, horror
**Avoid when:** disorientation is the primary effect you want — the opening of a psychological horror or a fever sequence wants the opposite, several unanchored strangenesses at once. It also fails if used for every sentence: an endlessly calm register flattens the real escalations later.
**Example:**
> Unanchored: "The vashen-tide swept the low quarter and the gull-priests declaimed the seventh renunciation."
> Anchored: "The tide came up through the floorboards again. Her mother put the bread on the high shelf and told the priest, politely, to shut up."

**Yields to:** `expo.deferred_explanation` — Disorientation is the effect you want.  
`expo.familiar_anchor`

### The Glossary Crutch

**Also called:** back-matter dependency, the flip-to-the-appendix problem
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** The narrative is unreadable without the glossary, so the audience is expected to leave the story, look a word up, and come back.
**Effect on the audience:** The spell breaks at every lookup. Readers who don't flip stay confused; readers who do flip stop reading the book and start studying it. Most do neither and put it down.
**Used for and where it works best:** —
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** always, as a dependency. A glossary is legitimate as a *bonus* for the enthusiast; it becomes a defect the moment a first-time reader needs it to follow a scene. The diagnostic: hand chapter one to someone with the back matter removed. Every place they stop is a place the prose failed, and the fix is in the prose, never in a longer glossary. Note that a film has no glossary at all, which is why adaptations of glossary-dependent books must solve this or die.
**Example:**
> Dependent: "He refused the naming-gift, so by ashkar he owed the household a season's teind."
> Self-supporting: "He refused the gift. His aunt started counting on her fingers — spring, summer, autumn — and he knew exactly what he now owed the house, and for how long."

**Yields to:** `expo.optional_appendix` — where the apparatus is a reward for the enthusiast rather than a dependency. The diagnostic is the same either way: hand chapter one to someone with the back matter removed.  
`expo.glossary_crutch`

### The Optional Appendix

**Also called:** back matter, the map, the family tree, the timeline
**What it is:** Supplementary apparatus — map, glossary, chronology, dramatis personae — offered as a reward for the reader who wants more, while the narrative stays complete without it.
**Effect on the audience:** Enthusiasts get depth to explore and a sense that the world was built rather than improvised. Everyone else loses nothing.
**Used for and where it works best:** Long fantasy, historical and multi-generational novels, series bibles, documentary companion material. The craft rule: write the apparatus *after* the narrative is finished and self-sufficient, never before — building it first tempts you to lean on it. A map is also a promise: readers expect to travel to most of the places on it, so leave off the continents you never visit.
**Best in:** formats: novel, series bible, non-fiction book, game narrative | genres: epic fantasy, historical, science fiction, heritage
**Avoid when:** the apparatus exists to patch confusion you could not solve in the prose — that is `expo.glossary_crutch` in a nicer jacket. Also avoid in short forms and in anything filmed, where there is nowhere to put it. An elaborate dramatis personae at the front of a 200-page novel signals a cast you failed to differentiate.
**Example:**
> A reader who never opens the map still knows the river runs south and the enemy is upstream, because a character complained about rowing.

**Yields to:** `expo.back_matter_craft` — Non-fiction making factual claims.  
`expo.optional_appendix`

## Visual and Environmental Delivery

### Environmental Exposition

**Also called:** environmental storytelling, the set tells it, props as history
**What it is:** The physical space carries the information — wear patterns, repairs, what is hoarded, what is thrown away, what has been painted over.
**Effect on the audience:** They read the space unconsciously and arrive at conclusions they believe are their own.
**Used for and where it works best:** Film, animation, games, and prose in a close third person. The craft rule: exposition lives in *evidence of use and of time*, not in inventory. A described room is a list; a room with a patched wall, a chair worn on one arm and a door that opens only halfway is a history. Pick the three details that imply a chronology and drop the rest.
**Best in:** formats: feature, series, game, animation, documentary, reel | genres: science fiction, horror, period drama, heritage, crime
**Avoid when:** the audience's attention is committed elsewhere — nobody reads the set during a chase or during a confession. It is also weak for abstract information: a room cannot state a law of inheritance.
**Example:**
> Inventory: "The workshop held a lathe, a bench, tools on the wall and a stove."
> Evidence: "Every tool had a painted outline on the wall. Two outlines were empty, and had been empty long enough that the paint inside them had gone the same grey as the paint outside."

**Yields to:** `dial.exposition_correction` — for anything abstract. A room can carry a state and cannot state a law of inheritance, so that one goes to a character with a reason to say it.  
`expo.environmental_detail`

### Diegetic Text

**Also called:** signage, in-world documents, the read-through, screen text
**What it is:** Information reaches the audience through text that exists inside the world — a notice, a headline, a ration card, a phone screen, a carved inscription.
**Effect on the audience:** Facts arrive in an institutional voice, which reads as objective and costs no character any credibility.
**Used for and where it works best:** Regimes, bureaucracies, wars, schools, hospitals, anywhere authority speaks in writing. The craft rule: the document must be *addressed to someone inside the world*, in that world's register, never to the audience — the moment a sign explains something an inhabitant already knows, it is `dial.on_the_nose` in printed form. Keep it to a length that can genuinely be read in the time you hold on it: roughly eight words per second on screen.
**Best in:** formats: feature, series, reel, short film, game, documentary | genres: dystopia, war, period, satire, horror, heritage
**Avoid when:** the audience cannot be assumed to read — young children's work, or a piece designed for sound-off viewing where captions already occupy the same screen space. Also poor for anything the authority would have reason to hide.
**Example:**
> Written for the audience: "NOTICE: Under the Emergency Powers Act, all citizens must carry papers at all times."
> Written for the world: "PAPERS READY. HAVE THEM OPEN. DO NOT MAKE THE QUEUE WAIT."

**Yields to:** `expo.environmental_detail` — where the audience cannot be assumed to read: young children's work, or sound-off viewing where captions already occupy the same screen space.  
`expo.diegetic_text`

### The Object That States the Rule

**Also called:** the loaded prop, costume as law, the telling object
**What it is:** A single object or item of dress encodes a social rule, a rank or a history, and the rule is delivered the first time someone reacts to it.
**Effect on the audience:** One frame or one clause carries what would otherwise be a paragraph of social exposition.
**Used for and where it works best:** Hierarchies, castes, guilds, religions, armies, families. The craft rule: deliver the rule through the *reaction*, not the object. A described sash is costume; a room going quiet when the sash enters is law. Establish the reaction once, cleanly, and the object works silently for the rest of the piece.
**Best in:** formats: feature, series, novel, animation, heritage video | genres: historical, fantasy, period drama, military, heritage
**Avoid when:** the piece has so many such tokens that the audience is asked to hold a heraldry chart. Two or three encoded objects across a feature is plenty; ten is a costume department showing off.
**Example:**
> "He wore the red cord. Nobody at the table said anything about it, and nobody reached for the bread until he had taken his."

`expo.object_tells_rule`

### The Off-Screen World

**Also called:** sound as worldbuilding, the world beyond the frame, implied elsewhere
**What it is:** The world outside the scene is delivered through sound, interruption and reported events — traffic, artillery, a public address system, someone shouting two floors down.
**Effect on the audience:** A sense that the story occupies a corner of something much larger and indifferent, achieved with no budget and no exposition.
**Used for and where it works best:** Single-location pieces, war, city stories, radio and podcast drama, any low-budget production that must feel large. The craft rule: the off-screen event must occasionally *change* what happens on screen — characters pause, lower their voices, move away from a window. A soundscape that never affects behaviour is wallpaper.
**Best in:** formats: feature, radio drama, podcast, series, short film, game | genres: war, thriller, science fiction, horror, kitchen sink
**Avoid when:** the story's point is isolation and there should be nothing out there. Also avoid in noisy viewing contexts — phone-speaker short-form loses almost all of this, so never put a load-bearing fact in it.
**Example:**
> "They kept talking. Somewhere north of the river something heavy landed, and the cups moved a little, and they kept talking, but quieter, and neither of them mentioned it."

**Yields to:** `expo.short_form_frontload` — in phone-speaker short form, which loses almost all of this. Never put a load-bearing fact in a channel the audience cannot hear.  
`expo.offscreen_world`

## Placement and Openings

### The Prologue of History

**Also called:** the history opening, the mythic preamble, the voice-of-god prologue
**What it is:** The piece opens with a compressed account of events preceding the story — a war, a founding, a fall — delivered by narration, montage or omniscient text.
**Effect on the audience:** Immediate scale, and a frame to read the first scenes against. Also immediate distance: nobody has been met yet, so nothing is at stake.
**Used for and where it works best:** Epics where the *present* situation is incomprehensible without a prior event, and where that event is genuinely spectacular. The craft rule: no more than ninety seconds or a page and a half, built from images and consequences rather than names and dates, and it must end on the single fact the first scene depends on. One of the honest uses of `open.backstory_open` — and if the prologue is a list of kingdoms and treaties, you have written `expo.lore_dump` with a score under it.
**Best in:** formats: feature, series pilot, novel, animation, documentary | genres: epic fantasy, science fiction, war, historical, heritage
**Avoid when:** the present-day story can carry its own explanation, which is most of the time. The failure mode is a prologue that hands the audience the outcome of a conflict before they care who was in it — the story then spends an hour catching up to information they are already bored of holding.
**Example:**
> Names and dates: "In the year 412, the Eleven Houses signed the Concord of Ash, ending the Salt Wars."
> Images and consequence: "They ended the war by poisoning the wells. Both sides agreed it had worked. Three generations later nobody living had tasted clean water, and everybody living knew whose fault that was."

**Yields to:** `expo.need_to_know_ladder` — where the present-day story can carry its own explanation, which is most of the time. A prologue that hands over the outcome of a conflict before anyone cares who was in it costs you an hour of catching up.  
`expo.history_prologue`

### Deferred Explanation

**Also called:** in medias res, start late and explain later, the cold open
**What it is:** The piece starts inside a situation the audience cannot fully parse, and the explanation arrives after they are already invested.
**Effect on the audience:** Forward pull. Confusion becomes a question rather than a complaint — provided the emotional situation is clear even while the factual one is not.
**Used for and where it works best:** Almost any opening, and mandatory where the setup is more interesting than its explanation. The craft rule: the audience must always understand *who wants what right now*, even when they understand nothing else. Factual confusion is tolerable for a long time; emotional confusion is tolerable for about thirty seconds. Pairs with `info.delayed_decoding` when the withheld explanation is itself the reveal.
**Best in:** formats: feature, series, novel, short film, reel | genres: thriller, crime, science fiction, comedy, mystery
**Avoid when:** the confusion has no resolution date. Deferred explanation is a debt and the audience tracks it. Defer past the midpoint with no partial payment and they stop assuming you have an answer.
**Example:**
> "She was already halfway across the yard with the boy on her hip before the sirens started, which told anyone watching that she had known first, and had decided not to tell them."

**Yields to:** `expo.need_to_know_ladder` — where the confusion has no resolution date. Deferral is a debt the audience tracks, and past the midpoint with no partial payment they stop assuming you have an answer.  
`expo.deferred_explanation`

### Absorb the Exposition into the Argument

**Also called:** exposition as ammunition, fact as weapon, contested history
**What it is:** A necessary fact is placed inside a disagreement, so the audience receives it as a move in a fight rather than as information.
**Effect on the audience:** They lean in. Facts deployed to win something are interesting in a way that facts offered neutrally never are.
**Used for and where it works best:** Backstory, history and institutional rules — anything two characters could plausibly disagree about the meaning of. The craft rule: the speakers must disagree about the *significance* of the fact while agreeing on the fact itself, which lets them state it without either seeming to inform the other. This is the scene-level partner of `dial.exposition_accusation`, and the mechanism behind `scene.absorb_and_delete` and `char.backstory_economy`.
**Best in:** formats: feature, series, novel, stage play, podcast drama | genres: drama, legal, political, family drama, crime
**Avoid when:** the information is neutral and nobody could reasonably care — manufacturing a fight to deliver a train timetable is worse than stating the timetable. Also avoid stacking more than two facts into one argument; the fight stops being a fight and becomes a delivery vehicle, which audiences hear instantly.
**Example:**
> Neutral: "Our father left the farm to your brother."
> Absorbed: "He left it to Sami." — "He left it to the eldest son. There's a difference, and you've spent nine years refusing to see it."

**Yields to:** `dial.exposition_correction` — where the information is neutral and nobody could reasonably care. Manufacturing a fight to deliver a train timetable is worse than a character simply being wrong about the time.  
`expo.absorb_into_argument`

## Nonfiction, Technical and Short-Form

### The Narration Contract

**Also called:** the documentary voice, the narrator's remit
**What it is:** A decided, consistent rule for what the narrator may say — facts only, or facts plus interpretation, or facts plus opinion — held for the whole piece.
**Effect on the audience:** Trust. An audience calibrates to a narrator's remit within about a minute, and is jolted whenever it silently widens.
**Used for and where it works best:** Documentary, heritage films, explainers, audio features. The craft rule: write the contract down in one sentence before scripting, then check every line against it. The commonest breach is drift — a narrator who reported facts for six minutes and then starts telling the audience how to feel, which reads as manipulation precisely because the earlier voice earned trust.
**Best in:** formats: documentary, explainer, heritage video, podcast, voiceover script | genres: history, science, heritage, true crime, nature
**Avoid when:** the piece is built on an unreliable or evolving narrator, where the drift *is* the design. Do that on purpose and signal it, or the audience reads it as incompetence.
**Example:**
> Contract: *the narrator states what is known and names who knows it; interpretation belongs to interviewees.*
> In practice: "Two excavation reports describe the room. Neither mentions a door." — not: "Strangely, no door is mentioned, as if someone wanted the room sealed."

**Yields to:** `info.unreliable_narrator` — where the piece is built on a narrator whose drift *is* the design. Do that on purpose and signal it, or the audience reads it as incompetence.  
`expo.doc_narration_contract`

### The Explainer Scaffold

**Also called:** question-first structure, promise-and-payoff explainer
**What it is:** Every unit of explanation opens with the question it answers, so the audience holds an open loop while the facts arrive.
**Effect on the audience:** Facts land in a slot that already exists. Retention rises sharply compared with the same facts delivered as statements.
**Used for and where it works best:** Explainers, educational video, technical documentation, museum text, training. The craft rule: each question must be one the audience would actually ask, phrased in their words, not in the subject's vocabulary — "why does bread go hard?" not "the mechanism of starch retrogradation." Then answer it completely before opening the next. Never hold two open questions at once.
**Best in:** formats: explainer, documentary, non-fiction book, educational, reel | genres: science, history, medical
**Avoid when:** the audience is already expert and the questions are beneath them, or the material is narrative rather than explanatory — question-scaffolding a story flattens it into a lecture with the suspense removed.
**Example:**
> Statement-first: "Starch retrogradation begins immediately after baking."
> Question-first: "Bread goes hard in a day. Here's the odd part: it isn't drying out. Wrap it in plastic and it still goes hard."

**Yields to:** `expo.rule_by_demonstration` — where the material is narrative rather than explanatory. Question-scaffolding a story flattens it into a lecture with the suspense removed.  
`expo.explainer_scaffold`

### Procedural Exposition

**Also called:** technical delivery, the how-it-works scene, mechanism exposition
**What it is:** A process, technology or procedure is explained by the order in which it is performed, with the explanation following the hands.
**Effect on the audience:** They follow complicated mechanisms without a diagram, because sequence is easier to hold than structure.
**Used for and where it works best:** Heists, surgeries, launches, repairs, crafts, investigations. The craft rule: explain in *action order*, never in system order — "first you do this, then this fails, and that's why you do that" — and stop at the level of detail the plot actually uses. A step the story never depends on gets one clause, or none. Accuracy beyond that point is for the writer, and belongs under `expo.iceberg_principle`.
**Best in:** formats: feature, series, documentary, novel, game narrative | genres: heist, medical, procedural, science fiction, science, war
**Avoid when:** the process's outcome matters but its mechanism doesn't. Then compress to a result and a cost. The failure mode here is the writer who researched for six weeks and cannot bear to lose any of it — `expo.lore_dump` in a lab coat.
**Example:**
> System order: "The lock is a four-wheel combination mechanism with a drive cam and a fence."
> Action order: "You turn until you feel the first wheel pick up the next one. That little click in your fingers is the lock telling you something it isn't supposed to."

**Yields to:** `expo.rule_cost_demo` — where the process's outcome matters and its mechanism does not. Compress to a result and a cost; the alternative is six weeks of research nobody can bear to lose.  
`expo.procedural_exposition`

### Short-Form Front-Loading

**Also called:** context in the first three seconds, the zero-runtime problem — short *video*, not short prose (for flash fiction see `struct.flash_fiction`, for the prose poem `lyric.prose_poem`)
**What it is:** In a piece too short to earn patience, the entire orienting context is compressed into the first line and the first image, and everything else is cut.
**Effect on the audience:** They know where they are before they decide whether to stay, which is the only decision that matters in short-form.
**Used for and where it works best:** Reels, Shorts, TikTok, trailers, ads. The craft rule: one fact, one image, one sentence — who, where, and why it is strange — then straight into the content. Context that arrives at second eight arrives after the scroll. And the strongest short-form context is *visual*, because a large share of viewers watch with sound off: use `expo.diegetic_text` or a burned line, not narration.
**Best in:** formats: reel, short, TikTok, trailer, ad, title sequence, explainer | genres: heritage, comedy, history, advertising
**Avoid when:** the hook depends on withholding where you are — some short-form works precisely by disorienting for four seconds and then revealing. That is a different structure, and it costs you a share of the audience, knowingly.
**Example:**
> Slow: "Today I want to talk about something from ancient Mesopotamia that most people have never heard of."
> Front-loaded, over a shot of the object: "This is a receipt. It's four thousand years old, and it's a complaint about bad copper."

**Yields to:** `expo.deferred_explanation` — Hook depends on withholding where you are.  
`expo.short_form_frontload`

### Heritage Specificity

**Also called:** the one true detail, exposition by artefact
**What it is:** Historical or cultural information is delivered through a single verifiable concrete detail — a price, a tool, a measure, a named practice — instead of a general description of the period.
**Effect on the audience:** Authority. A specific detail they did not know reads as research; a general description reads as textbook paraphrase, even when both are accurate.
**Used for and where it works best:** Historical drama, heritage video, documentary, museum and educational work. The craft rule: prefer details at human scale — what something cost, how long it took, who did the work, what it smelled like — over dynastic and political summary. And carry only details you can source, because the specificity that buys authority also makes an error catastrophic: a wrong particular discredits the whole piece in a way a vague generality never does.
**Best in:** formats: documentary, heritage video, feature film, non-fiction book, reel | genres: history, heritage, biography
**Avoid when:** the detail is contested among specialists and the piece has no room to flag uncertainty. Stating a disputed particular as settled fact is how heritage work loses the audience that knows most. Also avoid density: three sourced particulars per segment, not fifteen.
**Example:**
> General: "Life in the city was organised around the temple economy."
> Specific: "A brewer's wage was recorded in barley, by volume. A tablet from the same year records a dispute over three litres of it."

**Yields to:** `expo.implication_worldbuilding` — where the detail is contested among specialists and the piece has no room to flag uncertainty. Stating a disputed particular as settled fact is how heritage work loses the audience that knows most.  
`expo.heritage_specificity`

## Named Failures

### The Lore Dump

**Also called:** infodump, the worldbuilding download, the bible on the page
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A large block of background — history, cosmology, politics, taxonomy — delivered at once, ahead of any need for it, because the writer enjoys the world more than the story.
**Effect on the audience:** They skim. What they skim they do not retain, so the dump does not even achieve its own purpose — and readers who skim once learn that skimming is safe in this text and keep doing it.
**Used for and where it works best:** —
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** always, in this form. The tell is structural, not stylistic: the story stops. Diagnostic — delete the block entirely and read the following ten pages. If they still work, the block was never exposition, it was your notes. If two sentences are missed, those two sentences were the exposition; put them on the `expo.need_to_know_ladder` and bin the rest. Beautiful prose does not rescue this; it only makes the dump longer.
**Example:**
> Dump: "The Assembly had governed since the fall of the second dynasty, when the river houses, weakened by two decades of drought, ceded their charter rights in exchange for grain guarantees that were never honoured…"
> Kept: "The Assembly still owed her family grain from a famine nobody alive remembered. She mentioned it at every session. It had become a kind of joke, except to her."

**Yields to:** `expo.need_to_know_ladder` — always. Delete the block and read the following ten pages: if two sentences are missed, those two were the exposition. Put them on the ladder and bin the rest.  
`expo.lore_dump`

### The "As You Know" Family

**Also called:** As You Know Bob, maid-and-butler dialogue, the mutual briefing
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** Two characters tell each other things they both already know, so the audience can overhear. The phrase "as you know" is only the most honest version; the family includes the unprompted reminder, the recap between equals, and the rhetorical question asked of someone who obviously knows the answer.
**Effect on the audience:** The fourth wall thins. They feel written at, and the characters briefly stop existing and become employees of the plot.
**Used for and where it works best:** —
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** always. The failure is not the phrase, it is the missing motive — nobody in the scene needs the information, so the only reason it is spoken is the audience. The repair is never to delete "as you know"; it is to give one speaker a reason to say it: a disagreement (`dial.exposition_accusation`), an error to correct (`dial.exposition_correction`), or a listener who genuinely does not know. See `dial.known_information` for the line-level diagnosis.
**Example:**
> Failure: "As you know, Father, the mine has been closed since the accident."
> Repair: "You still say 'the accident.'" — "What would you like me to call it?" — "Eleven men. I'd like you to call it eleven men."

**Yields to:** `dial.exposition_accusation` and `dial.exposition_correction` — always. The failure is not the phrase, it is the missing motive: give one speaker a reason to say it, a disagreement or an error to correct.  
`expo.as_you_know`

### The Proper Noun Avalanche

**Also called:** name fog, the unearned index, page-one bombardment
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** Invented names arrive faster than the audience can attach meaning to any of them, usually in the first pages or first minutes, where there is no scaffold at all.
**Effect on the audience:** They stop trying. Names become noise — and once an audience has decided names are noise, they will also miss the three that mattered, so the avalanche damages the rest of the piece, not just its own paragraph.
**Used for and where it works best:** —
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** always, outside a deliberate and brief overwhelm effect. Diagnostic: circle every proper noun on the first page and ask of each whether the audience will need to recognise it again within twenty pages. Everything that fails becomes a description. See `expo.proper_noun_budget` for the working limit.
**Example:**
> Avalanche: "Marek of Hess rode with Duna and the Ashgar levies to meet Calen at Torrin Ford, where the Verath had crossed in the spring."
> Fixed: "Marek rode out with what men the valley could spare. They were going to the ford. Someone had crossed it in the spring, and nobody could agree who."

**Yields to:** `expo.proper_noun_budget` — always, outside a deliberate and brief overwhelm. Circle every proper noun on the first page and ask whether the audience will need to recognise it again within twenty pages; everything that fails becomes a description.  
`expo.proper_noun_avalanche`

## Repair

### The Deletion Test

**Also called:** cut it and see, the survival test
**What it is:** You delete a passage of exposition outright and read forward without restoring it, marking only the places where the story actually breaks.
**Effect on the audience:** Not something they experience — a diagnostic. Its effect on the piece is that most of the deleted material never comes back.
**Used for and where it works best:** Every second draft, and mandatory on any passage longer than half a page of pure information. The craft rule: read *forward* after cutting, never re-read the cut. Writers who reread the deleted block always find it necessary. The typical result on a lore-heavy manuscript is that about a tenth of the cut material is genuinely needed — the iceberg ratio arriving from the other direction.
**Best in:** formats: novel, screenplay, documentary, explainer | genres: any
**Avoid when:** you are testing a plant rather than an explanation. A plant is *supposed* to pass unnoticed, so deleting it breaks nothing until the payoff — which is why `info.buried_plant` must be checked against its payoff, not against the next ten pages.
**Example:**
> Deleted: three paragraphs on the guild's founding.
> Restored after the test: one clause — "the guild had been founded by people who lost a war, and it showed in how they wrote contracts."

**Yields to:** `info.buried_plant` — where you are testing a plant rather than an explanation. A plant is *supposed* to pass unnoticed, so it is checked against its payoff, not against the next ten pages.  
`expo.deletion_test`

### Move It Downstream

**Also called:** relocate the fact, exposition triage by position
**What it is:** Rather than rewriting a clumsy block of exposition, you move it to a later scene where the audience has a reason to want it.
**Effect on the audience:** The same sentences that read as a lecture at minute four read as a revelation at minute forty, because a question is now open.
**Used for and where it works best:** The first and cheapest repair for any exposition problem — try position before you try craft. The craft rule: find the scene where a character *asks* the question the block answers, or where the audience visibly needs it, and move the block to just before it. Very often the block also halves on arrival, because half of it was orienting material the later scene no longer requires.
**Best in:** formats: novel, screenplay, series, documentary, explainer | genres: any
**Avoid when:** the fact must be in place before a plant pays off, or when moving it creates an unexplained stretch the audience cannot ride. Some information genuinely has to be early; the point is that far less of it than writers assume.
**Example:**
> Page 6: "The house had been in probate for two years and neither sister could sell without the other."
> Moved to page 90, where one sister has just listed it: the same sentence, now a detonation.

**Yields to:** `info.buried_plant` — where the fact must be in place before a plant pays off, or where moving it leaves an unexplained stretch the audience cannot ride. Some information genuinely has to be early; far less of it than writers assume.  
`expo.relocate_downstream`

### Give the Listener a Reason to Resist

**Also called:** the hostile recipient, contested exposition
**What it is:** The character receiving the information does not want it, disputes it, or is trying to end the conversation, so every fact has to be forced through resistance.
**Effect on the audience:** Tension where there was none. Resistance turns a transfer of information into a small contest with a winner.
**Used for and where it works best:** The standard repair for a briefing scene that has gone flat, and for any scene where one character must explain at length. The craft rule: the resistance must be *specific and reasonable* — "I don't have time," "that's not what the file says," "you told me this last year and you were wrong" — because generic reluctance only slows the scene. Let the resister win a point; exposition that survives a real objection is exposition the audience believes.
**Best in:** formats: feature, series, novel, stage play, podcast drama | genres: thriller, drama, legal, science fiction, procedural
**Avoid when:** the explanation is long and the plot needs it delivered cleanly and fast. Resistance costs runtime, and a resisted three-minute briefing becomes a six-minute scene. Use `expo.exposition_under_pressure` instead when speed matters more than heat.
**Example:**
> Compliant: "So the transfers went through a shell company?" — "Exactly."
> Resisting: "Shell company." — "You've said that about every account you couldn't read." — "This one has a director who died in 2019." — "…Say that part again."

**Yields to:** `expo.exposition_under_pressure` — where the explanation is long and the plot needs it clean and fast. Resistance costs runtime: a resisted three-minute briefing becomes a six-minute scene.  
`expo.resistant_listener`
