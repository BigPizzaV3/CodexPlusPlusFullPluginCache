## The Information Map of a Whole Work

### Name the Gap Before the Device

**Also called:** the information selector, who holds it, where the gap runs, the position question
**What it is:** The card at the head of the `info.` shelf. It does not describe a technique. It asks the three questions the shelf's own pair graph asks over and over — **where does the gap run, who decides when it closes, and is this a state, a moment or a rule about placement** — and hands back a named shortlist for each answer. The axes were not invented: they were read off the fifty-five card-to-card `Yields to:` conditions this shelf carries, and the conditions that repeated became the questions. Every card cited here is cited by id.
**Effect on the audience:** None. This card never reaches the screen. It exists so that the card that does was chosen against the scene, the source and the clock, rather than picked because irony is the first thing anybody thinks of.
**Used for and where it works best:** Answer the three questions **in order and out loud, one sentence each, before you open the shelf**. The first eliminates most of the file; the second and third leave a shortlist of three to six. If you cannot answer question one — *between whom does this gap run* — you do not have an information problem yet, you have a scene with nobody in it, and the fix is upstream. Where two answers fight over one beat, that is two gaps, and one of them belongs to a different scene. And note the order: **whose fact it is comes before how you stage it**, because a fact you did not invent will not move to suit your act break.
**Best in:** formats: all | genres: all
**Avoid when:** **The piece is reportage and the facts are somebody else's.** This map's second axis is the one the pair graph cannot help you with — of the twelve reachable cards in the publication region, only two are entered by an edge from outside that region, so on a real-world piece the map is running on one axis while pretending to run on three. Read those cards directly rather than routing to them. Also **distrust axis one whenever your honest answer is its second value**: thirty-one of the seventy-five live there, so *level* is where this map stops narrowing and starts listing. And do not run it on a piece with no reveal and no withheld fact at all — a straight explainer, a demonstration, a recipe — where every answer comes out *level, yours, a rule* and the map has told you nothing you did not bring to it.
**Example:**
> *A heritage documentary. The scene is a survivor describing a night in a village that no longer exists.*
> **Where does the gap run?** Not between us and her. Between what she says and what can be established — `info.credibility_ladder` and its neighbours, not `info.dramatic_irony`.
> **Who decides when it closes?** Not me. The material does: there is one photograph and no register. `info.archive_as_reveal` if the photograph lands, `info.contradictory_sources` if it does not.
> **State, moment or placement?** A moment, and the only one in the sequence.
> *Shortlist of three. The irony position I had drafted was never on it.*

**The axes**

**Axis 1 — Between whom does the gap run, and which way?**
Asked first, because it is the condition the shelf repeats most: seventeen of the fifty-five card-to-card conditions turn on the direction of the gap and nothing else — *"You need the audience to feel a character's blindness"* (`info.suspense_shared_knowledge`), *"The pleasure of the piece is the puzzle"* (`info.dramatic_irony`), *"You need the bomb under the table"* (`info.restricted_narration`), *"The narrator must act on correct information"* (`info.naive_narrator`), *"The piece runs on irony, not on a reveal"* (`info.deducibility_audit`), *"The audience does not yet hold the fact"* (`info.open_secret`), *"The reveal belongs to the audience alone"* (`info.reveal_lands_on_character`), *"The outcome is known but not felt"* (`info.known_ending_structure`), *"The withheld character is the protagonist"* (`info.withheld_pov`), *"The scene is a relationship, not a fact"* (`info.mystery_audience_behind`). Five cards on the shelf answer this question by refusing it: they measure the gap instead of setting one, and *I cannot say yet* is a real answer with a real shortlist.

**Axis 2 — Who decides when the audience gets it: you, the material, or somebody outside the piece?**
Fourteen conditions turn on this, and they are the ones a reader holding a single scene would never have thought to write. They are stated as ownership, not as craft: *"The hole is imposed, not designed by you"* (`info.never_disclosed`), *"The wrong fact was yours, published"* (`info.disproved_fact`) and its exact reverse, *"The error is inside the story, not your publication"* (`info.the_correction`), *"You are making a claim, not showing a dispute"* (`info.disputed_history`), *"It is a fiction device, not a real accusation"* (`info.unverified_allegation`), *"The problem is the claim, not the person"* (`info.anonymity_decision`), *"Naming the bar would identify the source"* (`info.embargoed_fact`), *"A person could deliver the same fact"* (`info.the_document`) against *"The audience needs certainty about the event"* (`info.the_witness`), *"The story is about proof settling nothing"* (`info.physical_proof`), *"The moment was never shot"* (`info.finding_the_moment`). **This is the axis that decides whether the shelf's whole first half applies to you at all**, and one card on it carries the strongest evidence in the project that these conditions are trustworthy: `info.wholly_generated_piece` had its pair derived twice, by two readers who could not see each other's work, to the same target on the same condition — the moment the piece contains any captured material.

**Axis 3 — Is it a state, a moment, or a rule about where the fact sits?**
Twelve conditions are about duration and placement rather than position: *"More than sixty seconds of scene to fill"* (`info.surprise`), *"Runtime too short for the middle touch"* (`info.rule_of_three_placements`), *"Short runtime or a thin secret"* (`info.staged_reveal`) against *"Long form, or a slow burn is the product"* (`info.micro_reveal_ladder`), *"Density is the pleasure"* (`info.disclosure_budget`), *"One ending must land for everybody"* (`info.missable_reveal`), *"The character must drive the plot energetically"* (`info.isolating_secret`), *"The audience must act now"* (`info.hearsay_evidence`), *"A single work with no successor"* (`info.credibility_spend`). It is asked last because it is the only axis that separates the planting and auditing cards from everything else, and because a state and a moment are not alternatives to each other — they are answers to different halves of the same beat.

**The default, and the one against it**

**The default is `info.dramatic_irony`, and it is measured rather than asserted.** It is the most-yielded-to card on the shelf — five cards send a reader to it — and, which matters more, **the five fire on five different conditions from four different regions**: `info.suspense_shared_knowledge` (*"You need the audience to feel a character's blindness"*), `info.surprise` (*"More than sixty seconds of scene to fill"*), `info.naive_narrator` (*"The narrator must act on correct information"*), `info.restricted_narration` (*"You need the bomb under the table"*) and `info.deducibility_audit` (*"The piece runs on irony, not on a reveal"*) — the positions block, the surprise block, the narration block, and a negative-ledger pass that lives three files away. The shelf, asked five times from four directions where to fall back to, falls back here. The argument for it is the one its own card makes, and it is structural rather than a matter of taste: irony runs continuously through a scene instead of firing once at the end, which is what lets you afford a slow scene at all.

**The one against it is `info.delayed_decoding`, and the case for it is that it inverts the shelf's grammar rather than choosing a different position inside it.** Every other card here decides *when the audience is given the meaning*. This one gives them the event with the meaning stripped off — the sound, the impact, the shape in the water — and lets the understanding arrive several seconds later, in them, unassisted. Nothing on the shelf yields to it, nothing in the package does its job differently, and it is the only card here where the audience is behind and ahead at once: they hold the whole fact and none of its sense. Where irony tells the room what a moment means and makes them wait for the character to catch up, this makes the room do the catching up — the one thing irony can never ask of them. **Its stated cost is that it does not stack**: its own warning is that two in a row and the audience stops trusting their own eyes, so it is a single beat, paid for once. Its near cousin against the grain is `info.open_secret`, which is likewise yielded to by nothing and likewise refuses the premise — no concealment at all, and the tension is the labour of not-saying.

**Neither of them is the best card, and the shelf does not contain one.** The irony position is also the most worn position in the catalogue, and a piece assembled entirely out of defaults is a faster route to an ordinary one.

### The Information Map

**Also called:** the disclosure plan, the reveal schedule, the release map
**What it is:** A one-page document listing every fact the audience must eventually hold, the page or minute each is released, and the party who releases it. Not a ledger of who knows what at a moment (`info.knowledge_ledger`) but a plan of what leaves the writer's hands and when.
**Effect on the audience:** Invisible when right, immediately felt when wrong. A work without a map delivers three facts in ten minutes and then nothing for forty, and the audience reads that silence as the story losing interest in itself.
**Used for and where it works best:** Build it the moment a project passes about ninety minutes or two episodes. The craft rule: sort your facts into *needed to follow*, *needed to care*, and *needed to be surprised*, and release in that order, never mixing. Follow-facts go early and cheap; care-facts go in scenes with heat; surprise-facts go last and must already have been planted. The second rule is a rhythm rule — no stretch longer than about eight minutes or fifteen pages with zero new information, and no stretch with three new facts stacked. Audit the map before the second draft, not after.
**Best in:** formats: Limited Series, Feature Film, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Game Narrative | genres: Crime, Mystery, Thriller, Drama, Historical, Science Fiction
**Avoid when:** Never avoid building it — but do not write from it. A map is a schedule, not a story; scenes planned to deliver facts on time are the flattest scenes in any draft. Write the scene for its conflict, then check the map.
**Example:**
> FOLLOW: the farm is mortgaged (p.9, the bank letter). CARE: he mortgaged it without telling her (p.34, mid-row). SURPRISE: she countersigned it in 2009 and does not remember (p.171).
> *Nothing new between p.112 and p.140 — that is the sag everyone flagged.*

### What the Audience Never Learns

**Also called:** the permanent withhold, the unopened box, the sealed fact
**What it is:** A fact the story establishes as existing and deliberately never discloses — the contents of the letter, what he said at the door, the name of the man who paid. The absence is designed, marked, and permanent.
**Effect on the audience:** A hole they keep touching. Done right, it produces the sensation that the story's world exceeds the story's frame, and the characters go on existing after the last page. Done wrong, it produces the exact feeling of a missing scene.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when the *fact itself is smaller than the not-knowing* — what was in the suicide note matters less than a family spending thirty years apart over it. The craft rule that separates design from omission: something in the text must acknowledge the hole. A character asks and is refused, or the narration says plainly that this is not known. An unacknowledged withhold reads as a writer who forgot. Second rule: pay the audience elsewhere in the same beat. If they lose the letter's contents, they gain the sight of the reader's face — `info.reveal_lands_on_character` is what makes a permanent withhold survivable.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Stage Play, Short Story, Limited Series | genres: Drama, Tragedy, Psychological, Family Drama, Literary, Historical
**Avoid when:** The fact is the engine of the plot. Withhold the murderer permanently and you have not written ambiguity, you have written `info.the_cheat` with better manners. Also avoid in commercial serial: audiences who waited four seasons for a name will not accept its absence as art.
**Example:**
> He read it twice, folded it into his shirt pocket, and went back out to the yard.
> BROTHER: What does she say?
> FATHER: She says what she says.
> *He was buried in that shirt. Nobody checked the pocket, and every one of them knew nobody checked the pocket.*

**Yields to:** `info.embargoed_fact` — The hole is imposed, not designed by you.  
`info.never_disclosed`

### The Deliberately Unanswered Question

**Also called:** designed ambiguity, the open question, the unresolved beat
**What it is:** A question the story raises, sustains and refuses to close — distinct from a fact simply withheld, because here the *answer may not exist*. Whether the child was hers, whether he meant it, whether the miracle happened.
**Effect on the audience:** Continued thinking after the ending. The reader keeps re-arguing it, which is the most durable form of engagement available and the only one that outlives the runtime.
**Used for and where it works best:** Reserve it for *emotional and moral* questions, never logistical ones. "Did he love her?" can stay open forever; "who moved the body?" cannot, and audiences who forgive the first will punish the second in the same work. The craft rule: the ambiguity must be present from the opening, not introduced at the end — build the work so both readings are live throughout, then close on a concrete image rather than a thought, because an image sustains two meanings and a statement kills one. Test it by asking two readers which way it went; if both say the same thing, you wrote a weak answer, not an open question. See `end.open_ending` for the ending-side craft.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Feature Film, Stage Play, Short Film | genres: Psychological, Drama, Literary, Tragedy, Horror, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The genre contract promises closure — mystery, crime procedural, romance, kids' work. Also avoid using it to hide a decision you never made. Readers can tell the difference between a question held open and a writer who did not know, and the tell is that unmade decisions leave the earlier scenes uncommitted too.
**Example:**
> SISTER: Say it wasn't you. Just say the words and I'll never ask again.
> *She picks up her coat. She does not put it on. She stands there holding it, and outside a car alarm starts and nobody goes to it.*

**Yields to:** `info.fair_play_contract` — The genre contract promises closure.  
`info.deliberate_unanswered_question`

### The Disclosure Budget

**Also called:** information density, the reveal economy, facts per minute
**What it is:** A ceiling on how much genuinely new information a given form can absorb per unit of time, and a working decision about how to spend it. A ninety-second vertical episode has room for perhaps three new facts; a novel chapter for five or six; a documentary sequence for two.
**Effect on the audience:** Over budget, they retain nothing and feel lectured. Under budget, they feel the story idling. The budget is not about their intelligence, it is about how many facts can be attached to feeling at once — unattached facts evaporate within a scene.
**Used for and where it works best:** Set it before you write any information-heavy form: history, heritage, procedural, science fiction, anything with a world to explain. The craft rule: one new fact per unit of *emotion*, not per unit of time. If a scene has one emotional turn, it carries one fact that matters; hang three on it and two will be lost, and you will not be able to predict which two. Related: `expo.need_to_know_ladder` for ordering, `expo.lore_dump` for the failure. In short form the budget collapses hard — see `seq.shortform_serial`.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary, Heritage Documentary, Limited Series, Vertical Micro-Drama, Reels/Shorts, Novel | genres: Historical, Heritage, Science Fiction, Crime, Journalism, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** You are writing a work whose pleasure *is* density — a dense literary novel, a puzzle box for rewatch, a game with deep optional lore. There the budget applies to the critical path only; everything off it can be as thick as you like, as long as nothing load-bearing hides in the thickness.
**Example:**
> *Over budget:* "He was your father's brother, he ran the mill until the flood, and he's the one who signed the deed over to the church in '71."
> *On budget:* "That's my uncle." *Beat.* "He signed the mill away." *She says it the way other people say a death.*

**Yields to:** `info.missable_reveal` — Density is the pleasure; load the off-path layer.  
`info.disclosure_budget`

## Secrets Between Characters

### The Cost of Telling

**Also called:** the price of disclosure, the confession tariff, what it costs to say it
**What it is:** Designing a secret so that telling it has a specific, nameable, unaffordable price — not embarrassment, but a loss the character can itemise. The secret's strength is not what it contains; it is what disclosure destroys.
**Effect on the audience:** Patience. An audience who can name what telling would cost will wait a very long time for a character not to tell, and will not shout "just say it" at the screen — which is the single commonest audience reaction that kills a secret plot.
**Used for and where it works best:** Apply it to every secret in the draft as a test. Write the sentence: *if she tells, she loses ___ .* If the blank fills with "face" or "respect", the secret is too weak to carry a story. If it fills with "her daughter's citizenship", "the only house she has ever owned", "her brother's life", you have a structure. The craft rule: the cost must escalate with time, not sit flat. A secret that costs the same in episode eight as in episode one is a stalled secret — build in a deadline, a second person who learns it, or a lie told to cover it that is now worse than the thing.
**Best in:** formats: Limited Series, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Feature Film, Stage Play | genres: Family Drama, Crime, Social Drama, Drama, Tragedy, Historical
**Avoid when:** The story wants speed. A properly costed secret is slow by design; in thriller and short form you often want a secret that is merely dangerous, not expensive, so it can move. Also avoid costing a secret so high that keeping it makes the character monstrous, unless that is the arc you are writing.
**Example:**
> WIFE: Tell them what you saw. That's all. You saw a car.
> HE: And then I'm a man who waited eleven years. Whatever I was before, that's what I am after. To you as well — don't tell me it isn't.

`info.cost_of_telling`

### The Secret That Binds

**Also called:** the shared secret, complicity between characters, the pact
**What it is:** A secret held jointly by two or more characters, which creates an involuntary alliance between people who may otherwise want nothing to do with each other. They are now each other's risk.
**Effect on the audience:** A relationship the audience can read instantly without being told anything about it — two people behaving carefully around each other is legible from the back of the room. It also generates constant low tension, because every scene with both of them contains a thing that must not be said.
**Used for and where it works best:** The most efficient way to build a relationship with history in a single scene, and the best engine for a two-hander. The craft rule: bind people who are *unequal*, because the secret then does real work — the one with less to lose owns the one with more. Write one scene where they are forced to be civil in public, and let the audience watch the maintenance. Pairs with `rel.unspoken_agreement` and `rel.group_secret`; in ensembles, the binding secret is what makes a faction visible without a word of explanation.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Stage Play, Novel, One-Hour Drama | genres: Crime, Family Drama, Noir, Thriller, Social Drama, Historical
**Avoid when:** You want the audience to trust the relationship. A bound pair can never be simply close — everything warm between them reads as management. If a friendship needs to be believed in, do not put a corpse under it.
**Example:**
> *At the school gate, in front of other parents.*
> HE: You look well.
> SHE: I've been sleeping.
> HE: Good. That's good.
> *Neither of them has slept since March, and each of them knows the other has not.*

**Yields to:** `info.isolating_secret` — The relationship must read as simply close.  
`info.binding_secret`

### The Secret That Isolates

**Also called:** the solitary secret, the wall, the unsharable thing
**What it is:** A secret held by exactly one character, with no confidant available — so every relationship in their life is conducted across it. Not a plot secret but a condition of living.
**Effect on the audience:** Loneliness rendered structurally instead of described. The audience feels the isolation because they watch ordinary scenes fail: dinners that cannot land, kindness that cannot be received, a lie required to answer a harmless question.
**Used for and where it works best:** The right choice when the theme is shame, illness, sexuality, debt, faith lost, a crime not caught, or a diagnosis not shared. Use it where a bound secret would be too comfortable. The craft rule: write at least two scenes in which the secret is *irrelevant to what is happening* and still ruins it — a birthday, a joke, a job offer. The secret's real cost is not the crisis scenes, it is the ordinary ones, and audiences understand that faster than any speech. Second rule: give them one near-confession that fails for a mundane reason. A phone rings; someone comes in; she loses her nerve at the word "actually".
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Stage Play, Short Story, Limited Series | genres: Psychological, Drama, Family Drama, Social Drama, Tragedy, Heritage
**Avoid when:** The character must drive the plot forward energetically. Isolation is static by nature, and a whole film of a person not telling anybody anything needs a second engine — usually somebody else closing in, or a deadline the secret cannot survive.
**Example:**
> DAUGHTER: You're impossible to buy for. What do you even want?
> *Six weeks of results in his coat pocket, in the hall, ten feet away.*
> HE: Socks. Get me socks, I mean it — I'd genuinely like socks.

**Yields to:** `info.binding_secret` — The character must drive the plot energetically.  
`info.isolating_secret`

### The Confidant

**Also called:** the ficelle, the listener, the one who is told, the sounding board
**What it is:** A character who exists partly so the protagonist has somewhere to put the truth — the nurse, the bartender, the sister, the priest, the partner in the car. They receive information the audience needs delivered under pressure rather than in narration.
**Effect on the audience:** Access without narration. The audience gets interiority they can hear, spoken by someone who can push back — which converts what would have been a monologue into a scene.
**Used for and where it works best:** Reach for a confidant whenever a first-person or close-third story has to become a screenplay, and whenever a protagonist's interior life needs to be audible. The craft rule that stops them being furniture: the confidant must have *their own stake in the information*. The sister who is told about the affair must be the one who has to lie to their mother on Sunday. A confidant with nothing at risk is an exposition receptacle, and audiences feel the writer's hand inside them. Second rule: at least once, have them refuse to receive it — "don't tell me, I don't want to carry it" is often the best line in the relationship. See `rel.listener_friend` and `char.the_secret`.
**Best in:** formats: Stage Play, Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Podcast | genres: Crime, Drama, Family Drama, Mystery, Noir, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The isolation is the point. Giving a confidant to a character whose story is that nobody can be told dissolves the whole structure — see `info.isolating_secret`. Also avoid the confidant who only ever asks questions; that is not a character, that is an interview.
**Example:**
> HE: I need to say it out loud once.
> PRIEST: Then say it outside. In here I have to do something about it.

**Yields to:** `info.isolating_secret` — The isolation is the point.  
`info.the_confidant`

### The Open Secret

**Also called:** the thing nobody says, collective pretence, the unspoken known
**What it is:** A fact that every character knows and every character behaves as though they do not. Nothing is hidden; what is maintained is the pretence. The tension is not concealment but the labour of not-saying.
**Effect on the audience:** A very particular social dread, closer to comedy than to mystery, and available in both. The audience watches a room full of people spending energy on a fiction, and the suspense is over who will spend it badly.
**Used for and where it works best:** The engine of family gatherings, village life, workplaces, and high-context cultures where naming a thing is the offence rather than doing it. Use it where a conventional secret would be implausible — everyone knows the son is drinking, everyone knows the marriage is over, everyone knows who informed in 1963. The craft rule: give the pretence a *maintenance cost* the audience can see — a seat left empty, a name substituted, a photograph turned around. Then have an outsider or a child ask the plain question, and write the four seconds after it. That is the scene. Pairs with `sub.social_prohibition` and `rel.unspoken_agreement`.
**Best in:** formats: Stage Play, Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Short Film | genres: Family Drama, Social Drama, Comedy, Heritage, Historical, Tragedy
**Avoid when:** The audience does not yet know the fact. An open secret must be disclosed to the audience *early and flatly* — it is a dramatic-irony machine, not a mystery. Try to run it as a puzzle and you get a first half in which everyone is inexplicably odd.
**Example:**
> AUNT: And is Marcus coming?
> MOTHER: Marcus is working.
> AUNT: On a Sunday.
> MOTHER: He's always been a worker.
> CHILD: Is Marcus the one in prison?
> *Somebody asks for the salt.*

**Yields to:** `info.mystery_audience_behind` — The audience does not yet hold the fact.  
`info.open_secret`

## Evidence and Proof

### The Credibility Ladder

**Also called:** proof hierarchy, orders of evidence, how a story earns belief
**What it is:** The ranked forms in which a story can assert something and be believed — from weakest to strongest: a character says it; a second character confirms it; a document shows it; the audience sees it happen. Deciding which rung each claim needs is an information-control decision, not a plot one.
**Effect on the audience:** Belief, or the withholding of it. Audiences grade evidence automatically and unconsciously; a claim delivered one rung too low stays provisional in their minds no matter how many times you repeat it, and a story built on provisional claims never gains weight.
**Used for and where it works best:** Essential in crime, courtroom, journalism, documentary and any story that turns on whether something is true. The craft rule: put your *most contested* claim on the highest rung you can afford, and deliberately leave lesser claims low so the ladder is visible. If everything is proven at the same level the audience stops grading and starts skimming. Second rule, and it is the useful one: you can generate enormous tension by proving a thing to the audience at rung four and leaving the characters stuck at rung one — they know what we saw and cannot show it.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Novel, Documentary, Podcast, One-Hour Drama | genres: Crime, Journalism, Mystery, Social Drama, Thriller, Historical
**Avoid when:** The story's subject is the impossibility of proof — trauma testimony, disputed history, a case with no witnesses. There the ladder is the antagonist, and you should write it as one rather than quietly promoting claims to make the plot work.
**Example:**
> Rung one: "He was there." Rung two: the neighbour half-remembers a coat. Rung three: a fuel receipt, eleven kilometres away, 9:40.
> Rung four is the thing we saw in the opening minute, and there is no version of this hearing in which anyone else ever sees it.

**Yields to:** `info.disputed_history` — The subject is the impossibility of proof.  
`info.credibility_ladder`

### The Document

**Also called:** the paper, the file, the letter as evidence, documentary proof
**What it is:** A physical text inside the story — a letter, a ledger, a medical result, a land deed, a transcript — that carries information the audience receives directly rather than through a speaker. The document is the only witness that cannot change its story.
**Effect on the audience:** Unusual trust, plus the pleasure of reading over a shoulder. A document converts the audience from listeners into examiners, and they will scrutinise a shown page far harder than a spoken line.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when a claim must survive an unreliable speaker, and when you want the audience to reach a conclusion a beat before the character holding the page. The craft rule: show *less* of the document than you want to. One line, one date, one signature, and let the character's face carry the rest — a fully legible page dissipates the tension into reading time. Second rule: documents are strongest when they are *incidentally* damning. A deed that proves a lie is powerful; a deed that proves it in a clause about drainage is unforgettable. See `pov.found_document`, `voice.found_document`, `inter.found_document` for the narration and interactive variants.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Documentary, Heritage Documentary, Game Narrative | genres: Crime, Mystery, Historical, Heritage, Journalism, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The document is doing work a person should do. Writers reach for paper when a confrontation scares them, and a file read alone in an office is the flattest way to deliver a turn. If a character could tell the character, make them.
**Example:**
> *Handwritten, in the margin of the inventory, in a clerk's hand:*
> "Received of the estate: 40 dinars, 2 mules, 1 child (f., abt 9 yrs), name not given."
> *He reads it four times. It is the ninth line of a list of thirty.*

**Yields to:** `info.the_witness` — A person could deliver the same fact.  
`info.the_document`

### The Witness

**Also called:** testimony, the person who saw, the account
**What it is:** Information delivered as one person's account of an event the audience did not see — carrying, always, both the event and the teller. You never receive testimony; you receive testimony-plus-a-witness.
**Effect on the audience:** Divided attention, usefully. They are simultaneously assembling the reported event and assessing the reporter, which is why a testimony scene can do characterisation and plot in the same ninety seconds.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when the *reporting* is more interesting than the event — which is true far more often than writers assume, especially for violence, and for anything you cannot afford to stage. The craft rule: give the witness a reason to shade it. Fear, loyalty, a conviction they are protecting, a version they have already told three times. A neutral witness is a narrator with a costume on. Second rule: have someone in the scene notice the shading without naming it, so the audience is licensed to doubt without being told to. Relates to `dial.half_truth` — a witness is where half-truth and evidence meet.
**Best in:** formats: Stage Play, Feature Film, Limited Series, Novel, Podcast, Documentary | genres: Crime, Journalism, Historical, War, Social Drama, Mystery
**Avoid when:** The audience needs certainty about the event. Testimony can never deliver a fact cleanly — it always delivers it wearing someone. If the plot requires the audience to be sure, show it or document it.
**Example:**
> "They came at night. I want to say it was the fifteenth but my sister says the sixteenth and she was the one with the calendar."
> *Beat.*
> "There were four of them. There may have been five. I have said four for thirty years so let me say four."

**Yields to:** `info.the_document` — The audience needs certainty about the event.  
`info.the_witness`

### The Physical Proof

**Also called:** the object that cannot lie, material evidence, the thing itself
**What it is:** An object whose existence settles a question — a key that fits, a scar, a coat still wet, a photograph taken where someone claimed never to have been. The audience does not have to be told what it means, only shown that it is there.
**Effect on the audience:** Instant, wordless conviction. Physical proof is the one form of evidence that lands before the audience has finished reasoning, which is why it produces gasps where a document produces nods.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it at the top of the credibility ladder, once, for the claim your story cannot afford to have doubted. The craft rule: the object must have been *seen earlier in an innocent context* — planted, in the Part 1 sense, under a different reason for existing (`info.plant_and_payoff`, `info.buried_plant`). Proof that arrives new is not proof, it is a prop. Second rule: do not explain it. Let a character pick it up and stop talking, and let `info.reveal_lands_on_character` finish the job. The instant someone says "which means he must have been at the house", you have converted proof back into testimony.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Stage Play, Novel, Limited Series, Short Film, Game Narrative | genres: Crime, Mystery, Thriller, Noir, Family Drama, Historical
**Avoid when:** The story is about how proof fails to change anything — much political drama, much social drama, most true-crime documentary. There, producing an unanswerable object at the end writes a lie about the world you have spent two hours describing.
**Example:**
> She empties the bag onto the table: phone, keys, a child's plastic hairclip, shaped like a strawberry.
> *He has been saying for two hours that he has never met the girl. He stops saying it.*

**Yields to:** `info.contradictory_sources` — The story is about proof settling nothing.  
`info.physical_proof`

### Contradiction Between Sources

**Also called:** the Rashomon structure, conflicting accounts, testimony collision
**What it is:** The same event reported two or more times, incompatibly, with the story declining to adjudicate — or adjudicating much later, and at a cost. After Kurosawa's *Rashomon*, in which the accounts cannot be reconciled and no correct version is confirmed.
**Effect on the audience:** They are promoted from receiver to judge. Attention shifts from *what happened* to *who needs what to have happened*, which is a deeper and more uncomfortable engagement than a straight mystery.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when your subject is perception, blame, communal memory or a crime with no forensic answer. The craft rule that makes it play rather than confuse: the accounts must differ in *self-interest*, not in detail. Two versions of the same fight where one man threw the first punch is a story; two versions differing over the colour of a door is an error hunt. Second rule: keep one fact identical across every account and let the audience hang their footing on it. Three unmoored versions is vertigo, not structure. See `pov.same_event_twice` for the POV mechanics and `time.nonlinear_assembly` for the ordering.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Novel, Stage Play, Documentary, Podcast | genres: Crime, Journalism, Psychological, Historical, War, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The audience needs to invest emotionally in one version. Contradiction is corrosive to sympathy — you cannot grieve properly for an event you are still weighing. Also avoid three or more versions in under ninety minutes; two collide, three blur.
**Example:**
> HIM: She asked me to come. I have the message.
> HER: I asked him to bring the car back. That is the message.
> THE SON: Nobody asked anybody. He was already in the drive when the phone went — I heard it ring from upstairs and I heard him not answer it.

**Yields to:** `info.the_witness` — The audience must invest in one version.  
`info.contradictory_sources`

## The Reveal in Non-Fiction

### Documentary Reveal Structure

**Also called:** the doc turn, the third-act document turn, non-fiction disclosure design
**What it is:** Building a factual film or series so that information the filmmaker had from the start is released in a designed order, with at least one turn that changes the meaning of everything before it. The facts are found; the sequence is authored.
**Effect on the audience:** The same forward pull as fiction, with an added charge — this happened. A documentary reveal lands harder than a fictional one of equal size, because the audience cannot dismiss it as construction.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any documentary longer than twenty minutes, and mandatory in true crime and investigative series. The craft rule: the turn must be a turn *in the audience's understanding*, not merely a new fact — "and then we found the second account" is a development; "and then we understood that the charity had never had a second account" is a turn. Second rule, an honesty rule that is also a craft rule: never structure a reveal so that the audience believes something you already knew to be false for longer than the film can justify. Suspense built on the filmmaker lying by sequence is the genre's chronic vice, and it is visible on a rewatch. See `expo.doc_narration_contract` and `theme.theme_in_nonfiction`.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary, Heritage Documentary, Podcast, Limited Series | genres: Journalism, Crime, Historical, Heritage, Social Drama, Psychological
**Avoid when:** The material is grief or atrocity and a structural turn would make an audience *enjoy* the discovery. Some subjects must be disclosed flatly and early. If you find yourself protecting a fact about a dead child for act three, you are making entertainment out of the wrong thing.
**Example:**
> NARRATION, minute 8: "The council had no record of the inspection."
> Minute 41: "The council had no record of the inspection because the inspection was carried out by the company that built the flats. We have known this since minute eight. So has the council."

**Yields to:** `info.known_ending_structure` — Grief or atrocity: disclose flatly and early.  
`info.doc_reveal_structure`

### The Interview Admission

**Also called:** the on-camera turn, the admission beat, the interview reveal
**What it is:** A moment in a recorded interview where a subject says more than they intended, contradicts themselves, or concedes — and the edit is built to hold on it rather than move past it.
**Effect on the audience:** The strongest single beat available in non-fiction. Because it is unscripted, the audience reads the pause, the swallow, the reach for water as evidence in itself, and no narration can compete with it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Build the whole interview toward it, then structure the film around it. The craft rules are production rules as much as writing ones: ask the hard question late and in the middle of a run of easy ones, never first; ask it once and then say nothing, because the admission almost always arrives in the second silence, not the first; and never cut away at the moment of admission — hold the shot until the subject moves, not until you are comfortable. In the edit, the craft rule is restraint: do not follow an admission with narration explaining what was admitted. Let `dial.unanswered_line` work.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary, Heritage Documentary, Podcast, Limited Series | genres: Journalism, Crime, Social Drama, Historical, Psychological
**Avoid when:** The subject is vulnerable and the admission serves the film rather than the story. There is an editorial line between a powerful person conceding and a frightened person being worked; audiences smell the second and turn on the filmmaker, correctly. Also avoid engineering it in an interview you obtained by describing the film as something else.
**Example:**
> Q: Did anyone tell you the tank had been condemned?
> A: Not in those words.
> *Silence. Eleven seconds. He looks at someone off-camera.*
> A: I'm not going to sit here and say we didn't know. I'm not going to say that.

`info.interview_admission`

### Archive as Reveal

**Also called:** the found footage turn, the photograph that changes it, the archival counterpunch
**What it is:** Using archival material — footage, a photograph, a recording, a register — not as illustration but as the disclosure itself: the image arrives and it contradicts, confirms or completes what has just been said.
**Effect on the audience:** Proof with a date on it. The audience treats archive as physical evidence (`info.physical_proof`) rather than testimony, and it carries a second charge in heritage work, because the people in it are gone and the audience knows it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Place archive *after* the claim it settles, never before — an image shown first becomes wallpaper, shown second it becomes a verdict. The craft rule: run the archive silent for at least two seconds before any narration touches it, and let the audience find the thing themselves. Second rule, particular to heritage and historical work: caption it honestly on screen, because an undated or misattributed image is the fastest way to lose an audience who know the material (`info.heritage_audience_correction`), and a single wrong caption retroactively devalues every other image in the film. See `expo.heritage_specificity`.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary, Heritage Documentary, Limited Series, Podcast, Reels/Shorts | genres: Historical, Heritage, Journalism, War, Social Drama, Crime
**Avoid when:** You do not have the archive and are tempted to substitute lookalike or generative material for it. The moment recreated footage sits in the position of evidence, the film's whole credibility ladder collapses — and if you must use recreation, mark it unmistakably and never in a reveal slot.
**Example:**
> He says the school was closed by then. He says it twice, patiently, as if to a slow child.
> *Cut: a class photograph. Forty children on the steps. The date is stencilled in the corner in white. It is two years after the school closed, and he is in the back row, second from the left, in a tie.*

**Yields to:** `expo.disclosed_staging_note` — No archive exists; mark the recreation.  
`info.archive_as_reveal`

### Withholding From the Subject

**Also called:** the undisclosed cut, the un-shown rough, filmmaker-subject asymmetry
**What it is:** The information gap between the documentary maker and the person being filmed — what the subject has not been told about the film's angle, what they will not see before broadcast, what other interviewees have said about them. An information-control decision that sits outside the film and shapes everything inside it.
**Effect on the audience:** Felt indirectly, and later. Audiences increasingly read for it, and a film that has clearly ambushed a cooperative subject loses the room even when the subject deserved it. Conversely, a film that is visibly straight with its people buys enormous latitude for what it then shows.
**Used for and where it works best:** Every documentary makes this decision; the craft is making it consciously and consistently. The working line most experienced filmmakers draw runs along power: withholding your angle from an institution, a minister or a company that could shut you down is defensible; withholding it from a bereaved family who agreed to talk is not. The craft rule: decide before you shoot what you will and will not tell each subject, write it down, and hold to it — improvised disclosure under pressure in a kitchen is how documentaries get sued and how people get hurt. Second rule: never promise approval you do not intend to give; refusing a viewing outright is survivable, breaking a promise to show is not.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary, Heritage Documentary, Podcast, Limited Series | genres: Journalism, Social Drama, Crime, Historical, Heritage
**Avoid when:** The subject is vulnerable, a minor, bereaved, or not in a position to understand how editing works. There, the asymmetry is not a technique but an abuse of one, and most people being filmed have no idea how completely sequence can restructure what they said.
**Example:**
> *What she was told:* "It's a film about the estate, about what it was like growing up here."
> *What the film is:* an investigation into her brother.
> *What the release form says:* everything, for ever, in all media.
> *That gap is the film's real ethical position, whatever the narration says.*

`info.withholding_from_the_subject`

### Access as Structure

**Also called:** the getting of the information, the pursuit spine, the process film
**What it is:** Making the *acquisition* of information the story's spine — the requests, refusals, waits, leaks and doors — so the audience follows a hunt rather than a lecture. What you could not get becomes as informative as what you could.
**Effect on the audience:** Investment in facts they would otherwise skim. A document the audience watched someone fight for eleven months reads as a climax; the same document handed over in narration reads as a footnote.
**Used for and where it works best:** The standard rescue for investigative and heritage work with thin visual material, and the honest structure for any film where the institution will not speak. The craft rule: put the refusals on screen — the unanswered emails, the empty chair, the letter declining to comment — because a refusal is a piece of information, and stacking three of them creates an antagonist without an accusation. Second rule: give the pursuit a clock or a diminishing resource, or it becomes an errand list (`seq.errand_run`). Cross-reference `seq.investigation_sequence` for the beat design.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary, Heritage Documentary, Podcast, Limited Series, One-Hour Drama | genres: Journalism, Crime, Historical, Social Drama, Heritage
**Avoid when:** The filmmaker's difficulties are not interesting and the subject is. Access-as-structure is the commonest way a documentary about a massacre becomes a documentary about a producer's inbox. Use it only where the obstruction is itself part of what you are exposing.
**Example:**
> Request 1 — refused, "not held".
> Request 4 — refused, "vexatious".
> Request 9 — twelve pages, nine of them black.
> *The tenth page is a distribution list, and it has a name on it that is not supposed to exist until 1991.*

**Yields to:** `info.doc_reveal_structure` — The subject is interesting, the pursuit is not.  
`info.access_as_structure`

## Serial and Interactive Information

### What the Audience Carries Between Episodes

**Also called:** retention budget, the inter-episode memory, viewer carry
**What it is:** A realistic estimate of what an audience still holds when they return after a week, a season break, or a two-year hiatus — and the design of a series around that estimate rather than around the writers' room's perfect recall.
**Effect on the audience:** Invisible competence or quiet alienation. Audiences do not complain that they have forgotten; they complain that a show "got complicated" or "lost itself", which is almost always a retention failure being misdescribed.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any serialised work, and critical across a season gap. The working numbers: assume a weekly audience carries emotional states and relationships reliably, names and faces moderately, and plot mechanics badly. Binge audiences invert the middle two — they carry plot well and lose emotional charge, because they have not lived a week with it. The craft rule: never make a reveal depend on a plot detail last mentioned more than two episodes ago without re-touching it inside the episode, in dialogue, under a different reason (`info.buried_plant` again, used as maintenance). Second rule: re-establish relationships, not facts, after a long gap; if they feel the room, they will rebuild the plot themselves.
**Best in:** formats: Limited Series, One-Hour Drama, Podcast, Vertical Micro-Drama, Comics | genres: Crime, Drama, Mystery, Thriller, Science Fiction, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** Never avoid the estimate — but avoid letting it flatten you. Shows that write only to what the audience reliably holds end up with no long arcs at all. The answer to a hard memory load is maintenance, not simplification.
**Example:**
> *Ep 9, cold open, which exists solely to re-touch the ledger before Ep 10 needs it:*
> "Still nothing from the Cyprus account?"
> "It's not an account, it's a company, and no."
> "Same difference."
> "It really isn't, and that's the problem."

`info.between_episode_memory`

### Recap Without a Recap

**Also called:** in-scene refresh, organic reminder, the working recap
**What it is:** Re-delivering the facts an episode needs from inside the drama, so the episode stands alone without a "previously on" montage — and so bingeing audiences are not told things they learned forty minutes ago.
**Effect on the audience:** Nothing, which is the goal. A good recap-in-scene is felt only by the viewer who needed it; a bad one insults the viewer who did not, and a montage recap tells every viewer the episode's twist in advance by selecting exactly the three facts that matter.
**Used for and where it works best:** Essential now that most drama is consumed both weekly and in binges from the same file. The craft rule: put the reminder in a mouth that has a reason to be resentful or wrong about it. "You mean the one you swore had been shredded" delivers the fact and a grievance in one line. Second rule: recap facts, never feelings — restate what was signed, never restate that they are angry, because a restated emotion reads as the show not trusting its own last episode. Third: watch what the montage would have chosen and deliberately *do not* refresh that one item, since a refreshed fact is a flagged fact.
**Best in:** formats: One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Podcast, Vertical Micro-Drama, Comics | genres: Crime, Drama, Thriller, Mystery, Family Drama, Science Fiction
**Avoid when:** The form is genuinely episodic and a new viewer arrives every week, or the plot mechanics are so dense that in-scene refresh would eat the act. Procedural television uses the montage for a reason.
**Example:**
> *Instead of PREVIOUSLY ON: the will was changed on 3 March.*
> LAWYER: The third of March.
> SON: You've said the date four times.
> LAWYER: I'll say it until one of you tells me who drove her there.

`info.recap_without_recap`

### The Reveal a Player May Never Reach

**Also called:** the missable turn, optional disclosure, the unreached room
**What it is:** In interactive work, information placed behind an action the player may never take — a door not opened, a conversation not exhausted, a corpse not searched. The writer must design for both the player who finds it and the far larger number who do not.
**Effect on the audience:** For finders, ownership — a fact discovered feels earned in a way a fact delivered never does, and they will tell other players about it. For non-finders, ideally nothing at all; at worst, an ending that does not parse.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use missable placement for *depth*, never for *load*. The craft rule is a hard split: anything required to understand the ending sits on the critical path, and anything off the path must be enrichment only — motive, texture, the reason a thing was the way it was. Second rule: write the critical path version first and completely, then layer, so optional content can never become load-bearing by accident (`inter.skippable_layering`). Third: a missable reveal should change how a *later required scene feels*, not what it means — that way finders get a different colour and non-finders lose nothing. See `inter.environmental_storytelling` and `inter.second_run_reward`.
**Best in:** formats: Game Narrative, Interactive Fiction, Vertical Micro-Drama | genres: Horror, Mystery, Science Fiction, Crime, Psychological, Drama
**Avoid when:** The work has one ending that must land for everybody. If the twist depends on a letter in a drawer, most of your audience will finish the game having watched a different, worse story, and will be told by the internet that they played it wrong.
**Example:**
> *On the critical path:* the captain orders the deck cleared, and you clear it.
> *In a locker nobody has to open:* a crew roster, two names crossed out in the same pen, dated the day before you arrived.
> *Both players watch the same final scene. Only one of them hears the count is wrong.*

**Yields to:** `info.multiple_paths_one_fact` — One ending must land for everybody.  
`info.missable_reveal`

### Several Roads to One Fact

**Also called:** redundant disclosure, the multi-path reveal, fact triangulation
**What it is:** Delivering a single required fact through two or more independent routes — a conversation, a document, an overheard line — so that a player or reader who misses one still receives it, without anyone receiving it twice in the same form.
**Effect on the audience:** Reliability that never reads as repetition. Players who find it twice experience confirmation rather than redundancy, provided each route carries different *texture* around the same core.
**Used for and where it works best:** Mandatory in branching and open-structure work, and useful in any ensemble drama where a viewer's attention may be elsewhere. The craft rule: vary the *source's attitude*, not the wording. The guard says it with contempt, the letter says it in bureaucratic neutral, the widow says it as an apology. Same fact, three tempers, no duplication. Second rule: flag the fact as delivered in whatever state system you use, so a character who has already told you does not tell you again — `inter.state_flag` exists for this and its absence is the most common reason branching dialogue feels broken.
**Best in:** formats: Game Narrative, Interactive Fiction, Limited Series, Novel | genres: Mystery, Crime, Science Fiction, Thriller, Drama
**Avoid when:** The fact's power is in its single source. Some information should be fragile — heard once, from one person, possibly misheard. Triangulating that is how you turn a haunting detail into an item of inventory.
**Example:**
> *Route A, the barman:* "Cheaper to let it burn than to fix it, wasn't it."
> *Route B, the insurance file:* premium raised twice, cover reduced, page 3.
> *Route C, his daughter, not looking at you:* "He asked me what I'd want to keep. In September. He asked me in September."

**Yields to:** `info.hearsay_evidence` — The fact's power is its single fragile source.  
`info.multiple_paths_one_fact`

## Knowledge That Changes

### The Retcon

**Also called:** retroactive continuity, the revision, re-established fact
**What it is:** Changing a fact the work has already established — a death undone, a parentage rewritten, a motive reassigned — in a later instalment. Distinct from a reveal: a reveal reinterprets what was shown, a retcon contradicts it.
**Effect on the audience:** Depends entirely on one thing — whether the new version makes the old scenes *better* or *pointless*. Audiences forgive contradiction and never forgive being told that hours they invested did not count.
**Used for and where it works best:** Long-running serials, franchises and comics outgrow early decisions, and pretending otherwise produces worse work than a clean revision. The craft rule: retcon by *reinterpretation wherever possible*. Find the reading under which the original scenes were true but incomplete — he was not lying, he was repeating what he had been told — and you get a reframe with the force of `info.recontextualising_reveal` rather than an erasure. Second rule: never retcon to undo a cost the audience paid emotionally. Reversing a death is the classic case; it does not restore the character, it retroactively deletes the grief, and the audience will not spend it again.
**Best in:** formats: Limited Series, One-Hour Drama, Comics, Game Narrative, Novel | genres: Science Fiction, Crime, Drama, Thriller, Horror
**Avoid when:** It exists to repair a plot hole a viewer found. Most retcons are defensive, and audiences can tell a repair from a development within one scene. Inside the fiction, a quiet inconsistency does less damage than a loud correction of it; an error of fact in a published work is a different case, and `info.the_correction` governs it.
**Example:**
> *Season 2 established:* the file was destroyed in the fire.
> *Erasure retcon:* "There was a second copy." *Nothing that happened for two seasons now matters.*
> *Reinterpretation retcon:* "The file burned. We watched it burn. What nobody asked is who told us it was the file."

**Yields to:** `info.recontextualising_reveal` — Reinterpret rather than erase.  
`info.the_retcon`

### The Fact That Turns Out To Be Wrong

**Also called:** the disproved premise, the corrected belief, the false foundation
**What it is:** Information the story delivered honestly and the audience accepted, which is later shown — within the same work — to be untrue. Not a lie by the narrative but an error inside it: a misidentified body, a mistranslated word, a date everyone repeated.
**Effect on the audience:** Vertigo followed by re-reading, and — uniquely in this category — a lesson in their own credulity. They accepted it because a character they trusted said it, which makes the correction feel like something that happened to them rather than to the plot.
**Used for and where it works best:** Superb when the theme is institutional failure, inherited belief, or family myth. The craft rule that keeps it fair: the *source* of the wrong fact must have been visible at the time. Somebody said it, and the audience can name who. If the wrong fact arrived in neutral narration, correcting it is `info.the_cheat`. Second rule: dramatise the moment of *acceptance*, not just the moment of correction — a half-second early where someone asks "are we sure?" and is waved off buys you the whole reversal later.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Novel, Documentary, One-Hour Drama | genres: Crime, Historical, Social Drama, Journalism, Family Drama, Psychological
**Avoid when:** The audience has no emotional stake in the fact. Correcting a detail nobody invested in is a plot mechanic, and reads as the writer moving furniture. If it is worth disproving, it was worth someone's belief.
**Example:**
> *Episode 1:* "The coroner said drowning. It's on the certificate, it's been on the certificate for thirty years."
> *Episode 6:* The certificate says drowning. The coroner in 1994 was a GP with a locum contract, he signed four hundred of them that year, and he never saw the body.

**Yields to:** `info.the_correction` — The wrong fact was yours, published.  
`info.disproved_fact`

### Disputed History

**Also called:** contested memory, collective memory conflict, the version we were raised on
**What it is:** Building a work around the fact that a community holds an account of an event that other communities, or the record, dispute — and that the disagreement is itself the material, not an obstacle to the material.
**Effect on the audience:** Divided along lines the work does not control. Part of the audience arrives already holding one version with emotional force; part arrives with none. Everything you write is read twice, by two rooms, at once.
**Used for and where it works best:** Central to heritage, diaspora, post-conflict and national-history work. The craft rule: dramatise the *transmission*, not the adjudication. Show the grandmother telling it, the schoolbook printing it, the son repeating it wrong at a wedding — the audience then experiences how a version becomes true for people, which is honest whatever the record says. Second rule: if you take a position, take it through evidence on screen (`info.the_document`, `info.archive_as_reveal`), never through narration, because narrated adjudication in contested history reads as propaganda to exactly the half of the audience you needed. Third, for heritage makers: name your sources on screen. It costs four seconds and it is the difference between a film that can be argued with and one that can be dismissed.
**Best in:** formats: Heritage Documentary, Documentary, Limited Series, Feature Film, Novel, Podcast | genres: Heritage, Historical, War, Social Drama, Journalism, Tragedy
**Avoid when:** You are working inside one community's version and presenting it as settled to an audience that includes people it accuses. That is not disputed history, that is a claim, and the form will not protect you. Also avoid false balance on matters where the record is not in fact divided.
**Example:**
> GRANDMOTHER: They came on the feast day. Everyone knows they came on the feast day.
> ARCHIVIST, later, mildly: The dispatch is dated the eleventh. The feast is the nineteenth.
> GRANDSON: So she's wrong.
> ARCHIVIST: She was nine. She remembers it as the feast because that was the last feast.

**Yields to:** `info.unverified_allegation` — You are making a claim, not showing a dispute.  
`info.disputed_history`

## Rumour and Hearsay

### The Rumour Chain

**Also called:** gossip evolution, the telephone structure, information decay
**What it is:** A fact passed from character to character, changing at each hand-off as each speaker adds intention, flattery, fear or economy — so that the audience watches an event become a different event without any single person lying.
**Effect on the audience:** Comedy or dread, depending only on the stakes, with the same mechanism underneath. The audience holds the original, so every hand-off is dramatic irony; the pleasure is watching the distance grow.
**Used for and where it works best:** Village, school, workplace, army, congregation, group chat — any closed community with more talk than information. The craft rule: each distortion must be *motivated by the teller's need*, never random. One inflates to seem connected, one softens to avoid trouble, one sharpens to justify a grudge. Random drift is a party trick; motivated drift is characterisation of four people in half a page. Second rule: show the original to the audience first, briefly and plainly, or you have nothing to measure against.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, One-Hour Drama, Feature Film, Stage Play, Limited Series | genres: Social Drama, Family Drama, Comedy, Historical, Heritage, Crime
**Avoid when:** Your audience has not met the people doing the telling. A rumour chain is only funny or frightening if each link has a face; run it through strangers and it reads as montage filler.
**Example:**
> What happened: he was seen leaving the clinic.
> By the second telling: he was seen leaving the clinic with a woman.
> By the fourth: she was the one from the bank, and it wasn't the first time.
> By Sunday, three people have stopped greeting his wife, who has not yet been told anything at all.

**Yields to:** `info.hearsay_evidence` — The tellers are strangers to the audience.  
`info.rumour_chain`

### Hearsay as Evidence

**Also called:** the secondhand fact, "they say", reported knowledge
**What it is:** Information the audience receives only as report — nobody in the story witnessed it, and the source is a collective "they" or a chain no one can trace. The story treats it as usable while marking it as unverified.
**Effect on the audience:** Suspicion held in reserve. Audiences file hearsay at the bottom of the credibility ladder automatically, which makes it the perfect carrier for a fact you want them to hold but not to trust — and the perfect setup for later verification or collapse.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it to plant a fact cheaply that you intend to promote later. A rumour in act one costs a line and creates no obligation; the same fact proven in act three lands twice as hard because the audience half-remembers having dismissed it. The craft rule: give hearsay to a character the audience is inclined to discount — the drunk, the neighbour, the child, the one who is always talking. Second rule: decide on the page whether it is true, and write the story as though the audience will eventually find out, even if they never do.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Limited Series, Podcast, Stage Play | genres: Crime, Noir, Social Drama, Historical, Mystery, Heritage
**Avoid when:** You need the audience to act on the information now. Hearsay cannot carry urgency; nobody runs on a rumour. Promote it or drop it.
**Example:**
> "They say he came back for a week in '78. Nobody saw him, mind. My mother says the lights were on in the top room and my mother would say that."
> *Nineteen chapters later, a hotel register: seven nights, October 1978, paid in cash.*

**Yields to:** `info.credibility_ladder` — The audience must act now; promote the rung.  
`info.hearsay_evidence`

### The Community's Version of a Person

**Also called:** the town's story, collective characterisation, what everybody knows about him
**What it is:** A character the audience meets first as a consensus — a reputation held by a group, stated by several mouths, contradicted by none — before they meet the person. The gap between the version and the man is the story.
**Effect on the audience:** They arrive at the first real scene already prejudiced, and they feel the correction as their own error rather than the story's reversal. That is a more durable change of mind than any argued one.
**Used for and where it works best:** Strong in heritage, village and small-town work, and in any story about return, exile or disgrace. The craft rule: build the consensus out of at least three unrelated voices in three registers — an insult, a warning, a joke — because one speaker is an opinion and three are a climate. Second rule: the consensus must be *partly true*, or the correction is a simple swap and the community becomes a strawman. He did take the money; what nobody says is who he gave it to. Overlaps deliberately with `char.reputation_before_entrance`; the difference is that here the reputation is the group's, and the group is a character.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Limited Series, Stage Play, Heritage Documentary | genres: Social Drama, Heritage, Family Drama, Historical, Crime, Tragedy
**Avoid when:** The audience will meet the person within a page or two. A consensus needs time to set — establish it, then wait at least a full sequence, or it reads as a clumsy introduction.
**Example:**
> "Don't buy anything off him."
> "He's not a bad man. He's just not a man you'd lend to."
> "My father lent to him."
> *Forty pages on, he pays for the funeral of a woman he is not related to, in cash, and asks the priest not to say where it came from.*

**Yields to:** `char.reputation_before_entrance` — The reputation is one person's, not a group's.  
`info.communal_version`

## Writing for an Audience Who Already Knows

### Structuring a Known Ending

**Also called:** the foregone conclusion, writing to a known outcome, the inevitability structure
**What it is:** Building a work whose ending the audience already holds — a historical event, a famous life, a disaster, a scripture story — so the engine is *how* and *at what cost* rather than *what*.
**Effect on the audience:** Foreknowledge converts automatically into dramatic irony across the whole runtime; every hopeful scene is shadowed. The audience is never asking what happens, so what they ask instead is what you must supply: how these particular people got there, and whether it could have gone otherwise.
**Used for and where it works best:** Heritage, historical, biographical and faith work, plus any adaptation of a known story. The craft rule: relocate the question. If the outcome is fixed, the open questions must be local and human — will he tell her before the ship sails, will the brother be forgiven, who carried the message. Second rule: build at least one *live* uncertainty the audience genuinely does not hold, usually a minor character's fate, and let it carry the suspense the main line cannot. Third: resist the temptation to hint that it might go differently. Audiences find false hope about settled history insulting, and it costs you the dignity that is the form's main asset. See `tens.known_outcome_dread` for the scene-level tension craft.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Heritage Documentary, Documentary, Novel, Stage Play | genres: Historical, Heritage, War, Tragedy, Social Drama, Journalism
**Avoid when:** The outcome is known but not *felt* — a piece of history your audience can recite and has no relationship to. Then foreknowledge gives you irony without weight, and you are better off structuring for discovery and letting them learn it as new.
**Example:**
> *Everyone in the room knows the city falls.*
> SCRIBE: Copy it twice. One stays, one goes south with the boy.
> OVERSEER: The boy is eleven.
> SCRIBE: The boy is what we have.
> *The suspense is not the city. It is whether anything in that satchel is ever read.*

**Yields to:** `info.suspense_shared_knowledge` — The outcome is known but not felt.  
`info.known_ending_structure`

### The Adaptation's Double Audience

**Also called:** the split house, readers and newcomers, the adaptation knowledge gap
**What it is:** Writing an adaptation for two audiences simultaneously — those who hold the source's reveals and those who do not — without boring the first or stranding the second.
**Effect on the audience:** Two different films watched at once. The knowing half experiences dramatic irony and anticipation; the unknowing half experiences mystery. Both are legitimate; the failure is serving one and forgetting the other.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any adaptation of well-known material, and any remake. The craft rule: keep the source's *events* and move its *emphases*. The knowing audience is watching for how you handle the moments they own, so give them craft rather than novelty — a scene they know, staged so it plays differently. Second rule: never reverse a famous reveal to surprise the knowing half. It reads as contempt for the source and it strands the newcomers, who now have a twist with no setup. If you must change something, change what a character wants, not what happens.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, One-Hour Drama, Stage Play, Novel | genres: Drama, Crime, Historical, Science Fiction, Mystery, Tragedy
**Avoid when:** The source's central pleasure is its twist and your audience is overwhelmingly composed of people who hold it. Then adapt the *situation* rather than the plot, or accept that you are making a piece about consequence.
**Example:**
> *Readers know he is the one who wrote the letters. So the film does not hide it — it opens on him writing one.*
> *What readers do not have: his wife in the doorway, three feet behind him, for the whole minute, saying nothing.*

**Yields to:** `info.known_ending_structure` — Everyone holds the twist already.  
`info.adaptation_double_audience`

### The "Based on True Events" Frame

**Also called:** the veracity card, the opening claim, the non-fiction promise
**What it is:** The title card, foreword or framing line that tells the audience what kind of truth they are receiving — true story, based on, inspired by, composite, fiction. A single piece of information delivered before the story that reconditions every other piece.
**Effect on the audience:** It changes how the whole work is graded. Under a truth claim, implausibility becomes fascinating rather than sloppy; coincidence is forgiven; ambiguity is read as honesty. Withdraw the claim and the same script is judged as craft.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it deliberately when your material contains something an audience would otherwise refuse to believe. The craft rule: the claim must be *calibrated to what you actually did*. "Based on" covers compression and composite characters; "a true story" does not cover a fabricated confrontation, and audiences who find out feel the specific betrayal reserved for non-fiction. Second rule: an end card is an information beat too — the closing text about what happened afterwards is the last thing your audience receives, and it should complete a question the film opened, not dump a biography. Third: if you composite people, say so somewhere; every filmmaker who hid it was eventually asked about it.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Documentary, Heritage Documentary, Short Film | genres: Historical, Crime, Journalism, Heritage, War, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** You have invented the spine. A truth frame on a substantially fictional story is not marketing, it is a false claim about real people, and in heritage work it poisons the well for everyone making honest films about the same subject.
**Example:**
> *Opening card, honest and specific:* "Between 1962 and 1971 the state removed 1,148 children from this valley. Two families are shown here. Their names, and the order of some events, have been changed."
> *Closing card, answering the film's question rather than reciting a life:* "The register was found in 2019. It is nine pages long. Forty-one of the names are given only as 'infant'."

`info.true_story_frame`

### The Audience That Will Correct You

**Also called:** the expert audience, the community fact-check, the heritage tripwire
**What it is:** Writing for a body of viewers who hold the subject's factual detail personally — diaspora communities, veterans, believers, specialists — and who experience an error not as a slip but as a claim about them.
**Effect on the audience:** One wrong detail costs the whole work's authority with the people who care most, and they are the ones who circulate it. Conversely, one *correct* obscure detail buys total trust in ninety seconds, because it signals that someone did the work.
**Used for and where it works best:** Essential in heritage, religious, military, medical and regional work. The craft rule: spend your accuracy budget on the details that community uses as a *tell* — a garment, a dialect word, a prayer's wording, an instrument, a flag, a date on a feast. Those are checked; the ones outside that set are not. Second rule: when you must compress or invent, do it visibly and in the frame rather than quietly — a stated composite is argued with, a silent error is dismissed. Third, and it is the one people skip: show the work to two people from inside the subject before you lock it, and ask them what made them wince, not what they liked. See `expo.heritage_specificity`.
**Best in:** formats: Heritage Documentary, Documentary, Feature Film, Limited Series, Reels/Shorts, Short Film | genres: Heritage, Historical, War, Social Drama, Journalism, Drama
**Avoid when:** Never avoid the accuracy — but do not let it become the film. Work written to survive a fact-check and nothing else is inert, and a community that cannot fault your detail can still find your film lifeless. Accuracy is a floor.
**Example:**
> *What they check:* whether the mourners' shawls are the right side of 1900, whether the chant is the one used at a burial and not at a wedding, whether that crown belongs to that century.
> *What they do not check:* the courtyard, the weather, the name of the street.
> *Spend the budget on the first line.*

`info.heritage_audience_correction`

## Named Failures

### The Reveal Nobody Was Waiting For

**Also called:** the unasked answer, the inert twist, the reveal without a question
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A late, heavily staged disclosure of information the audience never wondered about, because the story never posed the question it answers. All the machinery of a reveal — the music, the pause, the reaction shot — spent on a fact with no prior demand.
**Effect on the audience:** Politeness. They register that something important has happened to somebody and feel nothing, then quietly lose confidence in the rest of the ending. It is worse than a small reveal, because the staging promises a size the content cannot pay.
**Used for and where it works best:** It does not work. The diagnostic: for every reveal in the draft, write the question it answers as a sentence, and find the page where a *character says that sentence out loud*. No page, no reveal. The fix is almost never to cut the disclosure — it is to go back and have someone ask, early, in a scene about something else (`scene.scene_question`). A second, faster test: cut the reveal entirely and see whether anything upstream now dangles. If nothing does, nothing was waiting.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always. The one adjacent thing that does work is the deliberately small disclosure staged *small* — a fact nobody asked for, delivered flatly, as texture. The failure is the mismatch, not the fact.
**Example:**
> *Inert:* "The house was never in his name." *Music. Slow push in. Nobody has mentioned the house since page 12.*
> *Repaired, page 40:* "Whose name is this place in, anyway?" "Don't start." "I'm only asking whose name it's in."

**Yields to:** `scene.scene_question` — Someone must ask the question first.  
`info.reveal_nobody_wanted`

### The Answer to a Question Never Asked

**Also called:** unrequested backstory, the explanatory reveal, origin creep
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A disclosure that exists to explain a thing the audience had already accepted — why the villain is cruel, how the rule of the world came to be, where the scar came from. It answers the writer's anxiety, not the audience's curiosity.
**Effect on the audience:** Shrinkage. The explained thing becomes smaller than it was unexplained, and a figure who was frightening becomes a case history. This is the mechanism by which prequels reliably diminish the works they serve.
**Used for and where it works best:** It does not work as a reveal, though the same material can be fine as texture delivered early and cheap. The diagnostic: does any character, at any point, ask *why*? If nobody in the story wanted the explanation, your audience did not either. Second diagnostic: after the explanation, does the audience's fear or admiration of the subject *increase*? If not, cut it. The commonest carrier is a monologue in the last twenty minutes, and the commonest excuse is that it makes the antagonist three-dimensional, which it does not — behaviour does that (`anta.write_the_case`).
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always as a late reveal. If the information genuinely deepens the work, demote it: deliver it early, in half a line, as something everyone in the world already knows, and let it be a fact rather than an answer.
**Example:**
> *The failure, at 1:48:* "You want to know why I do this? When I was nine, my father—"
> *The same information, doing better work at 0:14, from a bored colleague:* "He grew up in the yards. He'll tell you if you let him. Don't let him."

**Yields to:** `anta.write_the_case` — Build the figure from behaviour, not origin.  
`info.answer_to_unasked_question`

### The Fact Told Three Times

**Also called:** redundant disclosure, the double-told fact, over-servicing
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** The same piece of information delivered more than once in the same form — stated in dialogue, restated in narration, then confirmed in a later scene — usually because different drafts each solved the same clarity worry.
**Effect on the audience:** Condescension, felt as boredom. They do not think "I was told that"; they think the piece is slow. It is also the single most reliable way to flag a fact as important, so a triple-told plant becomes a telegraph (`info.telegraphing`).
**Used for and where it works best:** It does not — and note the distinction from the two techniques it resembles. `info.rule_of_three_placements` repeats a fact in three *different registers* to normalise it; `info.multiple_paths_one_fact` supplies alternate routes for audiences who missed one. The failure is repetition in the *same* register with the same attitude. The diagnostic: run a search for your ten critical facts, list every instance, and check the attitude of each. Two instances with the same attitude means one is dead. The fix is to cut the earliest, not the latest, because the later instance is usually the one attached to a scene that needs it.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always, with one exception — audio-only work and content consumed with divided attention genuinely needs more redundancy than the page, and in podcast and radio drama two clean statements of a critical fact is craft, not padding.
**Example:**
> *Told three times:* "The lease ends in March." / NARRATION: The lease ended in March. / "You've got until March, you know that."
> *Once, and better:* "March." *She writes it on the back of her hand, in front of him, and he watches her do it.*

**Yields to:** `info.rule_of_three_placements` — Repeat in three different registers instead.  
`info.thrice_told_fact`

### The Mystery With No Stakes

**Also called:** the consequence-free question, the idle puzzle, curiosity without cost
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A well-constructed open question — clues, suspects, a proper reveal — attached to an outcome that changes nothing for anyone the audience cares about. The machinery of mystery running with nothing loaded into it.
**Effect on the audience:** Mild interest that decays fast, and the classic mid-season abandonment. They can tell you what the question is and cannot tell you why they should want it answered, which is the precise condition under which people stop watching without complaint.
**Used for and where it works best:** It does not work, and it is extremely common in second seasons and in prestige drama that mistakes a puzzle for an engine. The diagnostic is one sentence: *if the answer is X, who is hurt, and do we love them?* If the honest answer is "an institution" or "a dead stranger", you have curiosity without stakes. The fix is not a bigger answer — it is to attach the existing answer to a present-tense relationship, so that solving it costs the protagonist something they currently have. Note the related trap: a mystery whose answer only damages people we met in flashback has the same disease.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always in long form. In short form a stakes-free puzzle can survive ninety seconds on novelty alone, and in some kids' and puzzle formats curiosity is genuinely sufficient — but nothing over twenty minutes runs on it.
**Example:**
> *No stakes:* who moved the boundary stone in 1904? *The farm was sold in 1960. Everyone involved is dead.*
> *Stakes:* who moved the boundary stone in 1904 — because the compensation claim is filed on Friday, and his brother's name is on it, and he is the only one who has seen the original map.

**Yields to:** `info.cost_of_telling` — Attach a nameable present-tense cost.
