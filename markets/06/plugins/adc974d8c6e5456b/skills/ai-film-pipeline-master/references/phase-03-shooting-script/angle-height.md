# Angle, Height and Azimuth

58 cards. Also in the other stage: a shot has a frame too. These decide a frame: what is in it, how it is lit, and what it is made of.

Every card carries what it is, what it does to the audience, where it works best, what to avoid, and the prompt written out in full.

---

### Pick the Angle from the Claim

**Also called:** the angle selector, choosing the camera's position, angle from what the shot asserts
**What it is:** The head of the `angle.` shelf. Every angle on this shelf is an **assertion** the camera makes before anyone speaks — about a person's standing, about where the viewer is allowed to stand, about whether the world is sound, or about a surface being worth looking at. Choose the angle from **the assertion the shot has to make**, then from **how loudly it may say it**, then from **whether it is a state you hold or a change you have to cut**. Three questions, in that order, and the third is the one people skip.
**Effect on the audience:** They read the assertion before they read the line. An angle chosen from the shot list — *we have not had a low one yet* — asserts something anyway, and what it asserts is usually not what the scene means.
**Used for and where it works best:** Run it once per setup, before the shot size and before the lens. Answer the three questions out loud in one sentence — *this shot rules on his standing, at the felt dose, held* — and the shortlist is four or five cards, not the whole shelf. Where two of the three answers feel arbitrary, the scene is not decided yet and the angle is not what is missing.
**Best in:** formats: all | genres: all
**Avoid when:** The three questions all assume the scene's subject is already settled, and the map answers just as confidently when it is not. The tell is that two of the three answers feel arbitrary — when that happens the map will hand you a card, and the card will be wrong. **Do not trust it alone.** Shoot the zero, and decide in the edit.
**Prompt:** `not a prompt line but a procedure: name the assertion, name the dose, say held or changing — then read the three lists and take the intersection`

---

**The axes**

Three questions. Each was built by counting the conditions that repeat across every `Yields to:` field on the shelf and every selectable `Avoid when:` line. Every selectable card answers all three, and the three are independent of each other.

1. **What is this angle a claim about?** — *a person's standing* (who is above whom) · *where the viewer stands* (with them, beside them, outside them) · *whether the world is sound* · *a surface, an object or a place worth reading*.
   Built from thirty-seven conditions of the form *"on a character who must keep their authority"*, *"in an intimate two-hander it cools the scene"*, *"with no story reason it reads as an empty trick"*, *"when the ground says nothing the shot carries no meaning"*.
2. **At what dose — the zero, the felt, or the declared?** — The shelf's spine. Almost every family on it is a **dial pair**, and the `Yields to:` line names the dose in so many words: `angle.slight_high` → `angle.high` *"when weakness must be declared, not hinted"*; `angle.subtle_dutch` → `angle.hard_dutch` *"when the broken world must be declared, not sensed"*; `angle.azimuth_three_quarter_front` → `angle.azimuth_frontal` *"when confrontation must be declared"*; `angle.extreme_low_worms_eye` → `angle.eye_level` *"quiet dialogue, where myth overstates"*. Nineteen conditions.
3. **Is it held, or does it change — inside the shot, or only across cuts?** — The question people skip, because a shot list is written one row at a time and the cards under this axis's last two values **cannot be chosen one row at a time**. `angle.azimuth_shift`: *"too few setups for the rotation to read"*. `angle.hitchcock_high`: *"if you climb the height in two quick steps the device shows"*. `angle.power_shift`: *"give the viewer two shots for the new state to settle"*. `angle.dutch_settle`: *"when the state does not change inside the shot"*. Eleven conditions.

**The default, and the one against it**

**The default: `angle.eye_level` at `angle.azimuth_three_quarter_front`.** The shelf names both itself — eye level is *"the default position every other angle is measured against"*, three-quarter front is *"the default position in narrative cinema"*. Zero dose, held, and it refuses to rule on standing, which is right when the writing has not ruled either. It is also the card `angle.high`, `angle.extreme_low_worms_eye`, `angle.chest_level` and `angle.overhead_45` all fall back to when their beat does not carry them.

**Against the grain: `angle.low_height_level`.** Drop the camera to knee or waist and **do not tilt**. Every other standing card on this shelf tilts, and the tilt is what spends the meaning — the moment you tilt up you have bought glorification whether the scene wanted it or not (`angle.low_height_level` → `angle.low`, *"when the beat wants glorification, tilt up"*). Its own text says this is *"the distinction most people miss between height and angle."* The reason to reach for it: it gives you the child's eye, the room's weight, the dog's world and the pressure of a standing figure — at zero dose, for free, without the shot announcing anything. On a heritage or documentary piece where every declared angle costs credibility, it is the card that buys the most and says the least.

**Where the new thing is.** Five of these are worth naming, because nothing on the shelf will ever route you to them and they are not marginal:

- **`angle.tatami_ozu`** — one of only two cards on the shelf with no `Yields to:` at all (`PAIR-AUDIT.md`: `execution-note`). Nothing substitutes for it. It is the only card that gets parity by **joining** the seated subject rather than standing level with them, and the seated conversation is the most-covered scene in the package.
- **`angle.power_shift`** — it is not an angle, it is a pair of angles and the moment between them. The only card on the shelf that tells you to choose for the **scene** rather than the shot.
- **`angle.azimuth_shift`** — the same idea on the horizontal: a rotation used as a dramatic line across a scene. Unreachable from any single-shot question, including mine, unless axis 3 is answered honestly.
- **`angle.low_through_glass`** and **`angle.worms_eye_grass`** — both put a **layer** between viewer and subject. No other card on the shelf does. That is an unclaimed axis, not a leaf: a fourth question — *is there something between the lens and the subject?* — would have two members today and is the most likely place the next card comes from.
- **`angle.stairs_up_down`** — recorded in `PAIR-AUDIT.md` as `only-one`: *nothing in the package does this job differently.* It makes the architecture the meter of class. Unreachable from a question about the camera.

### Eye Level

**Also called:** neutral angle, straight-on  
**What it is:** The camera sits at the subject's eye height with no tilt up or down. This is the default position every other angle is measured against.  
**Effect on the audience:** Neutrality and parity. The viewer sees the character the way they would see a person sitting opposite them, with no judgement and no sympathy imposed.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Natural dialogue, the interview, and any scene where the viewer should judge the character for themselves. In documentary and reportage it is the baseline because it reads as honest, and in drama you use it as zero so that every later departure from it carries meaning.  
**Best in:** formats: all | genres: all  
**Avoid when:** When you want authority, weakness or instability, eye level will not deliver it. And if a whole film sits at eye level you have thrown away an entire tool of meaning.  
**Prompt:** `eye-level angle, camera at the subject's eye height, no tilt, neutral and steady`  
**Yields to:** `angle.low` — When the shot must confer authority, not parity.  
`angle.eye_level`

### Low Angle

**Also called:** low shot, up-angle, aori (anime storyboard term)  
**What it is:** The camera sits below the subject's eye height and tilts up toward them. Ceiling or sky enters the frame behind them.  
**Effect on the audience:** The subject grows taller, stronger and more dangerous. The viewer feels smaller, looking up the way a child looks at adults.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The entrance of a king or a commander, the moment of victory, the villain announcing himself, and monumental architecture: the temple, the ziggurat, the statue in heritage documentary. In battle anime it carries the final line before the attack, and in car and sports advertising it turns the product or the athlete into a hero.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Trailer / Teaser, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Anime (Japanese 2D), Commercial / TVC, Sports Broadcast / Sports Film, Music Video, Game Cinematic / Gameplay, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography | genres: Action, Fantasy / Epic, Superhero, Mythology / Ancient Epic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, War, Shonen Battle (anime), Sports Drama  
**Avoid when:** On a weak or defeated character it turns into mockery unless the irony is deliberate. And if every shot is a low angle, nobody is bigger than anybody.  
**Prompt:** `low angle looking up at him, camera below eye level tilted upward, he towers over the frame with the sky behind`  
**Yields to:** `angle.high` — On a weak or defeated character, where it mocks.  
`angle.low`

### Extreme Low Angle / Worm's Eye View

**Also called:** worm's-eye, extreme up-angle  
**What it is:** The camera is flat on the ground or below knee height and tilts up hard, sometimes almost vertical. The figure or the building is seen entirely from underneath.  
**Effect on the audience:** Myth. The character becomes a giant or a god and the building becomes a mountain. Vertical lines converge above, so the sense of height is doubled.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Fantasy and epic when a creature or a leader has to read as more than human, and the beat where the villain climbs to his throne. In heritage work, from the foot of the ziggurat or the Ishtar Gate, so the viewer feels the scale of what the ancients built. In vertical short-form it is an opening shot, because the angle is strange enough to stop the thumb at once.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Trailer / Teaser, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Anime (Japanese 2D), 3D CG Animation, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Music Video, Game Cinematic / Gameplay, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography | genres: Fantasy / Epic, Mythology / Ancient Epic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Superhero, Monster / Kaiju, Horror, Shonen Battle (anime), Mecha (anime)  
**Avoid when:** In quiet dialogue it becomes comic overstatement. And do not put it on a close face with a wide lens unless you want the features distorted on purpose.  
**Prompt:** `worm's eye view, camera flat on the ground looking almost straight up, the figure towers into the sky, vertical lines converging above`  
**Yields to:** `angle.eye_level` — Quiet dialogue, where myth overstates.  
`angle.extreme_low_worms_eye`

### Ground Level Shot / Floor Level Shot

**Also called:** floor shot, ground shot  
**What it is:** The camera hugs the floor, the lens only centimetres up, and in most cases stays level rather than tilting up. The ground itself fills half the frame.  
**Effect on the audience:** We are down with the dust, the insect, the animal, the crawler and the dead. Feet become characters and anything standing reads as far away and tall.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Feet crossing a floor that tells the story: desert sand, temple paving, a battlefield after it is over. In wildlife documentary it is the small animal's own angle, and in horror it is the floor shot when something drags or crawls toward the camera.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Short Film, Documentary Feature, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Documentary Series, Anime (Japanese 2D), Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), AI-Generated Clip, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography | genres: Nature / Wildlife, War, Western, Horror, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Survival, Drama, Slice of Life  
**Avoid when:** When the ground says nothing, plain office tile for instance, the shot carries no meaning. And tilted far up it becomes a worm's eye view, which is a different angle with a different meaning.  
**Prompt:** `ground level shot, lens a few centimetres above the floor, level not tilted, boots crossing the frame with dust kicking up`  
**Yields to:** `angle.extreme_low_worms_eye` — Once the camera tilts up, that is the other card.  
`angle.ground_level`

### Low Height, Level Angle

**Also called:** low camera height without tilt, child height level  
**What it is:** The camera is low, at knee or waist height, but perfectly level with no upward tilt. The height changed and the angle stayed at zero. This is the distinction most people miss between height and angle.  
**Effect on the audience:** A strange and quietly powerful feeling: we are in a low world with nobody being glorified. Adults get cut off by the frame and their lives carry on above our heads.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Children's films and kids content, because it literally places the viewer in a child's world where adults are legs and voices. In drama use it to observe a character without judging them, and in sitcom and slice of life it is the calm room shot.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Kids Content, 2D Animation (Western / Cartoon), 3D CG Animation, Drama Series Episode, Short Film, Documentary Feature, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography, Interior Photography | genres: Family, Preschool / Early Learning, Coming of Age, Slice of Life, Drama, Fairy Tale  
**Avoid when:** Do not confuse it with the low angle: the moment the camera tilts up the meaning changes completely and becomes glorification. And in action the movement escapes above the frame.  
**Prompt:** `camera at a child's eye height, about one metre off the ground, perfectly level, adults cut off at the waist as they pass`  
**Example:** E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial (1982), the early scenes framed at the children's height with the adults' faces kept out of frame.  
**Yields to:** `angle.low` — When the beat wants glorification, tilt up.  
`angle.low_height_level`

### Tatami Shot / Ozu Low Static

**Also called:** tatami level, Ozu angle, pillow-height camera  
**What it is:** A locked-off camera at the height of a person seated on a tatami mat, about half a metre or less, level or tilted up slightly, with no movement at all. The style of the Japanese director Yasujiro Ozu.  
**Effect on the audience:** Respect, calm and contemplation. The camera is seated with the people instead of standing over them, so you feel like a silent guest in the house and time slows down.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Home and family scenes, quiet conversation around food, and the wordless farewell. In slice of life and family drama it gives dignity without melodrama, and in gentle anime it is the default angle for a tatami room.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Anime (Japanese 2D), Short Film, Documentary Feature, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography, Interior Photography, Real Estate Photography, Documentary Photography | genres: Slice of Life, Drama, Family, Tragedy, Romance, Seinen (mature anime)  
**Avoid when:** In action, or any scene built on fast rhythm, the stillness strangles the movement. And do not add camera movement to it; the stillness is what makes the meaning.  
**Prompt:** `static tatami-level shot, camera about half a metre off the floor, level, locked off, a family seated around a low table`  
**Example:** Tokyo Story (1953), Yasujiro Ozu, the family scenes shot from seated height.  
`angle.tatami_ozu`

### Knee Level Shot

**Also called:** knee height, knee shot  
**What it is:** The camera sits at the subject's knee height, level or tilted up slightly. You see legs, hanging hands and the floor close by.  
**Effect on the audience:** The world of a child or an animal, or an approaching threat, because we see the steps before the face. The subject feels larger without reaching full glorification.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Approaching footsteps in thriller and horror: the camera at knee height and the feet entering frame before we know who it is. In the western it is the hands beside the gun, and in kids content it is the puppy's or the toddler's angle as they move among adults.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Short Film, Kids Content, 2D Animation (Western / Cartoon), 3D CG Animation, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Trailer / Teaser, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography | genres: Thriller / Suspense, Horror, Western, Family, Preschool / Early Learning, Crime / Procedural, Slasher  
**Avoid when:** When the face carries the scene, knee level throws it away. And do not use it for long dialogue.  
**Prompt:** `knee-level shot, camera about knee height looking slightly up, footsteps entering frame before the face is revealed`  
**Yields to:** `angle.chest_level` — Long dialogue, or when the face carries the scene.  
`angle.knee_level`

### Hip Level Shot / Gun Level

**Also called:** hip shot, holster level, waist level  
**What it is:** The camera at the subject's hip height, usually level. Hands, belt, pockets and gun sit in the middle of the frame while the face is above it or cut off.  
**Effect on the audience:** The tension of waiting: the eye is on the hand that will draw, not on the face. On a walking subject it reads as swagger, confidence and weight.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The western duel, hands beside the belt before the draw. In crime and gangster stories it is the shot of the hand going into a pocket or passing an envelope. In fashion it is the walking shot that shows the belt, the trousers and the way the body moves.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Trailer / Teaser, Fashion Film, Music Video, Commercial / TVC, Game Cinematic / Gameplay, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography, Interior Photography | genres: Western, Crime / Procedural, Gangster, Action, Noir / Neo-Noir, Thriller / Suspense, Samurai / Chanbara  
**Avoid when:** In emotional scenes that need the face. And if nothing important is happening at hip height, a weapon, a hand, something in a pocket, the shot has no reason to exist.  
**Prompt:** `hip-level shot, camera at waist height, level, framing the hand hovering beside the holster, face cut off above frame`  
**Yields to:** `angle.chest_level` — Emotional scenes that need the face.  
`angle.hip_level`

### Chest Level

**Also called:** chest height camera  
**What it is:** The camera at the subject's chest height, slightly below the eyes, usually level or tilted up by a degree or two.  
**Effect on the audience:** Less formal than eye level and more present. The subject feels closer and heavier without the viewer sensing that they are looking up.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The modern default for dialogue and walking coverage: in drama and series when you want natural closeness without glorification. In a documentary interview it gives the guest a little weight - a choice to make on purpose, because on testimony `angle.interview_45` holds the camera at eye height and treats that weight as unearned - and in a vlog it puts the speaker nearer to the viewer than an eye level camera does.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Limited Series, Documentary Feature, Interview / Video Podcast, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography, Interior Photography | genres: Drama, Crime / Procedural, Romance, Verite / Observational, Coming of Age, Slice of Life  
**Avoid when:** When you want full neutrality, a legal interview or a testimony, lift it back to eye level. And on a child, chest height becomes a high angle looking down at them.  
**Prompt:** `chest-level camera, slightly below the eyes and level, natural and close, framed as a medium shot`  
**Yields to:** `angle.eye_level` — Full neutrality: legal interview or testimony.  
`angle.chest_level`

### Shoulder Level Shot

**Also called:** shoulder height  
**What it is:** The camera at the subject's shoulder height, between chest and eye, level. The shoulder passes just below the lens and the neck and jaw sit comfortably in frame.  
**Effect on the audience:** Closeness and companionship, as if you were standing beside the character or walking with them. The face sits just above centre, which gives it a quiet authority.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Walking and accompanying shots in drama and documentary when you want the viewer to feel they are moving alongside the character. It is also the natural height for over-the-shoulder coverage in dialogue, and for a vertical piece filmed by a second person walking with the speaker.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Short Film, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography, Interior Photography | genres: Drama, Crime / Procedural, Verite / Observational, Road Movie, Coming of Age, Romance  
**Avoid when:** Do not confuse it with the over-the-shoulder shot: this is a height, that is a relational framing. And on a group of mixed heights, somebody always ends up being looked down at.  
**Prompt:** `shoulder-level camera, level, tracking beside her as she walks, her face slightly above centre frame`  
**Yields to:** `frame.ots` — When the need is relational framing, not a camera height.  
`angle.shoulder_level`

### Slight High Angle / Just Above Head

**Also called:** above-head camera, soft high angle  
**What it is:** The camera sits a little above head height and tilts down by a few degrees, 5 to 15. It never reaches a declared high angle.  
**Effect on the audience:** Light surveillance. The viewer feels they are looking over the character without openly judging them, a small sense of worry or of tenderness.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The first beat of worry before a character is exposed: somebody at a desk waiting for news, a sleeping child watched by their mother. In thriller and crime it is the angle that makes the viewer feel someone is watching without knowing why, and in advertising it gives a product on a table the natural look a buyer has standing over it.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Short Film, Commercial / TVC, Product Video / Motion Design, Documentary Feature, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography, Interior Photography | genres: Thriller / Suspense, Drama, Crime / Procedural, Mystery / Whodunit, Family, Romance  
**Avoid when:** Do not use it in place of a full high angle when you want declared weakness; it does not get there. And on a character who must stay strong in the scene it drains them without anyone noticing.  
**Prompt:** `slight high angle, camera just above head height tilted down about ten degrees, quietly watching him at the desk`  
**Yields to:** `angle.high` — When weakness must be declared, not hinted.  
`angle.slight_high`

### High Angle

**Also called:** down-angle, looking down  
**What it is:** The camera sits above the subject's eye height and tilts down toward them. The ground fills the background instead of the sky.  
**Effect on the audience:** The subject shrinks, gets boxed in and is exposed. The viewer takes the position of the one who sees everything while the character can no longer hide.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The beat of weakness, defeat, illness or exposure: the suspect breaking down, a child in front of adults, a wounded man on the ground. In drama, pair it in the same scene with a low angle on the other party so the power between them is built by the cutting. In documentary it works on a map or a scale model so the viewer sits above the geography.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Limited Series, Short Film, Documentary Feature, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Anime (Japanese 2D), Trailer / Teaser, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography | genres: Drama, Thriller / Suspense, Crime / Procedural, War, Horror, Tragedy, Historical / Biopic, Psychological Horror  
**Avoid when:** On a character who has to stay strong it quietly strips their authority. And if you use it on every moment of weakness the film starts explaining itself.  
**Prompt:** `high angle looking down at her, camera above head height tilted down, she reads small against the floor`  
**Yields to:** `angle.eye_level` — On a character who must keep their authority.  
`angle.high`

### Extreme High Angle / Bird's Eye / Overhead / Top-Down / God's Eye

**Also called:** overhead shot, top shot, god's eye view, plan view  
**What it is:** The camera sits at a full 90 degrees above the subject looking straight down. The frame turns into a map: no horizon and no faces. This is the general angle for crowds, spaces and plans, unlike The God's-Eye Overhead in the signature moves chapter, which lands on one person as a judgement.  
**Effect on the audience:** It turns people into geometric pattern and strips their individuality, or it reads as a higher power watching: fate, history, death.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The crime scene, the body, the crowd, the desert, the battlefield, and the closing beat you want the viewer to withdraw from emotionally. In vertical short-form it is the strongest opening shot because the eye never sees this in daily life, and in heritage documentary it reveals the plan of a city or a temple in a single frame.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Documentary Feature, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Trailer / Teaser, Music Video, Commercial / TVC, Game Cinematic / Gameplay, AI-Generated Clip, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography | genres: Crime / Procedural, True Crime, War, Thriller / Suspense, Drama, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Mystery / Whodunit, Disaster, Psychological / Arthouse  
**Avoid when:** When you want sympathy: from directly above there are no faces and no eyes. And do not drop it into an intimate dialogue scene; it cuts the bond between viewer and character.  
**Prompt:** `top-down overhead shot, perfect 90 degree bird's eye view, subject centred on the ground, geometric composition`  
**Example:** Busby Berkeley's 1930s musical numbers, the dancers filmed from directly overhead as a kaleidoscope pattern.  
**Source:** reference — Library of Congress, National Film Registry essay on "Footlight Parade" (1933) by Randy Skretvedt, https://www.loc.gov/static/programs/national-film-preservation-board/documents/footlight_parade.pdf, checked 2026-09-24; supports that Berkeley's 1933 numbers were built on camera angles and the grouping of the chorus into geometric patterns rather than on dancing; the essay does not itself state the overhead camera position.  
**Yields to:** `sig.gods_eye_overhead` — When the overhead judges one person, not a crowd; and `angle.eye_level` — you want empathy; from directly above there are no faces.  
`angle.extreme_high_birds_eye`

### The Hitchcock High Angle

**Also called:** vulnerability high angle, diminishing angle  
**What it is:** A working principle of Alfred Hitchcock: when a character reaches their weakest point, raise the camera above them, and the weaker they get the higher it goes. The angle tracks the character's state, not the geography of the room.  
**Effect on the audience:** The viewer feels the descent before understanding it. Weakness is built gradually instead of being stated in dialogue.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The long collapse scene: every cut back to the character comes from a slightly higher position until the last one is straight down. Strong in thriller, psychological drama and crime when a character loses control of a room they used to own, and in a trailer a single one of these delivers the defeat with no dialogue.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Limited Series, Trailer / Teaser, Short Film, Anime (Japanese 2D), Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography, Interior Photography, Real Estate Photography | genres: Thriller / Suspense, Drama, Crime / Procedural, Psychological Horror, Noir / Neo-Noir, Mystery / Whodunit, Tragedy  
**Avoid when:** If you climb the height in two quick steps the device shows and reads as mechanical. And do not use it on a character who loses nothing in the scene.  
**Prompt:** `high angle on him that rises with each cut, camera climbing from just above head height to a steep look straight down as he loses control`  
**Example:** Psycho (1960), Alfred Hitchcock, the overhead angle on the staircase killing.  
**Yields to:** `angle.high` — Too few setups to climb; one declared high angle.  
`angle.hitchcock_high`

### The Dollhouse Angle / Miniature Overhead

**Also called:** dollhouse framing, doll's house shot  
**What it is:** A locked overhead or steeply high angle that makes the room and the people in it look like a toy model: clean walls, arranged furniture, small figures inside a box. A device used by Ari Aster.  
**Effect on the audience:** The viewer loses the sense that the characters are free. Somebody is moving them, and the horror comes from the loss of will rather than from a monster.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Psychological and family horror: the scene where the viewer has to feel the family is already fated. Plant it early to set the idea and repeat it at the end to close it. In advertising and product work the same angle makes a space look designed and perfect, and it is useful when a whole house or set has to be read in one shot.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Short Film, Drama Series Episode, Commercial / TVC, Product Video / Motion Design, 3D CG Animation, Stop Motion, AI-Generated Clip, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography | genres: Psychological Horror, Horror, Supernatural Horror, Drama, Tragedy, Surreal / Absurdist, Folk Horror  
**Avoid when:** When you want the viewer inside the scene, this angle keeps them outside watching. It weakens with repetition, so keep it to two or three times in a film.  
**Prompt:** `locked high angle looking down into the room like a dollhouse, walls and furniture squared to frame, the figures small inside the box`  
**Example:** Hereditary (2018), Ari Aster, the opening shot that moves from a miniature model house into the real bedroom.  
**Yields to:** `angle.overhead_45` — When the viewer must stay inside the scene.  
`angle.dollhouse_overhead`

### Overhead Descent / Top-Down Drop-In

**Also called:** descending overhead, crane down top-down  
**What it is:** Start from a high vertical angle looking down and let the camera descend straight toward the subject while holding the same angle. The frame closes on the target and the angle never changes.  
**Effect on the audience:** The feeling of a slow fall, or of a gaze closing in. The viewer descends from the place of fate toward the small detail that will decide everything.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Opening a scene or a chapter: begin on the roof or the courtyard from above and descend onto the thing that matters, blood on tile, a letter, a sleeping face. In heritage documentary, start on the site plan and come down onto a carving or a tablet, and in vertical short-form it holds the viewer from the first second because the movement reads with no words.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Short Film, Documentary Feature, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Trailer / Teaser, Music Video, Commercial / TVC, Game Cinematic / Gameplay, AI-Generated Clip | genres: Crime / Procedural, Thriller / Suspense, Drama, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Mystery / Whodunit, True Crime, War, Historical / Biopic  
**Avoid when:** If the camera tilts on the way down it becomes an ordinary move and the effect is gone. And do not use it when the thing you land on is not worth the wait.  
**Prompt:** `straight down overhead shot descending toward him, camera holds 90 degrees while dropping from high above to just over his body`  
**Yields to:** `angle.extreme_high_birds_eye` — When the landing does not earn the descent.  
`angle.overhead_descent`

### Subtle Dutch Angle

**Also called:** soft canted angle, micro tilt, slight cant  
**What it is:** The camera is rolled to one side by only 3 to 10 degrees. The horizon is off level but the viewer does not consciously register the tilt.  
**Effect on the audience:** Unease with no identifiable source. This is stronger than a declared tilt because it works under the surface: the image looks right and yet something is wrong.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The lying or suspicion scene: the character talks, everything looks normal, but the frame is off by a few degrees and the viewer senses something hidden. Strong in thriller, noir and psychological horror. In a crime interrogation, cant the suspect's coverage and keep the detective's level.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Limited Series, Short Film, Trailer / Teaser, Anime (Japanese 2D), Game Cinematic / Gameplay, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography | genres: Thriller / Suspense, Noir / Neo-Noir, Psychological Horror, Crime / Procedural, Mystery / Whodunit, Horror, Drama, Spy / Espionage  
**Avoid when:** In a settled or romantic scene it spoils the calm for no reason. And held across a whole film it becomes a style and stops being a signal.  
**Prompt:** `subtle dutch angle, horizon tilted about eight degrees, otherwise natural framing, quiet unease`  
**Yields to:** `angle.hard_dutch` — When the broken world must be declared, not sensed.  
`angle.subtle_dutch`

### Hard Dutch Angle

**Also called:** extreme canted angle, heavy cant, oblique angle  
**What it is:** A roll of 30 degrees or more, sometimes reaching 45. The horizon is visibly broken and the viewer sees the tilt at once.  
**Effect on the audience:** A declared statement that the world has broken: madness, poisoning, a dream, vertigo, open evil. It does not hide itself, it wants to be noticed.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The peak of a collapse, or the villain's entrance in a trailer, a music video or a game spot. In battle anime and superhero work it has become standard battle grammar, and in horror one of these in the right place beats ten of them.  
**Best in:** formats: Trailer / Teaser, Music Video, Anime (Japanese 2D), Game Cinematic / Gameplay, Feature Film, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Commercial / TVC, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography, Interior Photography | genres: Horror, Action, Superhero, Psychological Horror, Surreal / Absurdist, Cyberpunk, Shonen Battle (anime), Noir / Neo-Noir  
**Avoid when:** Repeated more than twice in a film with no reason it becomes a cliche and gets laughed at. The exception is a film built on it from start to finish. And in serious documentary it destroys credibility.  
**Prompt:** `hard dutch angle, camera rolled about 35 degrees, horizon cutting the frame diagonally, unstable and aggressive`  
**Example:** The Third Man (1949), the canted angles running through the Vienna night scenes.  
**Yields to:** `angle.subtle_dutch` — Serious documentary, or when repetition would cheapen it.  
`angle.hard_dutch`

### Dutch Settle / Roll to Level

**Also called:** roll to horizon, unwind the cant, settling cant, creeping dutch  
**What it is:** The shot starts canted and rolls on the lens axis until it reaches level, or the reverse: it starts level, cants across the shot and settles tilted until the cut. The tilt becomes an action inside the shot instead of a fixed state.  
**Effect on the audience:** The sense that something has snapped back into place, or that it is beginning to slip, and it is far stronger than a shot that was already canted when it started, because the viewer feels the world go wrong as it happens. Settling to level relaxes the viewer physically; rolling away from level tightens them slowly.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The moment a character wakes, comes back to themselves or decides to take control: start canted and settle to level on their first calm breath. Reverse it for poison, sedation or collapse, or for the moment betrayal lands and a mind takes its first crack. Strong in thriller, horror, music video and long dialogue shots when the state has to change inside one shot with no cut.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Limited Series, Short Film, Music Video, Trailer / Teaser, Anime (Japanese 2D), Game Cinematic / Gameplay, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), AI-Generated Clip | genres: Thriller / Suspense, Horror, Psychological Horror, Action, Surreal / Absurdist, Drama, Cyberpunk, Noir / Neo-Noir, Mystery / Whodunit, Supernatural Horror, Crime / Procedural  
**Avoid when:** Rolled too slowly with no dramatic reason it reads as a broken camera rather than a choice, and stacking it with another camera move in the same shot loses the effect. Do not over-cant either; too large an angle turns dread into comedy.  
**Prompt:** `shot begins with the horizon rolled 25 degrees and slowly rotates back to level as she opens her eyes, ending perfectly straight`  
**Yields to:** `angle.subtle_dutch` — When the state does not change inside the shot.  
`angle.dutch_settle`

### Dutch Roll / Camera Roll

**Also called:** roll axis move, rotating frame  
**What it is:** The camera rotates continuously around the lens axis during the shot, sometimes 180 degrees, sometimes a full turn. The image itself inverts, not the subject.  
**Effect on the audience:** A total loss of orientation: there is no up and no down. It suits falling, an explosion, space, dreams and any break from reality.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The fall or the blast in action, and the zero gravity beat in sci-fi. In music video and dance it is a rhythmic move landed on the beat, and in vertical short-form a 180 degree roll across a transition earns the replay.  
**Best in:** formats: Music Video, Trailer / Teaser, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Feature Film, Game Cinematic / Gameplay, Anime (Japanese 2D), Dance Film, AI-Generated Clip | genres: Action, Sci-Fi, Space Opera, Surreal / Absurdist, Cyberpunk, Horror, Superhero, Musical  
**Avoid when:** In dialogue, or any scene that depends on clear geography, the roll destroys the space. Held too long it makes the viewer genuinely dizzy.  
**Prompt:** `camera rolls continuously around the lens axis through the shot, horizon turning a full 180 degrees as he falls backward`  
**Yields to:** `angle.dutch_settle` — When the geography must stay readable.  
`angle.dutch_roll`

### The Villain Low Angle

**Also called:** menace up-angle, threat low angle  
**What it is:** A low angle built differently from the hero version: closer, a slightly wider lens, the face off centre, and top light so the brow shadows the eyes.  
**Effect on the audience:** Threat rather than admiration. The subject reads as bigger than the viewer and is never fully seen, and the missing part is what frightens.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The villain's entrance, the reveal of who he is, and the line that flips the scene. Strong in horror, crime and battle anime, and in a trailer it is the shot that sits right before the title card.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Trailer / Teaser, Anime (Japanese 2D), Game Cinematic / Gameplay, Music Video, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), 3D CG Animation, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography | genres: Horror, Crime / Procedural, Thriller / Suspense, Superhero, Gangster, Shonen Battle (anime), Noir / Neo-Noir, Monster / Kaiju  
**Avoid when:** Lit like the hero version it turns into admiration instead of fear; the difference is in the light as much as in the angle. And do not use it on a villain who is meant to stay ordinary and hidden.  
**Prompt:** `low angle close on him, camera below chest height tilted up, top light shadowing his eyes, face pushed off centre`  
**Yields to:** `angle.hero_low` — When the subject is to be admired, not feared.  
`angle.villain_low`

### The Hero Low Angle

**Also called:** heroic up-angle, hero shot  
**What it is:** A moderate low angle, 10 to 25 degrees, at medium distance, the face fully readable and the eyes lit, with sky or a backlight behind.  
**Effect on the audience:** Respect, confidence and a fresh start. The viewer looks up at the character without fear.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Standing up after the fall, the hero walking into the arena, the athlete in a sports spot. In heritage work it is the angle for the king or the priest standing before the temple, and in advertising it makes the user look assured while holding the product.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Trailer / Teaser, Commercial / TVC, Sports Broadcast / Sports Film, Music Video, Anime (Japanese 2D), Heritage / Historical Documentary, Game Cinematic / Gameplay, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography | genres: Action, Adventure, Superhero, Sports Drama, Fantasy / Epic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Shonen Battle (anime)  
**Avoid when:** On a character who has not earned the beat yet it becomes a false promise and weakens the ending. And if you push the tilt further it flips into the villain version.  
**Prompt:** `moderate low angle on him from mid chest height, eyes catching the light, sky and backlight behind, confident and open`  
**Yields to:** `angle.villain_low` — When the subject must threaten rather than inspire.  
`angle.hero_low`

### Low Angle With Ceilings

**Also called:** ceiling shot, low angle showing the ceiling  
**What it is:** A low angle set so the ceiling enters the frame above the subject. Orson Welles had real ceilings built into his sets so the camera could go this low.  
**Effect on the audience:** The room closes over the head. The subject reads as powerful and trapped at once: they fill the frame, and yet there is no way out above them.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Scenes of cornered power: the boss in his office, the detective in the interrogation room, a family in a low ceilinged room in period drama. Strong in noir, drama and crime when you want to say that authority is boxed in without writing a line for it.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Limited Series, Short Film, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Docudrama / Reenactment, Anime (Japanese 2D), Game Cinematic / Gameplay, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography | genres: Noir / Neo-Noir, Crime / Procedural, Drama, Historical / Biopic, Thriller / Suspense, Political Thriller, Tragedy, Gangster  
**Avoid when:** A blank textureless ceiling becomes dead space that pulls the eye. And in scenes that need openness and freedom it gives the exact opposite.  
**Prompt:** `low angle from waist height looking up at him, the ceiling visible above his head pressing down, the room feels closed`  
**Example:** Citizen Kane (1941), Orson Welles, the low angles that show the ceilings of the sets.  
**Yields to:** `angle.low` — When the scene needs openness; sky, not ceiling.  
`angle.low_with_ceiling`

### Low Angle Through Glass or Water

**Also called:** under-glass up-angle, from beneath the surface  
**What it is:** The camera sits under a transparent surface, a glass floor, a glass table, the surface of water, and looks up through it. The surface becomes a layer between viewer and subject.  
**Effect on the audience:** The sense of being somewhere we are not supposed to be. Reflection and refraction turn the scene into a dream, or into a threat sitting under the surface.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Drowning and suffocation in thriller and survival: from beneath the water while the figure thrashes above. In food and product work, shooting up through a glass table shows the hand and the product in a way that stops the scroll, and in horror it is the angle of whatever is watching from under the floor or from inside the pool.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Commercial / TVC, Product Video / Motion Design, Music Video, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Short Film, Documentary Feature, AI-Generated Clip, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography | genres: Thriller / Suspense, Horror, Survival, Drama, Supernatural Horror, Sci-Fi, Psychological / Arthouse  
**Avoid when:** A dirty pane or an uncontrolled reflection wrecks the image. And do not use it in a scene that needs the expression read clearly.  
**Prompt:** `low angle from beneath a glass surface looking up, hands pressing on the glass above the lens, reflections across the frame`  
**Yields to:** `angle.low` — When the expression must read clearly.  
`angle.low_through_glass`

### Under-the-Table Shot

**Also called:** below table angle, under the desk shot  
**What it is:** The camera sits below the tabletop looking at what happens underneath: knees, hands, legs. The space under the table becomes the scene.  
**Effect on the audience:** A secret. The viewer sees what the people at the table cannot, and becomes a partner in hidden information.  
**Used for and where it works best:** A pistol resting on a knee, a hand finding another hand, a leg shaking with fear, an envelope passed below. Strong in crime, suspense and romance, and in comedy it is the surprise reveal under a family dinner table.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Short Film, Sitcom / Multi-Cam Comedy, Commercial / TVC, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Anime (Japanese 2D), Vertical Micro-Drama, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography | genres: Crime / Procedural, Thriller / Suspense, Romance, Comedy, Gangster, Drama, Dark Comedy / Satire, Heist  
**Avoid when:** If nothing is happening down there the shot is filler. And if you reveal the secret too early you lose the tension you were building above the table.  
**Prompt:** `low angle from under the table looking across at their knees, his hand resting on a pistol out of sight of the others`  
**Yields to:** `angle.hip_level` — The passed envelope, read from table height.  
`angle.under_table`

### Low Over-the-Shoulder

**Also called:** intimidation OTS, up-angle over shoulder  
**What it is:** An over-the-shoulder taken from low and tilted up onto the far character, so the near shoulder sits as a dark mass at the bottom and the far face rises above it.  
**Effect on the audience:** Declared dominance: the one we look at is above us and we are not protected. The viewer takes the position of the one being pressed.  
**Used for and where it works best:** A confrontation between weak and strong: cover the weaker one over the shoulder from below and the stronger one over the shoulder from above, and the difference alone tells the story. Strong in crime, legal drama and father and son scenes, and in anime it is the shot of the master or the stronger opponent.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Limited Series, Anime (Japanese 2D), Short Film, Trailer / Teaser, Game Cinematic / Gameplay, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography, Interior Photography | genres: Crime / Procedural, Drama, Legal Drama, Thriller / Suspense, Shonen Battle (anime), Martial Arts / Wuxia, Gangster, Tragedy  
**Avoid when:** Shoot both sides from the same low angle and the power disappears and the scene flattens. And in a dialogue between equals the camera should not take a side.  
**Prompt:** `low over-the-shoulder, camera below the near shoulder looking up at the other man, his face high in frame and the shoulder dark in the corner`  
**Yields to:** `frame.ots` — A dialogue between equals, where the camera takes no side.  
`angle.ots_low`

### Rear Angle / Trailing Angle

**Also called:** following angle, behind the subject, back shot  
**What it is:** The camera sits behind the subject at shoulder height, looking forward in the same direction they are. The figure holds the foreground while the world ahead of them stays open.  
**Effect on the audience:** We travel with them instead of watching them. The viewer receives the information about the space at the same instant the character does.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Entering a new space: a door opening, a corridor, a temple, a battlefield. Strong in heritage documentary, in game work and in vertical short-form because the viewer feels they are the one walking in, and it is the best angle when the face must stay hidden.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Game Cinematic / Gameplay, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Documentary Feature, Music Video, Anime (Japanese 2D), Trailer / Teaser, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography | genres: Adventure, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Action, Drama, Mystery / Whodunit, Fantasy / Epic, Survival  
**Avoid when:** Held too long with no reveal of the face it tires the viewer and the bond with the character fades. And do not confuse it with the full back azimuth: this one travels with the subject, that one is a fixed position used for endings.  
**Prompt:** `rear angle behind her at shoulder height, camera following as she steps through the gateway, her face never seen`  
**Yields to:** `angle.azimuth_full_back` — A fixed ending, not travelling with them.  
`angle.rear_angle`

### Balcony Height

**Also called:** mezzanine height, gallery level  
**What it is:** The camera sits one or two storeys up, a balcony, a railing, an upper window, tilting down at a moderate angle. Not a bird's eye and not an ordinary high angle; it lives between them.  
**Effect on the audience:** A detached observer. We see the whole scene and the relationships inside it but we are not in it, like somebody watching a party from a balcony.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The crowd, the party, the market, the reception: it shows who is standing with whom before you drop into close coverage. In crime it is the angle of surveillance and of the sniper, and in wedding and event films it reads the size of the hall and the movement of people.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Wedding / Event Film, Documentary Feature, Concert / Live Event, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Sports Broadcast / Sports Film, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography | genres: Crime / Procedural, Drama, Thriller / Suspense, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Romance, Political Thriller, Spy / Espionage  
**Avoid when:** In an intimate two hander it cools the scene and keeps the viewer outside. And if all your coverage comes from up there the story ends up with no faces.  
**Prompt:** `high angle from balcony height one floor above, looking down across the crowded hall at a moderate tilt`  
**Yields to:** `angle.chest_level` — An intimate two-hander, where height cools it.  
`angle.balcony_height`

### Drone Oblique High Angle

**Also called:** aerial oblique, 45 degree drone shot  
**What it is:** The drone sits high and tilts down between 30 and 60 degrees, so part of the horizon or the skyline stays in frame. The height gives geography while the oblique keeps depth.  
**Effect on the audience:** Scale and place at once. The viewer understands where we are and how things sit in relation to each other, and still feels ground and sky rather than a flat map.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The establishing shot in heritage documentary and travel work: the site, the river, the city at sunset. In real estate advertising it is the core shot because it shows the building and its surroundings together, and in sports it reads the stadium and the crowd in one frame.  
**Best in:** formats: Heritage / Historical Documentary, Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Commercial / TVC, Sports Broadcast / Sports Film, Feature Film, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Wedding / Event Film, Trailer / Teaser, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography | genres: Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Nature / Wildlife, Adventure, Drama, War, Science / Educational, Disaster  
**Avoid when:** Used every time you move to a new location it turns the film into a property listing. And in emotional scenes the height kills the closeness.  
**Prompt:** `aerial oblique high angle from a drone, tilted down about 45 degrees, horizon visible at the top, the site laid out below`  
**Yields to:** `angle.drone_nadir` — When the ground should read as pattern, not place.  
`angle.drone_oblique_high`

### Drone Nadir / Straight-Down Aerial

**Also called:** nadir shot, top-down drone  
**What it is:** The drone camera points straight down at a full 90 degrees. There is no horizon at all and the image becomes geometric abstraction: roads, fields, rooftops, shadows.  
**Effect on the audience:** The world turns into pattern. The viewer loses the sense of gravity and scale and starts reading the ground as a living map.  
**Used for and where it works best:** An opening or a visual break in documentary and travel work: fields, salt flats, the roofs of an old city, a single car in the desert. In heritage documentary it is the clearest way to reveal the plan of an archaeological site, and in vertical short-form a rotating or advancing nadir stops the scroll in a second.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Title Sequence / Opening Credits, Commercial / TVC, Music Video, Video Essay, AI-Generated Clip, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography | genres: Heritage (civilisation-focused), Nature / Wildlife, Science / Educational, Historical / Biopic, Drama, Disaster, Survival, War  
**Avoid when:** On people close to camera it leaves them faceless and unsympathetic. And if the ground has no pattern and no colour the shot is simply empty.  
**Prompt:** `drone nadir shot, camera pointing straight down 90 degrees, no horizon, the field patterns filling the frame`  
**Yields to:** `angle.drone_oblique_high` — When the ground has no pattern; keep horizon and depth.  
`angle.drone_nadir`

### Top-Down on Hands

**Also called:** overhead hands shot, flat lay angle, tabletop overhead  
**What it is:** The camera hangs vertically above a working surface, a kitchen counter, a craftsman's bench, a notebook, looking down on the hands and the tools. The hands are the only character in frame.  
**Effect on the audience:** Total focus on the action. With no face to distract, the viewer follows the process step by step and feels they are the one doing it.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Cooking, crafts and teaching: kneading, carving, sewing, drawing, assembling a product. Strong in short-form and on YouTube because it works with the sound off and across languages, and in heritage documentary it is the best angle for recording a handicraft before it disappears.  
**Best in:** formats: Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Explainer, Documentary Feature, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Commercial / TVC, Product Video / Motion Design, Corporate / Training, Kids Content, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Drama Series Episode, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography | genres: Science / Educational, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Family, Slice of Life, Verite / Observational, Nature / Wildlife, Comedy, Preschool / Early Learning  
**Avoid when:** If the hands leave the frame or a shadow falls across the work the whole point is lost. And do not use it for a beat that needs the reaction on the face.  
**Prompt:** `top-down overhead shot on the work surface, hands kneading dough in the centre of frame, no face visible, even soft light`  
**Yields to:** `angle.overhead_45` — When the beat needs faces as well as the surface.  
`angle.hands_top_down`

### Upside-Down / Inverted Angle

**Also called:** 180 degree flip, inverted frame  
**What it is:** The image is inverted by 180 degrees: the ceiling below and the floor above. Either the camera itself is flipped or the shot is turned in the edit.  
**Effect on the audience:** A complete inversion of the world. The brain tries to correct the image and fails, which reads as dream, death or rebirth.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The hanging or dragged character, the head upside down after a crash, and the crossing between two realities in sci-fi and surreal work. In music video and title sequences it works as direct poetic imagery, and in horror it leaves the viewer unsure who is standing and who is lying down.  
**Best in:** formats: Music Video, Title Sequence / Opening Credits, Feature Film, Short Film, Experimental / Art Film, Trailer / Teaser, Anime (Japanese 2D), Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), AI-Generated Clip, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography | genres: Surreal / Absurdist, Horror, Psychological / Arthouse, Sci-Fi, Supernatural Horror, Fantasy / Epic, Tragedy, Psychological Horror  
**Avoid when:** With no story reason it reads as an empty trick and pulls the viewer out. And on a shot carrying text or a logo it simply becomes unreadable.  
**Prompt:** `fully inverted angle, the image rotated 180 degrees, the ceiling at the bottom of frame and the floor above`  
**Yields to:** `angle.hard_dutch` — When text or a logo must stay readable.  
`angle.upside_down`

### Worm's Eye Through Grass or Feet

**Also called:** grass level shot, between the feet angle  
**What it is:** The camera lies flat on the ground with grass, gravel or nearby feet directly in front of the lens, giving a soft foreground layer with the subject behind it.  
**Effect on the audience:** The feeling of hiding and watching: we are low and looking out from between things. The foreground layer also delivers instant depth.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The ambush and the stakeout in war films, westerns and wildlife documentary, and the beat of a hiding child or animal. In sports advertising it is the shot from inside the grass onto the ball and the boot, and in vertical short-form it opens well because the depth reads even on a small screen.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Commercial / TVC, Sports Broadcast / Sports Film, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Short Film, Game Cinematic / Gameplay, AI-Generated Clip, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography | genres: War, Western, Nature / Wildlife, Thriller / Suspense, Survival, Horror, Adventure, Sports Drama  
**Avoid when:** If the foreground layer swallows the subject the shot becomes a puzzle instead of suspense. And it has no place in clean formal coverage.  
**Prompt:** `worm's eye view from ground level, blades of grass blurred across the foreground, the soldier crawling in the distance beyond`  
**Yields to:** `angle.ground_level` — When the foreground layer swallows the subject.  
`angle.worms_eye_grass`

### Frontal Azimuth / Full Front

**Also called:** zero degrees, straight on, full face  
**What it is:** The camera stands directly in front of the subject at zero degrees of azimuth, the full face toward the lens and the shoulders parallel to the frame.  
**Effect on the audience:** Confrontation. Either total honesty and exposure, or challenge, or fear, because the subject sits square and looks back at us.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The confession, the testimony, the accusation, and the face of a character deciding to face something. In documentary it is the angle of the witness telling the truth, and in horror a full frontal on a motionless face frightens more than any movement.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Documentary Feature, Interview / Video Podcast, Drama Series Episode, Short Film, Anime (Japanese 2D), Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Commercial / TVC, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography | genres: Drama, Horror, Thriller / Suspense, Verite / Observational, True Crime, Psychological / Arthouse, Tragedy, Historical / Biopic  
**Avoid when:** In ordinary dialogue a full frontal flattens the face and removes its volume. And if the character is hiding something, the frontal contradicts the writing.  
**Prompt:** `full frontal azimuth, camera directly in front of her, shoulders square to frame, eyes toward the lens`  
**Example:** The Silence of the Lambs (1991), Jonathan Demme, the frontal close-ups almost on the lens in the Lecter interviews.  
**Yields to:** `angle.azimuth_three_quarter_front` — Ordinary dialogue; frontal flattens the face.  
`angle.azimuth_frontal`

### Three-Quarter Front Azimuth

**Also called:** 45 degree azimuth, three-quarter view  
**What it is:** The camera sits at roughly 45 degrees off the subject's line of sight: we see a face and a half, a near cheek and a far cheek. This is the default position in narrative cinema.  
**Effect on the audience:** Natural and comfortable, and it gives the face volume because the nose and cheek are sculpted by the light. The subject is open to us without confronting us.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Most dialogue and most coverage, and the over-the-shoulder grows naturally out of it. Strong in drama, series and interviews, and in advertising it makes a face friendly and believable.  
**Best in:** formats: all | genres: all  
**Avoid when:** When you want a declared feeling, confrontation or refusal, the middle position delivers nothing. And if every shot sits at 45 degrees the scene never develops visually.  
**Prompt:** `three-quarter front azimuth, camera about 45 degrees off his eyeline, near cheek toward the lens`  
**Yields to:** `angle.azimuth_frontal` — When confrontation must be declared.  
`angle.azimuth_three_quarter_front`

### Profile Azimuth

**Also called:** 90 degrees, full profile, side view  
**What it is:** The camera sits at 90 degrees to the subject's eyeline: one clean half of the face with no second eye. The subject looks out of frame, never toward us.  
**Effect on the audience:** Closure and stubbornness. The subject gives us nothing, so the viewer becomes an outside observer instead of a partner.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The moment a character refuses, lies or decides without speaking, and the silent two hander in a car. In period work it is the angle of the royal portrait and of Assyrian relief, and in music video it sculpts a singer's face.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Music Video, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Short Film, Anime (Japanese 2D), Fashion Film, Commercial / TVC, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography | genres: Drama, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Crime / Procedural, Tragedy, Noir / Neo-Noir, Romance, Samurai / Chanbara  
**Avoid when:** In long dialogue the profile cuts the connection and tires the viewer. And when you need a clear reaction in both eyes the profile hides one of them.  
**Prompt:** `full profile azimuth, camera at 90 degrees to her eyeline, one side of the face clean against the background`  
**Yields to:** `angle.azimuth_three_quarter_front` — Long dialogue, or when both eyes must read.  
`angle.azimuth_profile`

### Three-Quarter Back Azimuth

**Also called:** 135 degrees, rear three-quarter  
**What it is:** The camera sits at about 135 degrees: shoulder, neck and the edge of a cheek, with most of the face hidden. The subject is halfway between open and closed.  
**Effect on the audience:** Mystery and concealed intent. The viewer strains to see the face and cannot, so their attention sharpens.  
**Used for and where it works best:** A character withdrawing in the middle of a scene, the traitor before the reveal, somebody entering a place they are unsure of. In heritage and period work it is the first solution for hiding a face without losing the emotion, and in a trailer it makes the viewer wait for the reveal.  
**Best in:** formats: Heritage / Historical Documentary, Trailer / Teaser, Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Music Video, Anime (Japanese 2D), Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), AI-Generated Clip, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography | genres: Mystery / Whodunit, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Thriller / Suspense, Drama, Noir / Neo-Noir, Fantasy / Epic, Crime / Procedural  
**Avoid when:** In a scene that needs declared emotion the performance is simply lost. And overused, every character turns mysterious and the mystery stops meaning anything.  
**Prompt:** `three-quarter back azimuth, camera behind her right shoulder at about 135 degrees, only the edge of her cheek visible`  
**Yields to:** `angle.azimuth_three_quarter_front` — When the performance must read.  
`angle.azimuth_three_quarter_back`

### Full Back Azimuth

**Also called:** 180 degrees, back to camera, back shot  
**What it is:** The camera sits directly behind the subject: back, head and shoulders, with no face at all. They stand or sit looking away from us.  
**Effect on the audience:** Refusal, grief and solitude. The viewer projects their own feeling onto the back, because there is no face there to tell them what to feel.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The strongest closing shot in cinema: the character facing the sea, the city or the grave while we stay behind them. In heritage work it is the main face hiding angle, and as a final shot in a music video or a short-form piece it leaves an open feeling with no answer.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Short Film, Music Video, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Trailer / Teaser, Anime (Japanese 2D), Title Sequence / Opening Credits, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography | genres: Drama, Tragedy, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Romance, War, Psychological / Arthouse, Melodrama  
**Avoid when:** Dropped into the middle of a scene with no reason it kills the connection. And if you close every video with it, it becomes a repeated signature and the effect dies.  
**Prompt:** `full back azimuth, camera directly behind him, back of the head and shoulders filling frame, face not visible, city lights ahead`  
**Yields to:** `angle.azimuth_three_quarter_back` — Mid-scene: hide the face, keep the emotion.  
`angle.azimuth_full_back`

### Progressive Azimuth Shift

**Also called:** azimuth journey, rotating the coverage through a scene  
**What it is:** Moving the camera's position around the subject in steps across a scene: start three-quarter front, close down to profile when they resist, come round to frontal when they admit it. The azimuth becomes a dramatic line.  
**Effect on the audience:** The viewer feels the character turn before hearing it in the dialogue. Opening up and shutting down are said by position rather than by words.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The interrogation, the confession, the reconciliation, the break up: plan three azimuth positions matching three stages of the scene. Strong in drama, crime and legal series, and in documentary you can do the same across two interviews with the same person.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Limited Series, Short Film, Documentary Feature, Interview / Video Podcast, Anime (Japanese 2D), Vertical Micro-Drama | genres: Drama, Crime / Procedural, Legal Drama, Romance, Thriller / Suspense, Tragedy, Political Thriller, Melodrama  
**Avoid when:** Done in only two shots it does not read, and done in every scene it turns mechanical. And you must hold the 180 degree line while you move around, or the geography breaks.  
**Prompt:** `coverage that moves around her through the scene, starting three-quarter front, settling to full profile, ending frontal as she answers`  
**Yields to:** `angle.azimuth_three_quarter_front` — Too few setups for the rotation to read.  
`angle.azimuth_shift`

### The 180-Degree Rule

**Also called:** the axis of action, the line, screen direction rule  
**What it is:** Draw an imaginary line between the two characters, or along the direction of movement, and keep every camera position on one side of it. Each person then stays on the same side of the frame in every shot.  
**Effect on the audience:** The viewer never has to think about the space and is free for the story. Complete directional safety: who is where, and who is looking at whom.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Any two hander, any chase, any match: decide the line before the first setup and mark it on the floor plan. It matters in every genre, and it is critical in series, sports and action, where a directional error throws the viewer out of the scene at once.  
**Best in:** formats: all | genres: all  
**Avoid when:** It is a convention, not a law: break it when you want deliberate chaos. And with three or more people the line moves depending on who is speaking, so do not cling to one dead axis.  
**Prompt:** `keep the camera on one side of the line between them, she stays frame left facing right and he stays frame right facing left in every shot`  
**Not a choice:** this is **geometry every answer obeys**, not an entry to choose between. It sits on the `angle.` shelf and is not of it, in the same relation `light.key` and `light.ratio` have to the lighting shelf - every shot has one. A selector that offered this as an option would be offering a choice about whether to be comprehensible.  
**Yields to:** `angle.crossing_the_line` — When deliberate chaos is wanted.  
`angle.rule_180`

### Crossing the Line Deliberately

**Also called:** breaking the axis, jumping the line  
**What it is:** You move the camera to the far side of the 180 degree line on purpose, so the characters swap sides of the frame and their eyelines reverse.  
**Effect on the audience:** A sudden flip in the viewer's footing: something in the relationship or in the world has gone wrong. The body registers the error before the mind explains it.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The beat of betrayal, of a psychological break, of an explosion, or of power flipping between two people. In horror and thriller use it once in a film on the single most important moment, and in drama put it on the line that breaks the relationship.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Short Film, Anime (Japanese 2D), Experimental / Art Film, Trailer / Teaser, Music Video | genres: Thriller / Suspense, Psychological Horror, Drama, Horror, War, Crime / Procedural, Surreal / Absurdist, Tragedy  
**Avoid when:** Cross with no reason and the viewer reads a technical mistake rather than a meaning. And do not cross inside a complex action scene; the audience loses the thread entirely.  
**Prompt:** `cut to the reverse side of the line so they swap sides of frame, her eyeline flipping direction at the moment he lies`  
**Example:** Tokyo Story (1953), Yasujiro Ozu, whose shot and reverse pairs cross the axis as a matter of style.  
**Yields to:** `angle.neutral_axis` — Inside complex action; cross on the axis instead.  
`angle.crossing_the_line`

### The Neutral Shot on the Axis

**Also called:** on-the-line shot, neutral shot, buffer shot  
**What it is:** A shot taken from the line itself, so the subject faces almost straight into the lens and there is no left or right side. It is the shot you place in between to cross to the other side safely.  
**Effect on the audience:** A directional reset. The viewer loses the side for one beat and accepts whatever direction comes next without noticing.  
**Used for and where it works best:** When you have to cross the line inside a dialogue or a chase: put an on-the-line shot in between and move across after it. Useful in series and documentary when the location does not allow full coverage from one side, and in sports between two cameras on opposite sides.  
**Best in:** formats: Drama Series Episode, Feature Film, Documentary Feature, Sports Broadcast / Sports Film, Documentary Series, Short Film, News Report / Reportage, Interview / Video Podcast | genres: Drama, Crime / Procedural, Action, Sports Drama, Verite / Observational, Thriller / Suspense, Comedy, Mystery / Whodunit  
**Avoid when:** Held too long it turns into a confrontation shot and draws attention you did not intend. And do not use it as a permanent patch instead of planning the axis properly.  
**Prompt:** `neutral shot taken from the axis itself, the subject facing almost straight into the lens, no left or right bias`  
**Yields to:** `angle.rule_180` — When the axis should be planned, not patched.  
`angle.neutral_axis`

### The 30-Degree Rule

**Also called:** thirty degree rule, angle change rule  
**What it is:** Between any two consecutive shots of the same subject the camera must move at least 30 degrees around it, or the size must change by two steps. Less than that and the cut reads as a jump.  
**Effect on the audience:** The cut becomes invisible and the viewer stays inside the scene. A jump instead reminds them that there is a camera.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Planning the coverage of any dialogue scene or interview: every new setup sits at least 30 degrees from the last. It matters in series, documentary and vlogs shot on two cameras, and in the edit it explains why a particular cut hurts when nobody can say why.  
**Best in:** formats: all | genres: all  
**Avoid when:** Break it when the jump itself is the tool: tension, compressed time, a comic style. And in documentary interviews some editors use the jump on purpose as a mark of honesty.  
**Prompt:** `second angle on the same subject moved at least 30 degrees around him, size stepped from medium to close, so the cut reads smooth`  
**Not a choice:** this is **geometry every answer obeys**, not an entry to choose between. It sits on the `angle.` shelf and is not of it, in the same relation `light.key` and `light.ratio` have to the lighting shelf - every shot has one. A selector that offered this as an option would be offering a choice about whether to be comprehensible.  
**Yields to:** `cut.jump` — When the jump itself is the tool; and `size.ladder` — the camera cannot travel thirty degrees; jump the size at least two rungs on the same axis instead.  
`angle.rule_30`

### Eyeline Match and Eyeline Height

**Also called:** eyeline, matching eyelines  
**What it is:** The direction and the height of a character's gaze must match the real position of what they are looking at. If they are looking at a child their coverage has to be set so the gaze reads as going down, and the reverse when they look up at something.  
**Effect on the audience:** It builds the space in the viewer's head with no establishing shot. When it breaks, the viewer feels the two people are not in the same room even if they cannot say why.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Every scene where a person looks at a person or a thing: adult and child, standing and seated, guard and prisoner. It is critical in anime and 3D where the space is built from nothing, and in AI video where each shot is generated in isolation, so gaze direction and height have to be written into the prompt.  
**Best in:** formats: all | genres: all  
**Avoid when:** Do not let both characters look the same way at the same height; it reads as if they are watching a third thing. And if you break the match on purpose it has to mean something: a dream, a lie, a disconnection.  
**Prompt:** `his eyeline angled down to frame left and hers angled up to frame right, matched so they clearly look at each other`  
**Not a choice:** this is **geometry every answer obeys**, not an entry to choose between. It sits on the `angle.` shelf and is not of it, in the same relation `light.key` and `light.ratio` have to the lighting shelf - every shot has one. A selector that offered this as an option would be offering a choice about whether to be comprehensible.  
**Yields to:** `angle.crossing_the_line` — When the broken eyeline must mean a lie.  
`angle.eyeline_match`

### Planimetric Frontal Angle

**Also called:** flat frontal, perpendicular staging  
**What it is:** The camera is exactly perpendicular to the wall, the row or the face, zero tilt and zero azimuth, and every line in the scene runs either parallel to the frame or straight into it. A style associated with Wes Anderson.  
**Effect on the audience:** Order imposed on the world. The scene reads like an illustration or a puppet stage, and that order is exactly what makes any disturbance inside it funny or painful.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Dry comedy, fairy tale worlds and designed universes, plus introductions and comparisons, person against person or product against product. Strong in advertising and motion work because it is clean and symmetrical, and in documentary for presenting an object in a catalogue manner: a tool, a garment, a manuscript.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Commercial / TVC, Product Video / Motion Design, Title Sequence / Opening Credits, Short Film, Drama Series Episode, Explainer, Kids Content, 2D Animation (Western / Cartoon), Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography | genres: Comedy, Dark Comedy / Satire, Fairy Tale, Family, Drama, Surreal / Absurdist, Science / Educational, Heritage (civilisation-focused)  
**Avoid when:** In genuinely emotional scenes it reads cold, because the viewer sees the design before the person. And everything in frame has to be exact; one crooked line ruins the idea.  
**Prompt:** `perfectly frontal planimetric angle, camera square to the wall, symmetrical composition, subject centred facing the lens`  
**Example:** The Grand Budapest Hotel (2014), Wes Anderson, the flat frontal symmetrical staging.  
**Yields to:** `angle.azimuth_three_quarter_front` — Genuinely emotional scenes, where design reads cold.  
`angle.planimetric_frontal`

### Raking Angle

**Also called:** raking shot, shooting down the line  
**What it is:** The camera is set at an acute angle to a row of objects or people, or along a long surface, so it looks down the line rather than at it. The near end reads large and the far end falls away fast.  
**Effect on the audience:** Depth and repetition. The eye travels the row from the near end to the far one and feels the count and the length of the space without anyone counting.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The row of columns in a temple, a line of soldiers, a long dinner table, warehouse shelving, a line up. Strong in heritage work to show the extent of architecture, in advertising across a table of product, and in crime along a row of weapons or files.  
**Best in:** formats: Heritage / Historical Documentary, Feature Film, Commercial / TVC, Product Video / Motion Design, Documentary Feature, Drama Series Episode, Game Cinematic / Gameplay, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography | genres: Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, War, Crime / Procedural, Drama, Mystery / Whodunit, Science / Educational, Political Thriller  
**Avoid when:** If the elements are not alike there is no pattern and the point is lost. And faces very close to a wide lens distort badly.  
**Prompt:** `raking angle along the row of columns, camera set oblique to the line, near column large and the row receding into depth`  
**Yields to:** `angle.planimetric_frontal` — When the row should read square, not receding.  
`angle.raking`

### Angle Change as a Power Shift

**Also called:** angle reversal within a scene, flipping the power  
**What it is:** Inside one scene you start high on one character and low on the other, and at the turning point of the dialogue you swap them. The angle becomes the meter of who owns the room.  
**Effect on the audience:** The viewer feels the power move physically before understanding it in the words. The turn reads even when the dialogue stays quiet.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The interrogation, the job interview, the family confrontation, the blackmail scene. Strong in drama, crime and legal storytelling, and in series it is the cheapest and strongest thing you can do to a well written scene.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Limited Series, Short Film, Anime (Japanese 2D), Vertical Micro-Drama, Soap Opera / Telenovela, Trailer / Teaser | genres: Drama, Crime / Procedural, Legal Drama, Thriller / Suspense, Political Thriller, Tragedy, Gangster, Melodrama  
**Avoid when:** Reverse the angles with nothing changing in the writing and the shift becomes a visual lie. And do not do it in a single quick shot; give the viewer two shots for the new state to settle.  
**Prompt:** `coverage that starts high on her and low on him, then reverses after she stands, low on her and high on him for the rest of the scene`  
**Yields to:** `angle.ots_low` — When power is fixed and does not turn.  
`angle.power_shift`

### The Reverse Angle

**Also called:** reverse shot, counter shot  
**What it is:** A shot taken from roughly the opposite direction to the previous one, while staying on the same side of the line, to show the other party or what the character is looking at. It is the second half of the shot and answer pair.  
**Effect on the audience:** It completes the space and lets the dialogue breathe. The viewer receives the reaction, and without it the story stays one sided.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Every scene of dialogue or of looking: shot and answer, question and reply, shooter and target. The key is that the reverse should be matched, the same height and roughly the same size when the two are equals, and deliberately unmatched when they are not.  
**Best in:** formats: all | genres: all  
**Avoid when:** Change the height or the size between a shot and its reverse without a reason and the viewer senses something off in the relationship. And do not cut to the reverse before the first shot has finished doing its work.  
**Prompt:** `reverse angle from the opposite side of the conversation, same height and size as the previous shot, his reaction as she finishes speaking`  
**Yields to:** `angle.ots_low` — When the two are not equals; unmatched reverses.  
`angle.reverse`

### The Over-the-Top Reverse for a Fight

**Also called:** over-the-head reverse, fight reverse from above  
**What it is:** In fight coverage, instead of a standard over-the-shoulder, the camera sits above the near fighter's head and looks over him onto the opponent, then reverses the same way for the other side.  
**Effect on the audience:** It shows the distance between the two and the line of the strike clearly, while keeping the near body in frame as a physical threat.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Boxing, wrestling and sword coverage: this angle lets the viewer read who has the upper hand and where the strike will land. Strong in action, martial arts and battle anime, and in sports broadcast it is the referee's angle.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Sports Broadcast / Sports Film, Anime (Japanese 2D), Game Cinematic / Gameplay, Trailer / Teaser, Music Video, Short Film | genres: Action, Martial Arts / Wuxia, Samurai / Chanbara, Sports Drama, Shonen Battle (anime), War, Adventure, Sports Anime  
**Avoid when:** Climb too high and you lose the sense of threat and end up with a map. And you must hold the 180 degree line between the fighters or the direction of the blows flips.  
**Prompt:** `reverse angle from just above the near fighter's head looking over him at his opponent, both bodies in frame, the gap between them clear`  
**Yields to:** `frame.ots` — When the fight should press from beside, not above.  
`angle.over_the_top_reverse`

### The 45-Degree Interview Angle

**Also called:** off-axis interview, interviewer beside the lens  
**What it is:** The subject looks at the interviewer seated just beside the lens at a small offset, 10 to 45 degrees off axis, so the gaze lands near the camera but not in it. The camera sits exactly at the subject's eye height.  
**Effect on the audience:** Honesty without confrontation. The viewer feels they are listening in on a conversation between two people rather than being addressed.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The documentary interview, the video podcast, the expert testimony in a corporate piece. Move the camera height by one notch and the power reading changes, and if you shoot on two cameras set one at 15 degrees and the other at 45 so the cut between them respects the 30 degree rule.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Interview / Video Podcast, Corporate / Training, News Report / Reportage, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Video Essay, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography, Sports Photography | genres: Verite / Observational, True Crime, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Science / Educational, Political Thriller, Drama, Medical Drama  
**Avoid when:** Push the offset past 45 degrees and the gaze turns to profile and the viewer feels the disconnection. And a camera below the subject's eye height hands them an authority the testimony has not earned.  
**Prompt:** `documentary interview angle, camera at the subject's eye height, her gaze just off the lens to camera left, soft background behind`  
**Yields to:** `angle.down_the_barrel` — When the testimony must confront the viewer.  
`angle.interview_45`

### Up-the-Stairs and Down-the-Stairs Angles

**Also called:** stairway angles, vertical staging  
**What it is:** Shooting the staircase from below toward whoever is above, the climb, or from above toward whoever is descending, the fall. The stairs themselves become the meter of class and of fate in the scene.  
**Effect on the audience:** Vertical movement becomes meaning: up is power, money and escape, down is poverty, collapse and a closed fate.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Class stories, family stories and crime: keep the camera at the end of the stairs that represents the character's fate. In period work it is the angle of the palace or the temple staircase, and in horror a descent to a basement lets the angle alone build the fear.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Limited Series, Short Film, Anime (Japanese 2D), Heritage / Historical Documentary, Trailer / Teaser, Game Cinematic / Gameplay | genres: Drama, Thriller / Suspense, Horror, Crime / Procedural, Historical / Biopic, Tragedy, Psychological / Arthouse, Gothic / Vampire  
**Avoid when:** Use a staircase with no vertical idea in the story and it is only set dressing. And do not mix the two directions inside one scene without a reason; the class reading disappears.  
**Prompt:** `low angle from the foot of the staircase looking up as he climbs, then a high angle from the top as she descends into the dark`  
**Example:** Parasite (2019), Bong Joon-ho, the long descent through the stairways down to the semi-basement.  
`angle.stairs_up_down`

### The POV Height Rule

**Also called:** eye height of the viewpoint, subjective height  
**What it is:** If a shot represents a creature's point of view, the camera height must equal that creature's eye height exactly: a child at about a metre, a dog at forty centimetres, a giant at three metres. Height, not movement, is what tells the viewer whose eyes these are.  
**Effect on the audience:** The viewer believes they have entered another body. Any error in the height breaks the illusion even when everything else is right.  
**Used for and where it works best:** A child's viewpoint in family films, an animal's in wildlife documentary and kids content, a monster's or a giant's in fantasy and horror. In AI video state the height explicitly in the prompt, because it is the first thing that drifts, and in games it is the difference between a character camera and an observer camera.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Kids Content, 2D Animation (Western / Cartoon), 3D CG Animation, Game Cinematic / Gameplay, Documentary Feature, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Anime (Japanese 2D), 360 / VR, AI-Generated Clip, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography | genres: Family, Preschool / Early Learning, Fantasy / Epic, Horror, Nature / Wildlife, Monster / Kaiju, Adventure, Coming of Age  
**Avoid when:** Do not shoot a child's viewpoint from adult height and expect the feeling to land. And if a human point of view glides too smoothly the honesty of it disappears.  
**Prompt:** `point of view shot at a small dog's eye height, forty centimetres off the floor, adults towering above and cut off by the frame`  
**Yields to:** `angle.low_height_level` — When the child's world is observed, not inhabited.  
`angle.pov_height`

### Looking Down the Barrel

**Also called:** direct address, straight to lens, breaking the fourth wall look  
**What it is:** The subject looks straight into the lens, zero azimuth and zero tilt, and the gaze reaches the viewer rather than another character in the scene.  
**Effect on the audience:** It breaks the fourth wall. Either the viewer becomes an accomplice, or a target, and in horror the one being hunted.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The narrator addressing camera in comedy and satire, the testimony in documentary when you want maximum confrontation, and every commercial where a person speaks to the buyer. In vertical short-form it is the default, because the viewer is holding the phone, and in horror a single look into the lens can carry a whole scene.  
**Best in:** formats: Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Commercial / TVC, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Interview / Video Podcast, Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Sitcom / Multi-Cam Comedy, Explainer, Corporate / Training, Mockumentary, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography | genres: Comedy, Dark Comedy / Satire, Drama, Horror, Verite / Observational, Crime / Procedural, Family, Science / Educational  
**Avoid when:** In naturalistic drama it destroys the illusion at once unless the whole film is built on it. And with a performer who cannot hold the lens it reads false and uncomfortable.  
**Prompt:** `subject looking straight down the barrel of the lens, dead centre frame, direct address to the viewer`  
**Example:** Fleabag (2016), the lead turning to speak straight into the lens.  
**Yields to:** `angle.interview_45` — Naturalistic work, or a performer who cannot hold the lens.  
`angle.down_the_barrel`

### The 45-Degree High Oblique

**Also called:** high three-quarter down, overhead oblique  
**What it is:** The camera sits high and tilts down about 45 degrees, halfway between an ordinary high angle and a bird's eye. Depth survives and the face survives, yet the space still reads like a plan.  
**Effect on the audience:** Comprehension and control. The viewer sees how things are arranged and how they relate, without being cut off from the character entirely.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The table, the meeting, the plan, the map: who is sitting where and who faces whom. Strong in advertising and food work because the product keeps its depth instead of going flat, and in crime on a scene when you want the relationships between objects in a single frame.  
**Best in:** formats: Commercial / TVC, Product Video / Motion Design, Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Documentary Feature, Explainer, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Game Cinematic / Gameplay, Corporate / Training, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography | genres: Crime / Procedural, Drama, Heist, Science / Educational, Comedy, Thriller / Suspense, Mystery / Whodunit, Political Thriller  
**Avoid when:** On a tight face it enlarges the forehead and shrinks the chin. And when a scene needs full sympathy the height cools it.  
**Prompt:** `high oblique angle about 45 degrees down on the table, the whole layout visible with depth, faces still readable`  
**Yields to:** `angle.eye_level` — A tight face, or a scene needing full sympathy.  
`angle.overhead_45`

### Cheating the Angle

**Also called:** cheating, cheat the eyeline  
**What it is:** Moving the actor or the camera in one setup to a position that does not match the real geography, turning a shoulder, closing the gap by a step, adjusting the gaze height, so the frame reads better. The viewer never notices because they do not see both setups at once.  
**Effect on the audience:** The frame gets cleaner and the faces clearer with no loss of spatial belief. The shot serves the scene instead of serving the real distances.  
**Used for and where it works best:** When the dialogue is between a tall person and a short one, or standing and seated, or when the two are too far apart for a tight two shot: turn the actor slightly toward the lens or bring them a step closer. It matters in series and advertising, where time is short and the frame has to work first time.  
**Best in:** formats: Drama Series Episode, Feature Film, Commercial / TVC, Sitcom / Multi-Cam Comedy, Soap Opera / Telenovela, Short Film, Vertical Micro-Drama, Corporate / Training | genres: Drama, Comedy, Romance, Crime / Procedural, Melodrama, Legal Drama, Family, Medical Drama  
**Avoid when:** Cheat too far and the cut exposes it; the viewer feels the room changing. And in documentary and reportage it is out of bounds, because the honesty is the whole point.  
**Prompt:** `cheat her a quarter turn toward the lens so both faces are open to camera while they still read as facing each other`  
**Yields to:** `angle.over_the_hip` — Tall against short where cheating is out of bounds.  
`angle.cheat_the_angle`

### The Over-the-Hip Shot

**Also called:** over the waist shot, hip level over shoulder  
**What it is:** A relational shot taken from behind a person and over their waist or hip instead of their shoulder, so part of their body sits in the lower foreground while the other person stays in the middle of the frame. The camera sits far lower than an over-the-shoulder.  
**Effect on the audience:** The relationship becomes physical rather than conversational. The viewer sees the far person from a low position so they grow slightly, and whatever sits in the foreground reads as a threat or as protection.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The standoff before the draw in a western with the holstered weapon in the foreground, the stance before a strike in martial arts, and the fashion frame that wants a body line in front. It also serves a scene where a child faces an adult, shot over the adult's waist.  
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Drama Series Episode, Anime (Japanese 2D), 3D CG Animation, Game Cinematic / Gameplay, Music Video, Fashion Film, Trailer / Teaser, AI-Generated Clip, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography | genres: Western, Martial Arts / Wuxia, Action, Samurai / Chanbara, Crime / Procedural, Thriller / Suspense, Family Drama, Gangster  
**Avoid when:** The lower frame is usually filled by a large unreadable mass that clutters the composition. The exception is when the foreground body is already identified, a hand on a grip or a belt whose owner the viewer knows.  
**Prompt:** `over-the-hip shot, camera behind the standing gunman at waist height, his holster filling the lower left foreground, the opponent small and sharp in the centre of the street`  
**Yields to:** `frame.ots` — When the foreground body is unidentified and clutters.  
`angle.over_the_hip`

### The Aerial View

**Also called:** aerial category, from the air, airborne viewpoint  
**What it is:** Everything filmed from the air above building height, whatever the platform: drone, helicopter, aircraft or cable rig. It is the umbrella over the specific aerial angles that already exist in this category, the oblique aerial and the straight down aerial, which are the two that carry the actual visual decision.  
**Effect on the audience:** Height turns a person into part of a larger pattern. The higher the camera goes, the less individual empathy remains and the stronger the sense of system or fate becomes.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Opening a chapter or an episode, presenting a city, a site or a battle, and the ending that leaves the protagonist small inside their place. Use this umbrella when planning only, then pick the specific angle: oblique to present a place with depth, straight down to turn the ground into an abstract design.  
**Best in:** formats: Heritage / Historical Documentary, Documentary Feature, Feature Film, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Trailer / Teaser, Sports Broadcast / Sports Film, Documentary Series, Drama Series Episode, AI-Generated Clip, Generated Keyframe Feeding Video, Poster / Static Design, Portrait Photography, Architectural Photography, Street Photography, Still Life Photography | genres: Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, War, Nature / Wildlife, Adventure, Drama, Disaster, Science / Educational  
**Avoid when:** Every scene usually ends up opening from the air, height loses its value and becomes decoration. The exception is when the air is the subject, a film about flight or the aerial survey of an archaeological site.  
**Prompt:** `aerial view above rooftop height over the ancient mound, choose oblique for depth or straight down for pattern, slow steady forward travel, early morning light`  
**Not a choice:** this is a planning umbrella, not an executable angle. At execution select `angle.drone_oblique_high` for depth or `angle.drone_nadir` for pattern.  
**Yields to:** `angle.drone_oblique_high` — At execution it always defers: oblique for place, nadir for pattern.  
`angle.aerial`
