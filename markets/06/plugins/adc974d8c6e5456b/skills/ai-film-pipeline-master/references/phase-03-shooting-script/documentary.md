# Documentary, Interview and Reportage

123 cards. These decide a shot: how it moves, how it cuts, and how it sits in time.

Every card carries what it is, what it does to the audience, where it works best, what to avoid, and the prompt written out in full.

---

### Name the Claim Before the Shot

**Also called:** the documentary selector, claim before shot, what is this shot asserting
**What it is:** The card at the head of the `doc.` shelf. It does not describe a shot. It asks the three questions this shelf's own pair graph asks over and over — **who is making this picture, what is it claiming, and what has been refused** — and hands back a named shortlist for each answer. The axes were not invented: they were read off the hundred and twenty-nine `Yields to:` conditions the cards carry and off the boundaries in their `Avoid when:` fields, and the conditions that repeated became the questions. Every card cited here is cited by id.
**Effect on the audience:** None. This card never reaches the screen. It exists so that the card which does reach the screen was chosen against the source, the claim and the refusal, rather than picked because the shelf's interview block is the part everybody remembers.
**Used for and where it works best:** Answer the three questions **in order and out loud, in one sentence each, before you open the shelf.** The first answer leaves between a sixth and two fifths of the file; the second halves that again; the third normally leaves a shortlist of two to six. If you cannot answer question two — *what is this shot claiming* — you are not ready to shoot, you are ready to research, because on this shelf the claim decides the shot and not the other way round. Where two answers fight for one frame, that is two shots and one of them is a declared one.
**Best in:** formats: all | genres: all
**Avoid when:** **The piece only wears documentary grammar and owes nobody correspondence** — a brand film, a promo, a drama shooting a fake interview. There question two has the same answer for every card on the shelf, the map loses a third of its work, and the cards it hands back are cards whose entire value is a claim the piece is not making. Also **distrust axis three whenever your honest answer is its first value:** more than three cards in every five sit there, so on a piece with no permit, no refusal and no live clock this map has only two questions left. And it **cannot separate two cards sharing one cell** — `doc.ken_burns` from `doc.archive_format_frame`, `doc.b_camera_45` from `doc.b_camera_profile`. That is a leaf, settled by resolution or by the beat, and the cards settle it between themselves in their own `Yields to:` fields.
**Prompt:** `[Selector — produces no prompt. It returns card ids. The chosen card carries the prompt.]`

**The axes**

**Axis 1 — Who is making this picture: somebody you placed, the event, a record, or the production?**
Asked first because it is the split the shelf repeats most and the one that eliminates hardest. The conditions state it plainly: *"When nobody is in frame to point"* (`doc.point_at_the_thing`), *"When the style hides the interviewer, cut to a room detail"* (`doc.noddy`), *"When the surface, not the identity, carries the line"* (`doc.object_insert`), *"When the original document, not the type, should carry it"* (`doc.kinetic_quote`), *"When a hand on a real map carries more than a graphic"* (`doc.map_animation`), *"When a physical model can carry the rebuild honestly"* (`doc.reconstruction_overlay`), *"When the source must be seen being opened"* (`doc.the_receipt`), *"When the subject is alive and unfolding now"* (`doc.expository_mode`). Every one of those moves the picture from one maker to another, and nothing else about the shot changes.

**Axis 2 — What is this shot claiming, and who has to be able to check it?**
This is the shelf's truth contract and it is an axis, not a preference. **It binds only where the piece presents itself as fact** — a documentary, a report, a history piece the user wants realistic — and even there it is never a stop: a claim comes from the brief, then the skill, then a search, then the closest reasonable choice, recorded under `decisions`. On a stylised or fictional piece the brief decides and nothing here is checked. Thirty-odd `Avoid when:` fields draw the same boundary — *"passing off a shot from another location as the one being described"* (`doc.broll_illustrative`), *"standing in for a fact you failed to get"* (`doc.broll_atmospheric`), *"Do not present a reconstruction without saying it is a hypothesis"* (`doc.reconstruction_overlay`), *"Do not reconstruct anything you have no evidence for"* (`doc.dramatised_reconstruction`), *"a reaction recorded at a different moment is cheating"* (`doc.crowd_reaction_cut`), *"Do not intervene and then claim you observed"* (`doc.observational_follow`), *"on a piece presented as fact, take each line from the brief, the skill or a search"* (`doc.where_are_they_now`), *"a figure nobody can check is the first thing a critic pulls"* (`doc.stats_card`) — and the pair graph forks on it directly: *"When you need evidence, not a mood"* (`doc.vox_pop`), *"If the call is restaged, mark it as reconstruction"* (`doc.phone_call_visual`), *"When no portrait exists and a face would be a claim"* (`doc.dramatised_reconstruction`), *"When enlarging would expose the pixels; label it"* (`doc.zoom_circle_detail`), *"When the line is somebody's words and needs attribution"* (`doc.text_overlay_thesis`).

**Axis 3 — What has been refused: the place, a person, or the clock?**
Asked last because it rules out rather than in, and because it is the only axis that reaches the live block at all. The conditions are refusals stated as facts: *"When driving is unsafe, unlawful or distracting"* (`doc.car_interview`), *"When an aerial does not suit the piece, sell scale from the ground"* (`doc.stadium_drone_reveal`), *"When a flight over the crowd does not suit the piece"* (`doc.drone_over_crowd`), *"When the site wants dust and daylight rather than flame"* (`doc.night_site_torchlight`), *"When the object can be filmed out of the case"* (`doc.museum_glass_control`), *"Where there is no threshold, the orbit carries it"* (`doc.reveal_walk_into_site`), *"When the site is not theirs"* (`doc.expert_on_location`), *"In places that demand quiet or dignity, drop the face"* (`doc.selfie_walk`), *"When time is short and it will not repeat"* (`doc.process_craft_shot`), *"When the moment moves and will not come to you"* (`doc.waiting_for_the_moment`), *"When nothing new is happening, record the bridge instead"* (`doc.live_shot`).

**The default, and the one against it**

**The default is `doc.environmental_portrait` for the person and `doc.two_camera_interview` for the coverage — and it is measured by distinct conditions, not by inbound edges.** `doc.environmental_portrait` is reached four times and every one of the four is a different failure: `doc.interview_in_darkness` (*"When the contributor's place is part of the story"*), `doc.working_hands_interview` (*"When there is no task they genuinely perform"*), `doc.expert_on_location` (*"When the site is not theirs"*) and `doc.walking_interview` (*"When the walk crosses a space that adds nothing"*). Four specialisations, four separate ways of failing, one card catching all of them. `doc.two_camera_interview` is reached three times for three distinct reasons — a visible push undercutting the testimony, one contributor not wanting three positions, and a classical film that cannot carry a visible jump.

**Three cards on this shelf are reached more often and none of them is a default.** `doc.landscape_breather` takes four inbound edges from `doc.reaction_hold`, `doc.let_it_breathe`, `doc.nat_sound_pop` and `doc.chapter_card` — and all four say the same thing, *the film needs a rest*. `doc.expository_mode` takes four, from `doc.direct_cinema`, `doc.reflexive_mode`, `doc.observational_mode` and `doc.poetic_mode`, and all four say *the film has information the pictures cannot deliver*. `doc.reveal_walk_into_site` takes three and all three say *there is a threshold*. **One condition arriving many times is a door out of the shelf; many conditions arriving at one card is a fallback.**

**The one against it is `doc.empty_chair`.** Nothing routes to it, it is named by no `Yields to:` on this shelf, and it is the only card here where the production controls every element of the frame and still claims nothing except a refusal. Every other card under axis three's *a person* value still gets the person and then withholds part of them — `doc.interview_in_darkness` keeps the face and throws the room away, `doc.anonymous_interview` keeps the outline and has now withdrawn its own advice about hiding it. `doc.empty_chair` never has the person at all, and so it is the one shot on the shelf that can put the film's own access on screen. Its stated cost is precise and payable: *do not use it when there is no real absence, and do not let it imply guilt when the reason is innocent or human.* Its near cousin against the grain is `doc.reflective_silence`, which likewise nothing reaches and which is the only other card whose content is something the production was not given.

### Talking Head

**Also called:** sit-down interview, sync interview, sound bite shot  
**What it is:** A locked-off medium shot of a seated contributor speaking to an interviewer who stays out of frame. It is the base unit of any documentary built on testimony.  
**Effect on the audience:** It confers authority and credibility. A still face with no camera movement tells the viewer this testimony is reliable and worth going quiet for.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The backbone of documentary, reportage and the video podcast: testimony, memory, explanation. In heritage documentary it is the archaeologist or historian explaining what the site footage is showing. Keep it locked off and let all the life happen in the B-roll laid over the voice.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Interview / Video Podcast, Corporate / Training, Video Essay, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form | genres: Verite / Observational, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), True Crime, Science / Educational, Political Thriller, Nature / Wildlife, Drama  
**Avoid when:** Do not let it run long with no cutaway or B-roll; a single face loses the room after about thirty seconds. And do not use it for anything you could simply show instead of having someone say it.  
**Prompt:** `static medium interview shot, subject seated slightly off centre looking just past the lens, soft key from one side, background falling off into shadow`  
**Yields to:** `doc.float_the_sync` — Past thirty seconds, run the voice over B-roll.

### Interview Framing on the Thirds / Looking Room

**Also called:** off-axis eyeline, looking room, nose room  
**What it is:** The contributor sits on a third of the frame with the empty space in front of the eyeline, looking at the interviewer seated beside the lens rather than into it.  
**Effect on the audience:** The space in front of the eyeline makes the talk read as a conversation with a real person rather than an address to the audience. The viewer becomes a listener, not the one being spoken to.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The default for any documentary or news interview. The empty side is where the name super and the lower third live, and where an archive card or split screen can sit. In heritage documentary let that empty side open onto the relief or the artefact shelf, so the background carries meaning too.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Interview / Video Podcast, Corporate / Training, Video Essay, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form | genres: Verite / Observational, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), True Crime, Science / Educational, Political Thriller, Nature / Wildlife  
**Avoid when:** An eyeline running off the tight side of the frame usually cramps the shot, because the face gets pinned to the edge; the exception is when you want exactly that pressure on a contributor under threat. And do not centre the subject unless you mean confrontation.  
**Prompt:** `medium interview framing, subject on the left third looking across the empty right side of frame, eyeline just off the lens, static camera`  
**Yields to:** `doc.interrotron` — When you mean confrontation, not conversation.  
`doc.interview_thirds`

### Eyeline Side Switch / A and B Interview Sides

**Also called:** alternating eyelines, flipping the interview side  
**What it is:** One contributor is shot looking camera left, the next looking camera right, so that when you cut between them they read as two people in conversation instead of two people facing the same way.  
**Effect on the audience:** It makes testimony recorded in different places on different days read as a dialogue or an argument. If every contributor faces the same way, the film feels like everyone is reading from one script.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Any documentary with more than one voice, especially when you want to set one account against another. In true crime it is the detective on one side and the defence lawyer on the other. In heritage work it is two scholars who read the same inscription differently.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Interview / Video Podcast, Video Essay | genres: True Crime, Verite / Observational, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Political Thriller, Science / Educational, Mystery / Whodunit  
**Avoid when:** Do not flip the same contributor's side inside one film without a reason; the geography breaks and the viewer feels something is wrong without knowing what.  
**Prompt:** `interview with the subject looking camera left, matched by a second subject in the same film looking camera right, both static medium shots`  
**Yields to:** `doc.group_interview` — When both accounts can sit in one frame.  
`doc.eyeline_side_switch`

### Direct Address to Lens / Interrotron

**Also called:** eye-to-eye interview, Interrotron  
**What it is:** The contributor looks straight down the lens while seeing the interviewer's face reflected on glass in front of it, through a teleprompter style rig. They are talking to a person, but the viewer feels personally addressed.  
**Effect on the audience:** It breaks the wall. Direct eye contact makes the viewer accountable and leaves nowhere to hide from the witness, which doubles the weight of a confession or an accusation.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Heavy testimony: the confession, the accusation, the account you want the viewer judged by. The method is associated with director Errol Morris. It is strongest in true crime and political documentary, and in heritage work when a witness speaks about personal loss rather than history.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Interview / Video Podcast, Video Essay, News Report / Reportage | genres: True Crime, Political Thriller, Psychological / Arthouse, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Verite / Observational, War, Tragedy  
**Avoid when:** Do not use it for every contributor in a film; it loses its weight and becomes an empty house style. And do not use it for dry information, it is a confrontation shot, not an explanation shot.  
**Prompt:** `subject looking straight down the lens, direct eye contact with the viewer, static medium close-up, dark neutral background, soft frontal key`  
**Example:** The Fog of War, Robert McNamara answering straight into the lens.  
**Yields to:** `doc.talking_head` — For dry information; this is a confrontation shot.  
`doc.interrotron`

### Two-Camera Interview (Wide plus Tight)

**Also called:** A and B camera interview, two-size sync  
**What it is:** Two cameras record the same answer at once, one medium and one tight, both on the same side of the eyeline and offset enough in angle that a cut between them does not read as a jump.  
**Effect on the audience:** It gives the editor the freedom to cut inside an answer invisibly, and the move from wide to tight works like an emotional magnification with no camera move at all.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Every anchor interview in a documentary feature or series. Cut to the tight camera exactly on the heavy line or the moment the voice changes. In a video podcast it is what makes an hour-long episode watchable at all.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Interview / Video Podcast, News Report / Reportage, Corporate / Training, Video Essay | genres: Verite / Observational, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), True Crime, Science / Educational, Political Thriller, Drama  
**Avoid when:** Do not let the size difference between the two cameras be small; the cut becomes an ugly jump. Leave at least two size steps between them, not just a change of angle.  
**Prompt:** `two-camera interview setup, camera A medium shot and camera B tight close-up from a slightly different angle, both on the same side of the eyeline, static`  
**Yields to:** `doc.noddy` — With one camera, cover the internal cut instead.  
`doc.two_camera_interview`

### The 45-Degree B Camera

**Also called:** three-quarter B camera, offset second angle  
**What it is:** The second camera sits about forty five degrees off the A camera axis, on the same side of the eyeline, giving a three quarter face with more depth behind it.  
**Effect on the audience:** It breaks the flatness of the frontal face and adds a third dimension. The background changes completely, so the viewer feels moved to another part of the room without leaving it.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The long interview that needs many internal cuts. It is ideal when you want the place behind the contributor to register: the library, the workshop, the excavation, the museum shelves. In heritage work it is the angle that lets the site read as a background instead of a blank wall.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Interview / Video Podcast, Corporate / Training, News Report / Reportage, Video Essay | genres: Verite / Observational, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Science / Educational, True Crime, Political Thriller, Nature / Wildlife  
**Avoid when:** Crossing the eyeline with it breaks the cut, because on the wrong side the direction flips and the two cameras stop belonging to one conversation. And do not push it so tight that the background you placed it for disappears. The offset does not replace the size difference: as the second camera of `doc.two_camera_interview` it still keeps the size step that card asks for, because a change of angle alone cuts as a jump.  
**Prompt:** `second interview camera forty five degrees off axis, three quarter view of the face, background depth visible behind the shoulder, static`  
**Yields to:** `doc.b_camera_profile` — At the breaking, hesitating or evasive beat.  
`doc.b_camera_45`

### The Profile B Camera

**Also called:** side angle second camera, ninety degree B cam  
**What it is:** The second camera sits at roughly ninety degrees to the contributor, so the face is seen from the side and the eyeline travels out of frame toward the interviewer.  
**Effect on the audience:** It moves the viewer a step back, from listening to observing. A profile hides half the expression, so the image carries something withheld.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The moment of breaking, hesitation or evasion, and when a contributor remembers something painful and you do not want to crowd the face. Strong in true crime, and in heritage work for a witness recalling the loss of a house or a city.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Interview / Video Podcast, Video Essay, News Report / Reportage | genres: True Crime, Verite / Observational, Psychological / Arthouse, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Tragedy, Political Thriller, War  
**Avoid when:** Do not build on it as your main angle; a whole answer in profile cools the empathy. Keep it as the second angle you cut to at chosen beats.  
**Prompt:** `profile interview angle, camera ninety degrees to the subject, eyeline leaving frame toward the interviewer, shallow depth, static`  
**Yields to:** `doc.b_camera_45` — As the main second angle; profile cools a whole answer.  
`doc.b_camera_profile`

### Slow Push During the Answer

**Also called:** creeping push on sync, slow motorised dolly in on interview  
**What it is:** A very slow creep on the interview shot, starting medium and ending close on the face, at a speed the viewer does not notice but does feel.  
**Effect on the audience:** It tightens attention with no cut and no sound cue. As the frame closes the viewer's breath closes with it, so the line escalates without the contributor changing a thing.  
**Used for and where it works best:** A long answer that builds toward an admission or a conclusion, and a memory that opens slowly. In true crime and political documentary it is what walks you into the decisive line. In heritage work, on a historian describing the fall of a city.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Interview / Video Podcast, Video Essay, News Report / Reportage | genres: True Crime, Political Thriller, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Psychological / Arthouse, Verite / Observational, Tragedy, Mystery / Whodunit  
**Avoid when:** Do not push on every interview or it becomes a dead habit. And a push the viewer can see usually undercuts the honesty of the testimony, because the camera starts performing the emotion; the exception is a confessional piece that has already declared its own hand.  
**Prompt:** `imperceptibly slow dolly in on a seated interview, starting medium and ending in a close-up on the eyes, no reframing, locked horizon`  
**Yields to:** `doc.two_camera_interview` — When a visible push would undercut the testimony.  
`doc.slow_push_answer`

### Reaction Hold After the Answer

**Also called:** post-answer hold, holding the tail  
**What it is:** You do not cut when the contributor stops talking. You stay on the face for two or three silent seconds and record the breath, the blink and the jaw after the line.  
**Effect on the audience:** The silence after a line carries more than the line did. The viewer reads the face instead of hearing words, and information turns into feeling.  
**Used for and where it works best:** After any heavy line: a death, an admission, a painful memory, a moment of pride. It is the shot that sells testimony in documentary and true crime. In heritage work, right after a witness describes what his city lost.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Interview / Video Podcast, News Report / Reportage, Video Essay | genres: True Crime, Verite / Observational, Tragedy, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Psychological / Arthouse, Political Thriller, War  
**Avoid when:** Do not stretch it past the point of purpose, and do not use it after neutral information; silence after a statistic just reads as empty air.  
**Prompt:** `hold on the subject's face in silence after the line ends, no cut, catch the blink and the exhale, static close-up`  
**Yields to:** `doc.landscape_breather` — When the film must look away after the heavy line.  
`doc.reaction_hold`

### The Question Left In

**Also called:** audible question, keeping the interviewer's voice  
**What it is:** You keep the interviewer's voice asking the question in the cut, instead of hiding it and having the contributor absorb it into the answer. Sometimes the question appears as text on screen instead.  
**Effect on the audience:** It reveals that this is an encounter between people, which raises the sense of honesty and transparency. Sometimes the question itself is the event, when it is sharp or uncomfortable.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Participatory documentary, and investigative reporting when you want the viewer to see the confrontation and the moment a subject refuses to answer. In long-form YouTube it is what makes the conversation feel live rather than assembled.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, News Report / Reportage, Interview / Video Podcast, Video Essay, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Heritage / Historical Documentary | genres: True Crime, Political Thriller, Verite / Observational, Psychological / Arthouse, Mystery / Whodunit, Science / Educational, Comedy  
**Avoid when:** Do not keep it on every answer or the film becomes about the interviewer. And if the question is long or clumsy, put it on screen as text instead.  
**Prompt:** `keep the off-camera question audible before the answer, subject listening in frame before replying, static medium shot`  
**Yields to:** `doc.question_card` — When the question is long or clumsy.  
`doc.question_left_in`

### The Reflective Silence

**Also called:** thinking beat, silence before the answer  
**What it is:** You record and keep the pause before the answer, while the contributor thinks, swallows or looks away, and you leave it in the film at full length.  
**Effect on the audience:** The silence proves the question landed and touched something. The viewer leans in and waits, so the answer arrives with double the weight.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Hard questions: regret, loss, admission, an impossible choice. Strongest in true crime, psychological documentary and war testimony. In heritage work, when you ask a witness about the last time he saw his home.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Interview / Video Podcast, News Report / Reportage, Video Essay | genres: True Crime, Psychological / Arthouse, Tragedy, Verite / Observational, War, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Political Thriller  
**Avoid when:** Do not fabricate the pause in the edit from another take; the face will give you away. And do not use it on a routine answer, silence with no cause makes the film slow, not deep.  
**Prompt:** `hold the pause before the subject speaks, eyes drifting off then returning, no cut, static medium close-up with room tone only`  
`doc.reflective_silence`

### The Last Look to Camera

**Also called:** final glance at the lens, the look after the interview  
**What it is:** At the end of the interview, after the talking stops, the contributor turns and meets the lens for a single moment, sometimes an unintended glance the camera caught before it was cut.  
**Effect on the audience:** It breaks the contract of distance between viewer and witness in a single beat. It plays like a farewell, or a question handed back: now what will you do with this.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The end of a film or the end of a chapter. Place it as the last image after the final line of testimony, before the black or the end card. Very strong in war testimony, true crime and personal heritage documentary.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Interview / Video Podcast, Video Essay, News Report / Reportage | genres: True Crime, Tragedy, War, Verite / Observational, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Psychological / Arthouse, Political Thriller  
**Avoid when:** Usually once in a film, because a repeated look stops being a moment and becomes a trick; the exception is a portrait film built on the same returning gesture. And a contributor asked to perform it reads as acting, so let it happen on its own.  
**Prompt:** `at the end of the take the subject turns and looks directly into the lens for two seconds, then the frame holds and fades`  
`doc.last_look_to_camera`

### The Empty Chair

**Also called:** absent contributor shot, the refused interview  
**What it is:** You shoot the full interview set, lit, with the chair and the background, and no person in it, for a subject who refused, died or could not be reached. It differs from the compositional Empty Chair, which leaves a person shaped gap inside a dramatic scene: here the absence is real and stated as a journalistic fact.  
**Effect on the audience:** Absence reads louder than presence. The empty chair makes the viewer ask why this person is not here, turning the gap into either an accusation or a grief.  
**Used for and where it works best:** When an institution or a person refuses to speak in an investigation, and when a witness dies before the shoot. It works in true crime and political documentary, and in heritage work as a symbol for a generation with no one left to testify.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Video Essay, Interview / Video Podcast | genres: True Crime, Political Thriller, Tragedy, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Mystery / Whodunit, War  
**Avoid when:** Do not use it when there is no real absence, it becomes a cheap device. And do not let it imply guilt when the reason for the absence is innocent or human.  
**Prompt:** `fully lit empty interview set, chair and background ready, no person in frame, static medium shot, room tone only`  
**Yields to:** `doc.question_left_in` — When the refusal happened on camera, show it.  
`doc.empty_chair`

### The Environmental Portrait

**Also called:** interview in the subject's own space, contextual portrait  
**What it is:** The interview happens in a space that belongs to the person: their workshop, kitchen, library or excavation trench, and the place stays visible behind them, finishing the introduction.  
**Effect on the audience:** The background delivers half the introduction with no words. The viewer knows who this person is before the first sentence lands.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Introducing any character in documentary or reportage. The craftsman in his workshop, the archaeologist at her site, the farmer on his land. In heritage documentary, the scholar among the museum shelves or in front of the relief she studies, so the testimony is tied to place rather than to a studio.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Interview / Video Podcast, Corporate / Training, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Video Essay | genres: Verite / Observational, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Science / Educational, Nature / Wildlife, Slice of Life, Political Thriller, True Crime  
**Avoid when:** Do not let the background get so busy that it pulls the eye off the face, and do not pick a pretty location that has nothing to do with the person; the viewer smells the lie.  
**Prompt:** `environmental portrait interview, subject seated in their own workspace, tools and objects of their trade soft behind them, natural window key, static medium shot`  
**Yields to:** `doc.interview_in_darkness` — When place adds nothing or must be stripped.  
`doc.environmental_portrait`

### Interview in Darkness

**Also called:** black void interview, dark limbo background  
**What it is:** The contributor is lit by a single soft key and the background falls to complete black with no detail, so nothing is left in frame except the face.  
**Effect on the audience:** It removes time and place from the image. The testimony floats outside history, the face becomes the only event, and both focus and dramatic weight rise.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Existential or painful testimony, survivor accounts, and any film that needs every contributor to share one visual identity despite different shooting locations. Useful in heritage work when you want testimony to stay timeless rather than tied to an occasion.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Interview / Video Podcast, Video Essay, Trailer / Teaser | genres: True Crime, Tragedy, War, Psychological / Arthouse, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Political Thriller, Mystery / Whodunit  
**Avoid when:** Do not use it for a contributor whose place is part of their story; you throw away free information. And watch dark hair and dark clothing disappearing into the void.  
**Prompt:** `interview against a pure black background, single soft key from one side, no rim on the background, subject isolated in darkness, static medium close-up`  
**Yields to:** `doc.environmental_portrait` — When the contributor's place is part of the story.  
`doc.interview_in_darkness`

### Anonymous Interview (Backlit Silhouette, Altered Voice)

**Also called:** protected identity interview, silhouette interview  
**What it is:** The contributor is placed against a backlight so they read as a featureless silhouette, or only hands and back are shot, and the voice is **replaced** — by an actor, or by a transform that cannot be run backwards — with a card on screen that says so. **Pitch-shifting is not protection, and the measurement is not close.** In the standing benchmark for this, signal-processing disguise of the kind an editor applies leaves speaker identification **almost exactly as accurate as it was on the untouched recording**; the methods that genuinely work replace the voice by re-synthesising it. A simple shift moves one parameter and is reversible with ordinary software. What is needed is an actor reading the transcript — the approach documentaries that got this right have used — or a transform that alters many parameters irreversibly. This card said "the voice is pitched" until September 2026, and that sentence would have shipped a serving guard's recoverable voice.  
**Effect on the audience:** The fear itself becomes part of the information. The viewer understands that this testimony costs the speaker something, which raises the stakes without a single line of explanation.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The threatened witness, the whistleblower, the survivor who does not want their family identified. Standard in true crime and political investigation. These are the briefs it names and then hands over, because its method is withdrawn below. The hidden-face rule in heritage documentary, when you portray an ancient figure, is served by `doc.faceless_reenactment`, which hides a face nobody can know rather than protecting a person.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, News Report / Reportage, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Docudrama / Reenactment, Video Essay | genres: True Crime, Political Thriller, War, Verite / Observational, Mystery / Whodunit, Tragedy, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused)  
**Avoid when:** Watch for a reflection or a stray side light revealing features inside the silhouette, and keep it for contributors who need real protection, because used for atmosphere alone it tells the audience there is a danger that does not exist. **A silhouette hides a face and not much else, and it is worse than that.** The outline carries the uniform, the insignia, the cap, the build, the posture and the hair — and for a contributor whose job identifies them, a guard or a soldier or a nurse, the outline is the giveaway and the face never was. Worse: **a clean backlit silhouette is the exact input format gait-recognition systems are built to read**, so the most film-literate way to shoot a protected contributor hands an identifier over in its preferred form. Published work puts recognition from body, clothing and scene context at around **eighty per cent with the head completely blacked out**. Shoot them out of uniform, break the outline, or do not shoot the body at all.
**This package does not cover protecting a contributor's identity, and will not try.** The line
above exists to withdraw advice that was wrong, not to replace it. Concealment is a specialist
discipline with its own literature and its own failure modes, and the reason this file stops here is
that a *partial* method is worse than none: it is followed, it is believed, and the person it was
supposed to protect is the one who finds out it did not work.

What is worth knowing is only this, and only because it is what makes the refusal necessary: **the
obvious methods do not work.** A blurred or pixelated face is reconstructible. A pitch-shifted voice
measures as barely protected at all. A backlit silhouette is the preferred input format of
gait-recognition systems, and the outline still carries the uniform and the build. And the things
that identify a person are mostly not the face — the room's sound, the speaker's own vocabulary, the
view behind them, and the jigsaw of role, place and date that is harmless in any one piece and
conclusive together.

**So when a brief requires that somebody not be identifiable, this skill writes no concealment
method, and the work does not stop** (root [`../../SKILL.md`](../../SKILL.md) §0). Design everything else
in the scene — the testimony, the room, the cutaways, the hands, the place — and put one note in the
handoff: the concealment method is for whoever delivers the piece.

*(Markers are defined in [`../ENGINE-CHECK.md`](../ENGINE-CHECK.md), which owns the set and the rule that each one closes two ways — resolved, or `unobtainable`, with what was done instead.)*

**Prompt:** **none, deliberately.** This card withdrew its own advice above — a backlit silhouette
does not conceal a person, and the outline is the thing that identifies them — and a card that hands
the work over cannot also ship a shot for it. The rest of the
scene is prompted from its other cards; this one adds no concealment prompt.

*(This field used to carry a full silhouette prompt, and had even been revised once — the earlier
version framed the subject against a bright window, and that was corrected because a window is a
view and a view is a location. The correction reached the prose and left the prompt live. **That is
the defect: a card can be repaired where it is read and stay broken where it is executed**, because
the prompt is the field that gets copied into the generator.)*

**Yields to:** Root SKILL.md §0 — Any brief requiring a contributor not be identifiable: the rest of the scene is written, and the concealment method is a note in the handoff.  
`doc.anonymous_interview`

### The In-Car Interview

**Also called:** car sync, driving interview, dashboard rig  
**What it is:** The contributor drives or rides while the camera is rigged on the bonnet, on the windscreen or from the back seat, and the talk happens on the move with the landscape crossing the window.  
**Effect on the audience:** The car is a closed box that makes talk intimate, and the eyes on the road instead of on the interviewer lower the guard, so admissions arrive that never come in a chair.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Moving to a new place in the film, returning to a site of memory, and the difficult admission. Strong in personal documentary and documentary road films, and in heritage work when you take a witness back toward his old city and shoot the approach.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Video Essay | genres: Verite / Observational, Road Movie, True Crime, Psychological / Arthouse, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Political Thriller, Slice of Life  
**Avoid when:** Safety and the law come before the shot, and if the contributor is driving do not ask questions that distract. Window light changes every second, so shoot enough matching coverage.  
**Prompt:** `in-car interview, camera mounted on the bonnet looking back through the windscreen, driver talking, landscape reflecting across the glass`  
**Yields to:** `doc.walking_interview` — When driving is unsafe, unlawful or distracting.  
`doc.car_interview`

### The Working-Hands Interview

**Also called:** interview over the task, talking while working  
**What it is:** The contributor keeps working while answering: shaping clay, carving, sewing, cleaning an artefact, and the camera moves between the face and the hands.  
**Effect on the audience:** The task removes the tension and gives the speech a natural rhythm. The hands also hand the editor free cutaways that hide every internal cut.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Crafts, trades and applied science: the potter, the calligrapher, the conservator, the cook, the excavation worker. In heritage documentary it is the best way to carry testimony and craft in one setup, and it serves a hidden-face approach when you shoot only the hands.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Corporate / Training, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Video Essay | genres: Verite / Observational, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Science / Educational, Slice of Life, Nature / Wildlife, Family  
**Avoid when:** Do not let the noise of the work bury the words, record the two on separate tracks. And do not ask for a task they do not normally perform; staged slowness always shows.  
**Prompt:** `interview with the subject working at their bench while answering, cutaways of the hands mid task, natural window light, handheld medium and macro`  
**Yields to:** `doc.environmental_portrait` — When there is no task they genuinely perform.  
`doc.working_hands_interview`

### Group Interview Staging

**Also called:** multi-contributor interview, family interview setup  
**What it is:** More than one contributor in the same setup: a couple, siblings, a team. The staging decides who is primary, and the coverage needs a group shot plus a tight single on each person.  
**Effect on the audience:** The relationship itself becomes visible: who interrupts, who stays quiet, who checks the other before answering. You get drama that two separate interviews can never give you.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Families, partners, an excavation team, survivors of the same event. Use it when the agreement or the conflict between two accounts is the point. In heritage work, two generations of one family remembering the same house.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Interview / Video Podcast, News Report / Reportage, Corporate / Training, Video Essay | genres: Verite / Observational, Family, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), True Crime, Tragedy, Slice of Life, Political Thriller  
**Avoid when:** Do not line them up flat on a sofa; stagger them in depth and height. And beware that the dominant one will eat the whole conversation unless you address questions by name.  
**Prompt:** `two contributors seated at different depths in one frame, the speaker sharp and the listener soft behind, static medium wide, single tight coverage on each`  
**Yields to:** `doc.eyeline_side_switch` — When one contributor would eat the room.  
`doc.group_interview`

### Over-the-Interviewer Two-Shot

**Also called:** interviewer OTS, reverse two-shot in interview  
**What it is:** A shot of the contributor taken over the interviewer's shoulder, with the interviewer's shoulder and neck soft in the foreground, revealing this as an encounter between two people.  
**Effect on the audience:** It converts a statement into a conversation. The soft shoulder in the foreground adds depth and puts the viewer inside the room rather than in front of a screen.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Participatory documentary, the confrontation in investigative reporting, and any beat where you need the interviewer's presence acknowledged. In video podcasts it is the shot that ties the two speakers together.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, News Report / Reportage, Interview / Video Podcast, Video Essay, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Corporate / Training | genres: True Crime, Political Thriller, Verite / Observational, Mystery / Whodunit, Science / Educational, Psychological / Arthouse, Comedy

**Prompt:** `over-the-shoulder two-shot in an interview, interviewer's shoulder soft in foreground, contributor sharp and framed on the far third, static`  
**Yields to:** `doc.talking_head` — In a film built on pure testimony.

### The Noddy / Listening Cutaways

**Also called:** noddies, reverse listening shots, cutaway nods  
**What it is:** Short shots of the interviewer listening, nodding or asking, usually recorded after the interview from the reverse angle, and used to cover cuts inside an answer.  
**Effect on the audience:** They make the edit invisible, protect the continuity of an answer and give the viewer a rest from one face.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Every news package and long broadcast interview needs them. Shoot them straight after the interview in the same light and the same seating. If your style hides the interviewer, replace them with a hands shot or a detail from the room.  
**Best in:** formats: News Report / Reportage, Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Interview / Video Podcast, Corporate / Training, Video Essay | genres: Verite / Observational, Political Thriller, True Crime, Science / Educational, Mystery / Whodunit, Drama  
**Avoid when:** Avoid exaggerated nodding, it looks staged and cheapens the report. A noddy covers a cut and carries no meaning of its own: never cut it in as the reaction that gives a line its weight, and never let it stand in for the contributor's own pause — a beat that carries meaning stays on its own clock (`doc.reflective_silence`, `doc.crowd_reaction_cut`). And make sure the interviewer's light and wardrobe have not changed between the interview and the cutaways.  
**Prompt:** `reverse shot of the interviewer listening and nodding slightly, same lighting and seating as the interview, static medium close-up`  
**Yields to:** `doc.object_insert` — When the style hides the interviewer, cut to a room detail.  
`doc.noddy`

### Floating the Sync / Interview as Voice-Over

**Also called:** sync as VO, laying the interview under B-roll  
**What it is:** You lift the contributor's audio off the interview shot and let it continue over B-roll, so the witness becomes the narrator instead of staying a fixed face in frame.  
**Effect on the audience:** It frees the picture from the chair. The viewer hears the testimony while seeing what it describes, so information and feeling arrive together.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The core rule of documentary editing: open on the face for a line or two so the witness is established, float the audio over B-roll, then return to the face on the emotional beat. In heritage work, the historian's voice over site and relief footage.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Video Essay, Corporate / Training, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form | genres: Verite / Observational, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), True Crime, Science / Educational, Nature / Wildlife, Political Thriller, War  
**Avoid when:** Do not float the audio before the viewer knows whose voice it is, or it becomes a disembodied narrator. And a picture that contradicts the line spoken over it usually cancels both; the exception is a deliberate irony the film has taught the viewer to read.  
**Prompt:** `hold the interview shot for the first line then dissolve to matching B-roll while the same voice continues over the picture`  
**Yields to:** `doc.talking_head` — Until the viewer knows whose voice it is.  
`doc.float_the_sync`

### Multi-Camera Video Podcast

**Also called:** three-camera podcast, talk show setup  
**What it is:** Three locked-off cameras run at once, one on the host, one on the guest and a wide holding both, and the edit switches between them by who is speaking.  
**Effect on the audience:** It makes very long talk watchable. Switching gives visual rhythm to what is really audio, and the wide re-establishes the relationship between the two people.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The video podcast, the long interview and the conversational corporate piece. Cut to the wide when they laugh or talk over each other, return to the singles on the point. Crop your vertical shorts straight out of the speaker's own camera.  
**Best in:** formats: Interview / Video Podcast, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Video Essay, Corporate / Training, Documentary Series, News Report / Reportage, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok) | genres: Verite / Observational, Comedy, Science / Educational, Political Thriller, True Crime, Slice of Life, Psychological / Arthouse  
**Avoid when:** Do not switch on every sentence; fast switching tires the eye. Switch when the speaker or the meaning changes, not on a metronome.  
**Prompt:** `three-camera talk setup, locked medium on each speaker plus a wide two-shot, matched lighting, switch on the speaker change`  
**Yields to:** `doc.two_camera_interview` — One contributor: two sizes, not three positions.  
`doc.podcast_multicam`

### The Vox Pop

**Also called:** man on the street, street interview  
**What it is:** Very short interviews with ordinary people in the street, all answering the same question, shot quickly in a medium frame with the live street kept behind them.  
**Effect on the audience:** It gives a collective pulse without pretending to be a survey. The run of short answers builds rhythm, and the contrast between faces sketches a city or a public mood.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Opening a news report, taking the temperature of a street after an event, and breaking the rhythm of a film full of experts. In heritage content, ask the neighbourhood what they know about the mound next to them; the answers become the subject.  
**Best in:** formats: News Report / Reportage, Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Video Essay, Heritage / Historical Documentary | genres: Verite / Observational, Political Thriller, Comedy, Slice of Life, Science / Educational, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic  
**Avoid when:** Do not present it as representative data, it is a mood not evidence. And do not shoot them all against one background; the visual variety is half its value.  
**Prompt:** `quick street interview, medium shot of a passer-by answering, busy street alive and soft behind them, handheld, natural light`  
**Yields to:** `doc.stats_card` — When you need evidence, not a mood.  
`doc.vox_pop`

### Presenter Piece to Camera

**Also called:** PTC, stand-upper, direct presenter address  
**What it is:** The presenter stands in the place and speaks straight to the lens, with the location behind them carrying part of the information, not just decorating it.  
**Effect on the audience:** It proves the presenter was really there, which raises the credibility of the report. Direct address also creates a personal contract with the viewer.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The news report, the presenter-led documentary and the travel film. Use it for the information the pictures cannot show: the background, the number, the date. In heritage work, the presenter in front of the wall saying when it was built and by whom.  
**Best in:** formats: News Report / Reportage, Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Corporate / Training, Explainer | genres: Verite / Observational, Political Thriller, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Science / Educational, Nature / Wildlife, Adventure, Slice of Life  
**Avoid when:** Do not stand against a blank wall, you throw away the reason for being there. And keep it short; direct address past twenty seconds turns into a lecture.  
**Prompt:** `presenter piece to camera, medium shot, speaker addressing the lens with the location clearly readable behind them, natural light, static`  
**Yields to:** `doc.stand_up` — At a news scene, where being there is the point.  
`doc.piece_to_camera`

### The Walk-and-Talk Presenter

**Also called:** walking PTC, moving stand-up  
**What it is:** The presenter talks to camera while walking, with the camera travelling backwards ahead of them on a gimbal or Steadicam, so the background changes through the sentence.  
**Effect on the audience:** Movement gives energy and progression. The story feels like it is advancing and the place is revealed gradually instead of presented all at once.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Opening an episode, bridging between sections, and entering a new location. In heritage work, the presenter walks the corridor between the walls while explaining, and the camera reveals the courtyard behind him on the last word, so you land information and a reveal in one shot.  
**Best in:** formats: News Report / Reportage, Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Corporate / Training, Explainer | genres: Verite / Observational, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Adventure, Nature / Wildlife, Science / Educational, Political Thriller, Slice of Life  
**Avoid when:** Watch the ground and the operator walking backwards; one stumble kills the take. And do not walk through a neutral space, movement with no reveal is wasted.  
**Prompt:** `presenter walking and talking to camera, gimbal tracking backwards at a steady pace, location revealing itself behind the shoulder, eye level`  
**Yields to:** `doc.piece_to_camera` — When the space has nothing to reveal.  
`doc.walk_and_talk_presenter`

### The Expert on Location

**Also called:** expert walk-through, specialist in situ  
**What it is:** Instead of shooting the expert in an office, you bring them to the site itself, so they explain standing at the thing they are describing, touching it and pointing at it.  
**Effect on the audience:** The information becomes physical. The same words delivered at the stone land ten times harder than in a studio, because the eye can verify what the ear is told.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Science, heritage and nature documentary. The archaeologist in the trench, the conservator at the wall, the geologist on the canyon edge. Ask them to begin the line while touching the object, and keep a medium frame so hand and face read together.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Explainer, Video Essay, Corporate / Training | genres: Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Science / Educational, Nature / Wildlife, Verite / Observational, Adventure, Mythology / Ancient Epic  
**Avoid when:** Do not drag an expert to a location that is not theirs just because it looks good; the discomfort shows in the delivery. And guard against wind noise on open sites.  
**Prompt:** `expert explaining on location, medium shot, hand resting on the carved stone they describe, site readable behind them, natural light`  
**Yields to:** `doc.environmental_portrait` — When the site is not theirs.  
`doc.expert_on_location`

### The Point-at-the-Thing Beat

**Also called:** the point and cut, indicate and reveal  
**What it is:** The speaker points at a specific detail, and you cut immediately to a close shot of that same detail, so the gesture becomes the bridge between the two shots.  
**Effect on the audience:** It directs the viewer's attention with no extra words. The hand says where to look and the cut answers at once, so the film feels responsive.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Every field explanation: an inscription, a crack in the wall, a mark on a map, a part of a machine. In heritage work it is how you take the viewer to a detail they would never have found alone, a wedge or a symbol inside the relief.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Explainer, Corporate / Training, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Video Essay | genres: Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Science / Educational, Nature / Wildlife, Verite / Observational, Mystery / Whodunit, True Crime  
**Avoid when:** Do not cut before the gesture completes, an early cut breaks the link. And make sure the close shot is of the very same thing, not something similar.  
**Prompt:** `speaker points at a carved detail on the wall, cut to a tight insert of that exact detail, matched light and angle`  
**Yields to:** `doc.zoom_circle_detail` — When nobody is in frame to point.  
`doc.point_at_the_thing`

### Screen Recording as Evidence

**Also called:** screen capture, desktop documentary, screen-life insert  
**What it is:** You record a real screen as it opens and scrolls: an email, a post, a file, a search result, keeping the cursor and the loading visible so it reads as happening now. It differs from The Screen-Recording Proof Shot in advertising, which shows an app working as proof of a product: here the screen is a source and a document.  
**Effect on the audience:** It delivers the feel of direct evidence. The viewer sees the source rather than being told about it, and the moving cursor implies a human doing the looking.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The video essay, investigative reporting and the explainer. Use it to show a document, a post or a figure from an official source, and push into the relevant area instead of reading it aloud. In heritage work, showing an excavation publication or a museum database record.  
**Best in:** formats: Video Essay, News Report / Reportage, Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Explainer, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Corporate / Training, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok) | genres: True Crime, Political Thriller, Science / Educational, Mystery / Whodunit, Verite / Observational, Comedy, Psychological / Arthouse  
**Avoid when:** Do not expose personal data or the names of uninvolved people, blur them before you publish. And keep the capture short, a static screen kills the pace.  
**Prompt:** `clean screen recording of a document being opened and scrolled, cursor visible, slow push into the key paragraph, highlight appearing`  
**Yields to:** `doc.the_receipt` — When the source needs citing, not navigating.  
`doc.screen_recording`

### Illustrative B-Roll

**Also called:** cover shots, illustrative cutaways  
**What it is:** Shots that show exactly what the narration or the testimony is talking about: he says the market, you see the market; she says the river, you see the river.  
**Effect on the audience:** It anchors the information and puts eye and ear on the same job, so the viewer understands faster and remembers longer.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The backbone of any report or expository documentary. Tie the shot to the exact line it belongs to, not the line before or after. In heritage work, if the narration names the city gate, show that gate and not a similar one from another site.  
**Best in:** formats: News Report / Reportage, Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Explainer, Corporate / Training, Video Essay, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form | genres: Verite / Observational, Science / Educational, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Political Thriller, Nature / Wildlife, True Crime  
**Avoid when:** Do not turn the film into a literal illustration of every word, it becomes dull and obvious. And the bigger sin: passing off a shot from another location as the one being described.  
**Prompt:** `clean illustrative cutaway of the exact place named in the narration, static wide then a matching detail, natural light`  
**Yields to:** `doc.broll_atmospheric` — When literal illustration turns dull and obvious.  
`doc.broll_illustrative`

### Atmospheric B-Roll

**Also called:** mood shots, texture shots, poetic cutaways  
**What it is:** Shots that explain nothing and build feeling instead: dust turning in a shaft of light, a curtain moving, water running, a shadow crawling across a wall, a bird crossing.  
**Effect on the audience:** It opens psychological space between facts. The viewer breathes and feels the place instead of receiving data, and the feeling that survives the film usually came from these shots.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Right before or right after a heavy beat, between chapters, and under poetic narration. In heritage documentary: dust inside the temple, light crawling over the relief, the river at first light. These are the shots that make a film feel ancient and outside time.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Video Essay, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Music Video, Title Sequence / Opening Credits, Trailer / Teaser | genres: Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Verite / Observational, Nature / Wildlife, Psychological / Arthouse, Tragedy, Mythology / Ancient Epic, Slice of Life  
**Avoid when:** Do not stack them without cause or the film turns into an empty music video. Each one works between two facts; standing in for a fact you failed to get is where the shot turns into decoration.  
**Prompt:** `atmospheric texture shot, dust particles drifting through a shaft of light in a stone chamber, slow drift, no subject, ambient sound only`  
**Yields to:** `doc.broll_illustrative` — When the mood shot is standing in for a missing fact.  
`doc.broll_atmospheric`

### Cutting the Picture to the Word

**Also called:** sync to narration, cutting on the noun  
**What it is:** You place the head of each shot on the exact word it belongs to inside the narration, so the cut lands on the noun or the number and not a beat late.  
**Effect on the audience:** It makes the film feel precise and under control. The viewer never notices the sync but feels everything is in its place, and the information goes in without resistance.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Any voiced piece: the report, the explainer, the expository documentary. Mark the key words in the script first, then hang the shots on them. In heritage documentary, the king's name on the statue shot, the city's name on the aerial.  
**Best in:** formats: News Report / Reportage, Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Explainer, Corporate / Training, Video Essay, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form | genres: Science / Educational, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Political Thriller, Verite / Observational, Nature / Wildlife, True Crime  
**Avoid when:** Do not sync every word or the film reads like an illustrated dictionary. Sync the key words only and let the rest of the shot run on.  
**Prompt:** `cut the shot head exactly on the spoken keyword in the voice-over, hold the shot through the rest of the sentence`  
**Yields to:** `doc.broll_cover_vo` — When syncing every word; cut on ideas instead.  
`doc.broll_sync_to_narration`

### The Process / Craft Shot

**Also called:** how it is made shot, craft sequence  
**What it is:** You shoot a complete process through its stages: the clay kneaded, shaped, fired, finished. Each stage is covered at a different size and the cut links them in order.  
**Effect on the audience:** It feeds an old hunger in the viewer: how is this thing made. Attention becomes automatic because the brain wants to see the finish.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The strongest weapon in heritage and craft documentary: making pottery, weaving cloth, laying a mudbrick wall, copying an inscription. Shoot hands, face, wide and macro for every stage, and save the finished object for the last shot. It also serves a hidden-face approach, because the hands carry the story.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Explainer, Corporate / Training, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Vlog / YouTube Long-Form | genres: Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Science / Educational, Verite / Observational, Nature / Wildlife, Slice of Life, Family  
**Avoid when:** Do not cut a stage off half way, the viewer feels the gap. And do not ask the maker to slow down for the camera, staged movement always shows.  
**Prompt:** `full process sequence of a craftsman shaping clay on the wheel, wide then hands macro then finished piece, natural window light`  
**Yields to:** `doc.sequence_shooting` — When time is short and it will not repeat.  
`doc.process_craft_shot`

### The Hands Montage

**Also called:** hands sequence, hands only cutaways  
**What it is:** A run of close shots of hands at work: holding, opening, cleaning, writing, praying, taken from varied angles and sizes and cut to a rhythm.  
**Effect on the audience:** Hands speak of age, skill and fatigue with no face. The viewer reads skin and movement and completes the person in their imagination, so the empathy widens instead of attaching to one face.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Introducing a trade or a generation, covering a voice-over, opening a chapter. In heritage documentary it is the core device for portraying an ancient figure with no face: hands holding a clay tablet, fingers tracing a relief, a palm pouring water.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Docudrama / Reenactment, Title Sequence / Opening Credits, Video Essay, Music Video, Commercial / TVC | genres: Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Verite / Observational, Tragedy, Slice of Life, Nature / Wildlife, Family, Mythology / Ancient Epic  
**Avoid when:** Watch for details that betray the period: a modern watch, nail polish, the wrong ring in a heritage piece. And do not let the montage run without escalation, it turns into empty decoration.  
**Prompt:** `macro hand shots working clay and tracing carved lines, varied angles, no faces, shallow depth, warm low sun`  
**Yields to:** `doc.process_craft_shot` — When the montage has no escalation.  
`doc.hands_montage`

### The Object Insert

**Also called:** cutaway insert, detail insert  
**What it is:** A very tight shot of a single object tied to the story: a key, a letter, a photograph, a broken pot, with the camera still and the object sitting in the light.  
**Effect on the audience:** The object becomes evidence or symbol. The tight frame grants it importance beyond its size, and the viewer registers that it matters before anyone explains why.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Every time a witness names a physical thing, and every time you need a cut inside an answer. In true crime it is the evidence. In heritage work it is the cylinder seal, the inscribed brick, the old family photograph. Always shoot them before you need them.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Video Essay, Docudrama / Reenactment, Corporate / Training, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok) | genres: True Crime, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Mystery / Whodunit, Verite / Observational, Tragedy, Science / Educational  
**Avoid when:** Do not shoot the object in flat light that hides its texture, a flat object dies on screen. And an object the story never mentions usually creates noise rather than information; the exception is a detail you are planting now to pay off later.  
**Prompt:** `tight insert of a single object on a dark surface, raking side light revealing texture, shallow depth, static camera`  
**Yields to:** `doc.macro_artifact` — When the surface, not the identity, carries the line.  
`doc.object_insert`

### Shooting a Sequence (Wide, Medium, Tight, Detail, Reverse)

**Also called:** five-shot sequence, sequence discipline  
**What it is:** For any field event you shoot five shots: a wide for the place, a medium for the action, a tight on the hands, a detail, and a reverse from behind or in front of the person.  
**Effect on the audience:** It guarantees the scene can be cut to any length and still read. The viewer gets where, what and who with no narration, and the camera feels like it was everywhere.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Core discipline for reportage and field documentary when time is short and the event will not repeat. Hold each shot at least ten seconds and keep it steady. In heritage work, the same method on an excavation, from the wide down to the trowel in the worker's hand.  
**Best in:** formats: News Report / Reportage, Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Corporate / Training, Video Essay, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form | genres: Verite / Observational, Science / Educational, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Nature / Wildlife, Political Thriller, Slice of Life, War  
**Avoid when:** Do not shoot everything at one size or chase the action with the zoom; you will come back with material that cannot be cut. And watch the line of action between shots.  
**Prompt:** `five-shot field sequence of one activity, wide then medium then hands tight then detail then reverse, each held ten seconds, steady`  
**Yields to:** `doc.long_observational_hold` — When the event rewards one unbroken shot.  
`doc.sequence_shooting`

### The Landscape Breather

**Also called:** breathing shot, pause shot, visual rest  
**What it is:** A still wide of the landscape with no action and no words, placed between two heavy sections and held longer than a normal shot.  
**Effect on the audience:** It gives the brain a moment to digest what it just heard. After hard testimony the viewer needs to look at something still, or half of the next section will be lost.  
**Used for and where it works best:** After a confession or a heavy fact, between chapters, and before a change of subject. In heritage documentary: the horizon, the desert or the river at dusk after a description of a massacre or a ruined city. Give it at least five seconds.

**Prompt:** `static wide landscape at dusk, no people, slow ambient movement of light and dust only, held long, wind and distant sound`  
**Yields to:** `doc.broll_atmospheric` — When texture suits the pause better than a horizon.  
`doc.landscape_breather`

### The Follow-Behind

**Also called:** back-of-head follow, trailing shot  
**What it is:** The camera travels behind the subject at shoulder height and follows them into a place or along a path, with the face never seen.

**Effect on the audience:** It compresses long time into a short space. The viewer reads a whole life or a whole era in twenty seconds, and the music carries the emotion instead of narration.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Establishing a character's past, telling an era, the memorial or remembrance passage. In heritage documentary: historical excavation photographs or the city as it was a century ago, cut to a slow, sober rhythm.

### The Day-in-the-Life Structure

**Also called:** dawn to dusk structure, one day arc  
**What it is:** You build the film or the chapter on a single day: waking, work, the meal, evening, sleep. The light becomes the clock and the viewer reads the hour from the picture.  
**Effect on the audience:** It supplies a ready-made structure without a plot. The viewer instinctively knows where they are in the story, and the daily repetition reveals character better than any interview.  
**Used for and where it works best:** A portrait of a person, a place or a trade, and observational documentary generally. In heritage work: a day with a site guard or a potter, from dawn light on the clay to the lamp going out. Shoot the light in the right order so the sense of time holds.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Corporate / Training, Video Essay, News Report / Reportage | genres: Verite / Observational, Slice of Life, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Nature / Wildlife, Family, Historical / Biopic, Science / Educational  
**Avoid when:** Do not claim one day if you shot three with different light, the eye catches it. And do not leave the day without an event; even an ordinary day needs one turning point.  
**Prompt:** `dawn to dusk sequence of one working day, matching light progression through the shots, observational handheld, natural sound`  
**Yields to:** `doc.day_timeline` — In platform form, mark the hours with stamps.  
`doc.day_in_the_life`

### The Question Card

**Also called:** on-screen question, text question card  
**What it is:** The question appears as text on a plain background or over picture, instead of the interviewer's voice, and the answer follows straight after.  
**Effect on the audience:** It renders the question neutral and toneless, so all the attention goes to the answer. The card also gives a small pause that organises the film into sections.  
**Used for and where it works best:** A documentary built on the same set of questions put to every contributor, the video essay and short-form content. Ideal when you want to compare different people's answers to one question without your own voice in the film.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Video Essay, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Interview / Video Podcast, News Report / Reportage, Corporate / Training | genres: Verite / Observational, Science / Educational, Political Thriller, Comedy, Psychological / Arthouse, True Crime, Slice of Life  
**Avoid when:** Keep the text short, a question longer than two lines cannot be read in the time available. And watch the text position in vertical so the platform interface does not cover it.  
**Prompt:** `question appearing as clean white text on black for two seconds, then hard cut to the contributor answering in a static medium shot`  
**Yields to:** `doc.question_left_in` — When the encounter itself should be heard.  
`doc.question_card`

### The Documentary Cold Open

**Also called:** teaser open, pre-title hook  
**What it is:** You open with the strongest moment you have: a shocking line from an interview, a piece of call audio, an unexplained image, before any introduction or title, and then the title lands.  
**Effect on the audience:** It buys the viewer's attention before they decide to leave. The question the cold open raises stays hanging and pulls the whole film along.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Every documentary, every episode of a documentary series, and any long video on a platform. Take the heaviest fifteen seconds in your material and put it first. In heritage work: a raking-light shot of a relief with a question about who carved it and why.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Video Essay, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Trailer / Teaser | genres: True Crime, Mystery / Whodunit, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Political Thriller, Thriller / Suspense, Science / Educational, War  
**Avoid when:** Do not burn the big reveal in the open, take the question and leave the answer. And a shot that is not in the film is the one that costs you, because the viewer waits for it and it never comes.  
**Prompt:** `open on the strongest fifteen seconds of material with no context, cut to black, then the title card, then begin the film properly`  
**Yields to:** `doc.nat_sound_open` — When the strongest material is a sound, not a line.  
`doc.doc_cold_open`

### The End Cards / Where Are They Now

**Also called:** outcome cards, epilogue text  
**What it is:** White text on black at the end of the film stating what happened to the people, the case or the site after filming, one or two lines per card.  
**Effect on the audience:** It delivers closure the footage cannot, and leaves a strong last impression because the viewer reads it in silence. Written information feels official and final.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The end of any documentary with an open case or an unresolved fate: a verdict, the death of a contributor, a factory closing, a site listed as heritage or looted. Order them from lightest to heaviest and save the strongest for last.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Video Essay, Docudrama / Reenactment | genres: True Crime, Political Thriller, Tragedy, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), War, Verite / Observational, Science / Educational  
**Avoid when:** Do not run more than four cards, the viewer stops reading. And on a piece presented as fact, take each line from the brief, the skill or a search, the closest reasonable choice recorded under `decisions`; on any other piece the brief's words are used as given.  
**Prompt:** `white serif text on black stating the outcome, held four seconds per card, silence or a single sustained note underneath`  
**Yields to:** `doc.last_look_to_camera` — When the footage can close it without text.  
`doc.where_are_they_now`

### Observational Follow / Fly on the Wall

**Also called:** fly on the wall, verite follow  
**What it is:** The camera follows the event as it happens with no intervention and no direction: no added light, no second takes, and the operator chases the action and reframes on the fly.  
**Effect on the audience:** An honesty no staging can buy. The flaws themselves, focus catching up and imperfect framing, tell the viewer this really happened and nobody arranged it.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The family, the operating room, the courtroom, the day on the dig, the moment the news lands. Best when the event itself is stronger than any narration. In heritage work: the excavation team the instant something comes out of the soil, when the first reaction is the shot.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Video Essay, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality | genres: Verite / Observational, Slice of Life, Family, True Crime, Science / Educational, Nature / Wildlife, War, Political Thriller  
**Avoid when:** Do not intervene and then claim you observed; the mode is a contract with the viewer. And do not chase with the zoom, move the camera or let the action come to the frame.  
**Prompt:** `handheld observational follow of a real event, reframing as it happens, available light only, focus adjusting live, natural sound`  
**Yields to:** `doc.participatory_mode` — If you intervene, declare it and enter the film.  
`doc.observational_follow`

### The Long Observational Hold

**Also called:** long take observation, sustained static shot  
**What it is:** One static or near-static shot held for thirty seconds or more on a real situation, with no cut and no narration, letting the event develop inside the frame.  
**Effect on the audience:** It forces the viewer to observe instead of consume. After a few seconds of boredom they start noticing details they would never have caught, and the image becomes an experience rather than information.  
**Used for and where it works best:** A ritual, a repetitive task or an act of waiting: a prayer, baking bread, washing a find, a guard sitting at the site. Use it once or twice in a film as rhythm anchors; in heritage documentary it delivers the slow time of a place.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Video Essay, Experimental / Art Film, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form | genres: Verite / Observational, Slice of Life, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Nature / Wildlife, Psychological / Arthouse, Faith / Biblical Epic, Historical / Biopic, Tragedy  
**Avoid when:** Do not hold on a situation with no development; length with no change drives the viewer away. And do not repeat it often, more than twice in a film becomes exhausting.  
**Prompt:** `single static shot held for a full minute on a real task, no cut, no music, ambient sound only, subtle changes inside the frame`  
**Yields to:** `doc.sequence_shooting` — When nothing develops inside the frame.  
`doc.long_observational_hold`

### Waiting for the Moment

**Also called:** rolling and waiting, the patience shot  
**What it is:** You keep rolling and wait, asking for nothing: waiting for the bird to lift, the person to break, the sun to enter the opening, the child to turn. The only decisions are when to start and how long to stay.  
**Effect on the audience:** A moment that arrives by itself carries an authenticity no direction can imitate. It is usually the shot the viewer remembers long after every fact is forgotten.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Observational, nature and heritage documentary. On archaeological sites: waiting for the sun line to reach the relief at a precise hour, or for the shadow to fill the courtyard. Set up an hour early and hold your position.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Video Essay, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Experimental / Art Film, Music Video | genres: Verite / Observational, Nature / Wildlife, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Psychological / Arthouse, Slice of Life, Faith / Biblical Epic, Tragedy  
**Avoid when:** Do not get impatient and ask people to repeat the moment, a repeat has no soul. And do not wait aimlessly, decide exactly what you are waiting for before you roll.  
**Prompt:** `locked-off camera waiting on an empty scene until the light reaches the carved wall, no cut, no movement, long take`  
**Yields to:** `doc.observational_follow` — When the moment moves and will not come to you.  
`doc.waiting_for_the_moment`

### Let It Breathe

**Also called:** holding the shot, resisting the cut  
**What it is:** You let the shot run two or three seconds after the information or the action ends, instead of cutting on the last word or the last movement.  
**Effect on the audience:** It gives the viewer time to digest and to react privately. A film that never gives time feels tense and crowded even when its content is calm.  
**Used for and where it works best:** In heritage documentary and any film built on place or ritual. After the aerial of the site, after the close on the carving, after the witness's last line. Judge by ear, not by seconds: cut when you feel full, not before.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Video Essay, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Experimental / Art Film, Music Video | genres: Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Verite / Observational, Nature / Wildlife, Psychological / Arthouse, Faith / Biblical Epic, Tragedy, Slice of Life  
**Avoid when:** Do not let every shot breathe or the film goes slack. Breath is spent at the important points, not on every cut.  
**Prompt:** `hold the shot three seconds after the action ends before cutting, no reframe, ambient sound continuing`  
**Yields to:** `doc.landscape_breather` — When the shot on screen cannot hold the extra seconds.  
`doc.let_it_breathe`

### Revealing the Place Through a Person

**Also called:** human scale reveal, guided discovery  
**What it is:** Instead of presenting the place with a direct wide, you follow a person moving through it: opening a door, lighting a lamp, walking a corridor, so the space is revealed at the pace of their movement.  
**Effect on the audience:** It gives scale a human unit. The viewer measures the space against the body they can see, so the temple becomes genuinely enormous rather than an architectural picture.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The first entry into any large location: the temple, the palace, the storeroom, the museum after closing. In heritage documentary it is your strongest tool for scale, and it serves the hidden-face rule because the figure is shot from behind or as a silhouette.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Docudrama / Reenactment, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Trailer / Teaser, Video Essay, Music Video | genres: Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Mythology / Ancient Epic, Faith / Biblical Epic, Adventure, Nature / Wildlife, Verite / Observational, Fantasy / Epic  
**Avoid when:** Do not let the person take all the attention, here they are a measuring stick and not the subject. And remember to settle at the end; a reveal with no rest point is wasted.  
**Prompt:** `figure with a lamp walking into a vast dark hall, camera following, the space revealing itself in the moving pool of light, slow steady move`  
**Yields to:** `doc.site_scale_figure` — When the figure should measure, not lead.  
`doc.reveal_place_through_person`

### Direct Cinema

**Also called:** American observational school  
**What it is:** A documentary school that records reality with no narration, no interviews and no added music, using a handheld camera and synchronous sound, while the filmmaker tries to disappear completely.  
**Effect on the audience:** The viewer feels present in the room and judges for themselves. With no narration the burden of meaning shifts to the audience, and watching becomes active rather than passive.  
**Used for and where it works best:** When the event itself is a complete story: an election campaign, a band on tour, a family crisis, an excavation season. It is the root of all modern observational documentary and emerged in the United States in the nineteen sixties.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, News Report / Reportage, Video Essay, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality | genres: Verite / Observational, Slice of Life, Political Thriller, Family, True Crime, War, Nature / Wildlife, Psychological / Arthouse  
**Avoid when:** It does not suit a subject that needs historical context; with no narration the viewer gets lost. And do not claim it if you staged the scenes, it becomes a stylistic lie.  
**Prompt:** `handheld synchronous-sound observation with no narration and no music, available light, long takes, camera reacting to the event`  
**Yields to:** `doc.expository_mode` — When the subject needs historical context.  
`doc.direct_cinema`

### The Expository Mode

**Also called:** voice of God documentary, narrated documentary  
**What it is:** A film driven by a voice-over that leads the viewer, with the pictures serving and illustrating the words. The classical mode of television and educational documentary, from Bill Nichols's classification.  
**Effect on the audience:** Clarity and authority. The viewer receives ordered information with no effort, and a confident voice carries the feel of official truth.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Any subject that needs context, history and numbers: heritage, science, nature and the long report. Write the narration first and gather the pictures onto it, and make every line deliver something new.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Explainer, News Report / Reportage, Corporate / Training, Video Essay, Kids Content | genres: Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Science / Educational, Nature / Wildlife, Political Thriller, War, Mythology / Ancient Epic, Faith / Biblical Epic  
**Avoid when:** Narration that describes what we can already see is usually waste, because the picture has said it first; the exception is a line naming something the viewer cannot identify, a site, a date, a word. And watch the tone of authority; a lecturing voice pushes a modern audience away.  
**Prompt:** `write the narration first then cut pictures to it, one new fact per line, shots landing on the key words`  
**Yields to:** `doc.observational_mode` — When the subject is alive and unfolding now.  
`doc.expository_mode`

### The Participatory Mode

**Also called:** interactive mode, filmmaker in the film  
**What it is:** The filmmaker enters the film: appears in frame, asks, reacts, and their presence becomes part of the event rather than outside it. Its roots are in French cinema verite, and it is one of Bill Nichols's modes.  
**Effect on the audience:** A different kind of honesty: instead of claiming neutrality, the film admits the camera changed the situation. Viewers trust it more because they can see the terms of the game.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Investigations built on confrontation, the personal journey, and any film where the maker's relationship to the subject is part of the story. In heritage work: a researcher's journey to a site or to their family's origin, with the question driving the film.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Video Essay, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality | genres: Political Thriller, True Crime, Verite / Observational, Psychological / Arthouse, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Adventure, Comedy  
**Avoid when:** Do not step into frame if your presence adds no meaning, the film turns into one about you. And beware turning someone else's story into your own heroics.  
**Prompt:** `filmmaker visible and audible inside the scene, asking and reacting on camera, handheld, natural light and sound`  
**Yields to:** `doc.direct_cinema` — When your presence adds no meaning.  
`doc.participatory_mode`

### The Reflexive Mode

**Also called:** self-aware documentary, showing the apparatus  
**What it is:** The film shows its own apparatus: the camera, the microphone, the crew, the retakes, and questions how truth is constructed instead of serving it finished. One of Bill Nichols's modes.  
**Effect on the audience:** It makes the viewer doubt intelligently instead of swallowing. Declared doubt builds higher trust, because the film is not claiming more than it can prove.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Subjects about memory and contradictory testimony, and films about a history with competing accounts. In heritage work: when the ancient text itself is disputed, show the scholars disagreeing instead of presenting one version as settled.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Video Essay, Experimental / Art Film, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form | genres: Psychological / Arthouse, Political Thriller, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), True Crime, Verite / Observational, Mystery / Whodunit, Science / Educational  
**Avoid when:** Do not use it to show off the craft; exposing the camera with no intellectual reason is decoration. And do not doubt everything, a film that establishes nothing leaves nothing behind.  
**Prompt:** `show the crew and the rig inside the shot, keep the clapper and the retake in the cut, let the making be visible`  
**Yields to:** `doc.expository_mode` — When the film must establish rather than doubt.  
`doc.reflexive_mode`

### The Performative Mode

**Also called:** subjective documentary, first-person documentary  
**What it is:** The film presents an openly subjective experience: the maker in the first person, their memory, family and feelings, with truth offered as lived experience rather than as a report. One of Bill Nichols's modes.  
**Effect on the audience:** It delivers what a statistic cannot. The viewer bonds with one specific person, so a huge subject opens through a small personal door.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Displacement, identity, family memory, the loss of a language or a city. In heritage work: a film about a civilisation told through someone searching for their own roots, so dry history becomes a person the viewer can walk beside.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Video Essay, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Short Film, Experimental / Art Film | genres: Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Tragedy, Psychological / Arthouse, Family, War, Political Thriller, Verite / Observational  
**Avoid when:** Do not shrink a community's story into your own, the collective testimony must stay audible. And do not let emotion replace documentation; a personal claim is not a source.  
**Prompt:** `first-person narration over personal material, home footage and present-day filming intercut, intimate handheld framing`  
**Yields to:** `doc.talking_head` — When the community's testimony must stay audible.  
`doc.performative_mode`

### The Archive Photo Push / Ken Burns Effect

**Also called:** Ken Burns effect, pan and scan on stills  
**What it is:** A slow move inside a still image: a push, a pull or a pan across a detail, sometimes travelling from one face to another inside the same archive frame.  
**Effect on the audience:** It brings a dead image to life. The move walks the eye through the photograph in the order you choose, turning a still into a scene that can be cut against narration without going stiff.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Any historical or heritage film that leans on photographs and documents. Push into exactly the detail the narration names: the eye, the hand, the name on the paper, the broken part of the statue. The technique is associated with director Ken Burns.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Video Essay, Explainer, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Title Sequence / Opening Credits | genres: Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), War, True Crime, Political Thriller, Family, Tragedy, Science / Educational  
**Avoid when:** Do not move every image at the same speed and in the same direction, it turns monotonous. And if the scan is low resolution the push exposes the pixels, so start from a high resolution copy.  
**Prompt:** `slow push into an archival photograph ending on one face, subtle drift, grain preserved, no visible cropping edges`  
**Yields to:** `doc.archive_format_frame` — When the scan is low-res or the frame is the evidence.  
`doc.ken_burns`

### The Archive-to-Now Match

**Also called:** match cut from archive to present, same frame match  
**What it is:** You shoot today's frame from the same angle and the same size as an archival image, then cut or dissolve between them so the street, the building or the carving lines up exactly.  
**Effect on the audience:** It makes time itself visible. The viewer measures what changed and what did not in a single glance, and the passage of centuries arrives with no words.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Historical and heritage documentary: the site photographed in nineteen thirty and the same angle today, or a family in front of a house and that house now. Take the photo to the location and match the angle on your monitor before you roll.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Video Essay, Explainer, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality | genres: Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), War, Political Thriller, Tragedy, Family, Science / Educational, Verite / Observational  
**Avoid when:** If the angle is not an exact match the whole effect dies and the cut just confuses. And mind the lens, a different focal length breaks the match even from the same spot.  
**Prompt:** `dissolve from a black and white archival photograph to a present-day shot framed from the identical angle and focal length, locked off`  
**Yields to:** `doc.then_and_now_dissolve` — When you want nostalgia, not shock.  
`doc.archive_to_now_match`

### The Then-and-Now Dissolve or Morph

**Also called:** time dissolve, past to present morph  
**What it is:** A graduated transition between an old and a current image of the same place, either as a slow dissolve or as a gradual morph that shifts the elements of one into the other.  
**Effect on the audience:** It renders time passing rather than time jumping. Unlike the hard match cut, the dissolve lets both eras exist together for a moment, producing nostalgia instead of shock.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The chapter about nostalgia or loss, and the end of a section about a city that changed. In heritage work: a dissolve from a reconstruction of the site as it stood to its ruined state today, one of the strongest transitions the form has.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Explainer, Video Essay, News Report / Reportage, Title Sequence / Opening Credits | genres: Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Tragedy, War, Mythology / Ancient Epic, Family, Political Thriller, Faith / Biblical Epic  
**Avoid when:** Do not use it for every transition or it becomes a tired television formula. And a slow dissolve between two images that do not share a composition is just visual mud.  
**Prompt:** `slow dissolve from a reconstruction of the intact temple to the same angle on the ruined site today, matched horizon and scale`  
**Yields to:** `doc.archive_to_now_match` — When you want the shock of an exact match.  
`doc.then_and_now_dissolve`

### Text on Archive

**Also called:** annotated archive, highlighted document  
**What it is:** You place text, a line or a circle over an archival image or document: a name, a date, an arrow to one person, a translation of a line, sometimes with the rest of the frame dimmed.  
**Effect on the audience:** It turns a mute image into readable evidence. The viewer sees exactly where to look and what it means, so the document becomes part of the argument rather than decoration.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Investigative reporting, historical documentary and the video essay. In heritage work: isolating a sign inside a relief and translating it, or marking a king's name in cuneiform, so the viewer grasps something they could never read themselves.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Video Essay, Explainer, Corporate / Training, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok) | genres: Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), True Crime, Political Thriller, Science / Educational, Mystery / Whodunit, War  
**Avoid when:** Do not crowd the frame with elements, one per shot. And altering the archive itself is the line you keep: drawing over an image is one thing, changing its content is another.  
**Prompt:** `archival document on screen with the rest dimmed, a clean highlight box and a short translation line appearing beside the marked text`  
**Yields to:** `doc.zoom_circle_detail` — On a moving image rather than a document.  
`doc.text_on_archive`

### Split Screen, Archive Versus Present

**Also called:** side-by-side archive, comparison split  
**What it is:** The screen divides in two: archive on one side, the present on the other from the same angle, both playing at once instead of transitioning between them.  
**Effect on the audience:** The comparison becomes instant and continuous. The viewer's eye travels between the two eras and finds the differences alone, so the conclusion feels like theirs rather than the narrator's.  
**Used for and where it works best:** When the difference is detailed and needs time to read: a building facade, a carved wall before and after looting, an old and a new stadium. In vertical, split top and bottom rather than left and right.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Video Essay, Explainer, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Sports Broadcast / Sports Film | genres: Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Political Thriller, Science / Educational, War, True Crime, Sports Drama  
**Avoid when:** Do not split two images that do not share a composition, the comparison means nothing. And do not hold it long; after five seconds the eye is full and wants something new.  
**Prompt:** `split screen with the archival frame on the left and the present-day frame on the right, identical angle and scale, both static`  
**Yields to:** `doc.side_by_side_comparison` — When the two sources are not archive and present.  
`doc.split_screen_archive`

### Respecting the Archive Frame

**Also called:** pillarbox archive, keeping the original ratio  
**What it is:** You present archive at its original ratio with its grain and flaws intact, held inside a black frame or a simple surround, instead of blowing it up and cropping it to fill the screen.  
**Effect on the audience:** It announces to the viewer that this is real old material, not a processed version. The frame itself becomes a marker of period, and the visual difference gives clarity when moving between eras.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Any documentary mixing old archive with modern photography. Leave a four by three frame inside black, or place a blurred enlargement of the same image behind it for vertical. In heritage work, historical excavation photographs keep their own frame so they read as documents.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Video Essay, Explainer, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Vlog / YouTube Long-Form | genres: Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), War, Political Thriller, True Crime, Family, Science / Educational  
**Avoid when:** Do not upscale archive to fill the frame and lose its edges, the important information is often right there. And do not clean off the grain, you erase the evidence of age.  
**Prompt:** `archival footage presented in its original four by three ratio inside a black surround, grain and scratches preserved, no crop`  
**Yields to:** `doc.ken_burns` — When a still must move against narration.  
`doc.archive_format_frame`

### The Animated Map

**What it is:** A map that moves on screen: a point appearing, a line drawing between two cities, a border filling with colour, or a zoom from the globe down to one location.  
**Effect on the audience:** It solves geography in a single beat. The viewer grasps where events sit and how they connect, and the movement makes dry information followable.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Every historical, war or investigative documentary with more than one location. In heritage work: a map of Mesopotamia with the two rivers and the city sites, and a line tracking a king's road or the spread of an empire. Tie it to the year named in the narration.

`doc.map_animation`

### The Lower Third

**Also called:** name strap, chyron, banner  
**What it is:** A graphic in the bottom third of the screen carrying information about who or what is on camera: name, role, place, time, held for a few seconds then removed.  
**Effect on the audience:** It delivers credibility and context without stopping the film. The viewer takes information with the eye while the ear is busy, so facts compress without crowding.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The first appearance of every contributor in a documentary or report, and every move to a new location. Keep it simple: name on one line, role on the second. In heritage work: the site name and its approximate date on its first establishing shot.  
**Best in:** formats: News Report / Reportage, Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Corporate / Training, Explainer, Interview / Video Podcast, Video Essay | genres: Verite / Observational, Political Thriller, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Science / Educational, True Crime, Nature / Wildlife, Sports Drama  
**Avoid when:** Do not lay it over a face or an important detail, and do not hold it past five seconds. In vertical, lift it above the zone the platform interface covers.  
**Prompt:** `clean two-line lower third animating in from the left, name above and role below, held four seconds, no drop shadow clutter`  
**Yields to:** `doc.name_super` — When the introduction should carry cinematic weight.  
`doc.lower_third`

### The Name Super

**Also called:** full-screen name card, title super  
**What it is:** The person's or the place's name is set large over the image itself rather than in a strap, sometimes centre frame or in negative space left there deliberately.  
**Effect on the audience:** It gives cinematic weight and turns an introduction into a stylistic beat. A large name announces that this person matters to the film, not that they are a passing voice.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Introducing the principal characters in a documentary feature or series, and opening a chapter with a place name. Frame with space for the type before you shoot. In heritage work: the ancient city's name set large over the aerial of the site.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, Trailer / Teaser, Title Sequence / Opening Credits, Video Essay, Music Video, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok) | genres: Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), True Crime, Political Thriller, War, Mythology / Ancient Epic, Sports Drama, Adventure  
**Avoid when:** Do not use it for every speaker, it loses meaning and starts to read as advertising. And do not lay the type over a busy area, large text needs real empty space.  
**Prompt:** `large clean name typography over a wide shot with deliberate negative space, type fading in, image continuing to move behind it`  
**Yields to:** `doc.lower_third` — For a passing voice, use the strap.  
`doc.name_super`

### The Stats Card

**Also called:** figure card, data super, number graphic  
**What it is:** A number or a statistic presented alone on screen or over a calm shot, in large type with a word or two of explanation and a small source line underneath.  
**Effect on the audience:** It stops the rhythm and fixes the figure in memory. A viewer forgets a spoken number but remembers one they saw large and in silence.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The number the whole argument rests on: the casualties, the artefacts looted, the area, the age of the site in years. Always carry a small source line, and give it its own beat rather than burying it in speech.  
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Heritage / Historical Documentary, News Report / Reportage, Explainer, Video Essay, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Corporate / Training | genres: Political Thriller, Science / Educational, War, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), True Crime, Nature / Wildlife  
**Avoid when:** Do not show more than one figure per shot, and on a piece presented as fact tie every number on screen to where it came from — the brief, the skill or a search — because a figure nobody can check is the first thing a critic pulls. And a number with no comparison evaporates, tie it to something the viewer can picture.  
**Prompt:** `single large number on a dark background with a two-word label and a small source line, held four seconds, silence underneath`

### Golden-Hour Heritage Establishing

**Also called:** low sun on ruins, magic hour site shot  
**What it is:** Shooting the site in the hour after sunrise or before sunset, when the sun is very low, so shadows stretch, the stone turns gold and every surface texture appears.  
**Effect on the audience:** It gives dignity and permanence. Low light makes ruins feel alive and warm instead of dead stone, and removes the midday harshness that flattens everything.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Every establishing shot in heritage documentary. Shoot the same site at sunrise and at sunset so you own two shadow directions. And keep modern markers out of frame so the shot stays timeless and reusable in any cut.

**Avoid when:** Do not shoot everything golden, a film entirely in warm light turns into a tourism advert. And remember the light changes every five minutes, so order your shots before you reach the site.

**Used for and where it works best:** All museum filming, and usually the only chance at objects that never leave display. Drape black cloth around the lens, rotate the polariser until the reflection dies, and work at about thirty degrees to the glass.

### Raking Light on Relief Carvings

**Used for and where it works best:** The core tool for filming Assyrian and Babylonian reliefs and any wall carving. Use the low sun at dawn or dusk, or a single artificial source from the side at less than fifteen degrees, and drift the source slowly so the carving breathes.

### The Ancient Site Drone Pattern (Orbit, Reveal, Top-Down)

**Also called:** three-move aerial set, site aerial package  
**What it is:** Three fixed aerial moves for every site: an orbit around the monument, a slow rise revealing the surroundings, and a vertical top-down over the plan. All shot in one session under the same light.  
**Effect on the audience:** It gives the viewer the site in every dimension: the form, the context and the plan. Three shots are enough to teach a place the viewer has never visited.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Every site that appears in a heritage documentary should own all three. Use the rise to open a chapter and the top-down when the narration explains a city or temple plan. Shoot them in low light.  
**On the first sight of a site, the orbit is one of two options and neither is the rule.** The other
is `doc.reveal_walk_into_site`, which gives scale from human height. Which one the shot wants depends
on the place: a site with a threshold to cross — a temple hall, a doorway, a corridor into a chamber
— usually earns more from being walked into, because human scale is the thing an aerial cannot
recover later. An open ruin, a tell, a city plain has nothing to walk into and the orbit carries it.
Decide per site, and record which you chose and why.  
**Best in:** formats: Heritage / Historical Documentary, Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Trailer / Teaser, News Report / Reportage, Video Essay, AI-Generated Clip | genres: Heritage (civilisation-focused), Historical / Biopic, Mythology / Ancient Epic, War, Nature / Wildlife, Adventure, Science / Educational  
**Avoid when:** Do not use all three back to back, spread them through the film.  
**Prompt:** `three aerial moves over an ancient site, slow orbit around the main structure, gentle rise revealing the plain, and a vertical top-down over the plan, low sun`  
**Yields to:** `doc.reveal_walk_into_site` — A site with a threshold earns being walked into.  
`doc.site_drone_pattern`

**Also called:** VO SOT NAT structure, package build

### The Live Shot

**Also called:** live cross, live hit, remote live  
**What it is:** The reporter speaks live on air from the scene, usually in a static medium shot with the event or its location behind them, sometimes moving to show something during the broadcast.

**Used for and where it works best:** The first five seconds of any vlog, video essay or explainer. Write the line as a promise or an unresolvable question: what happened, what you found, why this is wrong. In vertical it is the single line that decides whether the viewer stays or scrolls.  
**Best in:** formats: Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Video Essay, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Explainer, Corporate / Training, News Report / Reportage, Documentary Feature, Kids Content | genres: Comedy, Science / Educational, Political Thriller, Verite / Observational, True Crime, Slice of Life, Psychological / Arthouse  
**Avoid when:** Do not open with a greeting and an introduction, you lose half the audience in three seconds. And do not promise something the video never delivers.  
**Prompt:** `open on a tight medium shot talking straight to the lens, one sentence stating the question, hard cut into the first B-roll`  
**Yields to:** `doc.doc_cold_open` — When the material beats a stated promise.  
`doc.talk_to_camera_opener`

### The Jump-Cut Monologue

**Also called:** YouTube jump-cut delivery, tightened monologue  
**What it is:** You speak to camera in one setup and the edit removes every pause, hesitation and breath, so visible jumps appear while the speech runs unbroken. Unlike the jump cut inside an interview, this is a whole delivery style rather than a way to shorten an answer.  
**Effect on the audience:** It gives density and pace. Information compresses, energy rises, and the jumps themselves have become a recognised language associated with informality and honesty in platform content.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Every vlog, explainer and short essay. Take the best read of each sentence and chain them, and keep your movement inside the frame small so the jumps do not feel violent. In vertical this method lets you deliver twice the words in the same time.  
**Best in:** formats: Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Video Essay, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Explainer, Corporate / Training, Meme / Comedy Edit, Kids Content, News Report / Reportage | genres: Comedy, Science / Educational, Political Thriller, Slice of Life, Psychological / Arthouse, Verite / Observational, True Crime  
**Avoid when:** Do not strip every breath, speech with no pause exhausts the ear and sounds robotic. Leave a short beat before the important line so it can land.  
**Prompt:** `single locked medium shot of one person talking, every pause removed so visible jump cuts appear, matching frame and light throughout`  
**Yields to:** `doc.let_it_breathe` — Before the line that must land.  
`doc.jump_cut_monologue`

### The B-Roll Insert on the Noun

**Also called:** flash insert, quick cutaway gag, noun insert  
**What it is:** A very short shot, half a second to a second, dropped over the speech exactly on one word: a photo, an archive clip, a meme, a drawing, then straight back to the face. Unlike sustained sync to narration, this is one flash and not continuous cover.  
**Effect on the audience:** It converts a word into an image on the instant, adding rhythm, humour and energy. In serious content it anchors a fact immediately instead of leaving it abstract.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Vlogs, video essays and explainers. Whenever you name a place, a person, a product or a number, flash it on the word and return to the face. The rule: never longer than a second, and the voice keeps running underneath without a break.  
**Best in:** formats: Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Video Essay, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Explainer, Meme / Comedy Edit, News Report / Reportage, Corporate / Training, Kids Content | genres: Comedy, Science / Educational, Political Thriller, Slice of Life, True Crime, Psychological / Arthouse, Verite / Observational  
**Avoid when:** Do not insert on every word, the video turns cluttered and exhausting. And do not use an image that does not match the word, a random insert distracts instead of clarifying.  
**Prompt:** `flash a half-second image over the talking head exactly on the noun, voice continuing underneath, hard cut back to the face`  
**Yields to:** `doc.broll_cover_vo` — When the picture must cover continuously, not flash.  
`doc.broll_on_the_noun`

### The Day Timeline Structure

**Also called:** hour-by-hour vlog, chronological vlog build  
**What it is:** You build the video in the order of the hours: morning, the journey, the event, the evening, marking the transitions with a time stamp or a light cue that shows the hour.  
**Effect on the audience:** It gives the viewer a clear sense of progress so they stay. Knowing the day will end in something functions as an implicit promise that pulls them toward the finish.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Travel vlogs, the day in the life and field coverage. Shoot a transition shot for every stage, a light change, a clock, a meal, a place, and lay the time stamp on it. And keep the strongest event of the day in the final third.  
**Best in:** formats: Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Travel / Real Estate / Hospitality, Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Video Essay, Corporate / Training, Wedding / Event Film | genres: Slice of Life, Verite / Observational, Comedy, Family, Adventure, Nature / Wildlife, Heritage (civilisation-focused)  
**Avoid when:** Do not present the day literally if half of it is dull, cut without mercy. And do not use more than five time stamps, too many stall the rhythm.  
**Prompt:** `chronological vlog structure with a transition shot and a time stamp for each stage of the day, matching light progression`  
**Yields to:** `doc.day_in_the_life` — In documentary form, let light mark the hours.  
`doc.day_timeline`

### The Screen Clip, Freeze and Annotate

**Also called:** freeze and mark, pause and point  
**What it is:** You play a clip or a screen, freeze it at the critical frame and mark it up: a circle, an arrow, a text label, or a dim over everything except the relevant area.  
**Effect on the audience:** It turns the viewer into an analyst. The freeze gives time to read and the markup says exactly where the evidence is, so the argument becomes checkable rather than asserted.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The video essay, sports analysis, film criticism and debunking. Freeze on the frame that carries the proof, add a single graphic element, clear it after two seconds and resume.  
**Best in:** formats: Video Essay, Explainer, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, News Report / Reportage, Documentary Feature, Sports Broadcast / Sports Film, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Corporate / Training | genres: Science / Educational, Political Thriller, True Crime, Mystery / Whodunit, Sports Drama, Comedy, Psychological / Arthouse  
**Avoid when:** Do not put more than one mark on a frame, the screen crowds and the evidence gets lost. And do not freeze longer than three seconds, the viewer feels the video has stalled.  
**Prompt:** `freeze the clip on the key frame, dim everything except one area, draw a single clean circle, hold two seconds, resume playback`  
**Yields to:** `doc.zoom_circle_detail` — When a still only needs a punch-in.  
`doc.screen_clip_freeze_annotate`

### The Zoom and Circle on a Detail

**Also called:** punch in and circle, highlight zoom  
**What it is:** You punch in digitally on part of an image or screen and put a circle or a box around it, sometimes dimming the rest, with the move made in the edit and not in camera.  
**Effect on the audience:** It tells the viewer exactly where to look in one beat. The push conveys discovery and the circle conveys investigation.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The video essay, image analysis and fact checking. Use it when the detail is small and decisive: a signature, a number, a face in the background, a discrepancy in a carving. The rule: one push, one circle, one point.  
**Best in:** formats: Video Essay, Explainer, News Report / Reportage, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Documentary Feature, Corporate / Training, Sports Broadcast / Sports Film | genres: Mystery / Whodunit, True Crime, Political Thriller, Science / Educational, Comedy, Psychological / Arthouse, Historical / Biopic  
**Avoid when:** Watch the resolution, over-enlarging exposes the pixels and weakens the evidence. And do not push into something the viewer still cannot make out afterwards.  
**Prompt:** `digital punch in on one corner of the image with a clean circle appearing around the detail, rest of the frame dimmed, held three seconds`  
**Yields to:** `doc.text_on_archive` — When enlarging would expose the pixels; label it.  
`doc.zoom_circle_detail`

### The Text Overlay Thesis

**Also called:** thesis card, argument text, statement on screen  
**What it is:** The central claim the video defends is set on screen in clear type, over a calm image or a plain background, and held for several seconds.  
**Effect on the audience:** It turns speech into a declared position. The viewer reads the line and retains it, and text carries more commitment than voice because writing feels final.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Early in a video essay after the hook, at the end of a section as a summary, and before the part that proves the case. Write it in the shortest possible form, one or two lines, and read it aloud at the same moment.  
**Best in:** formats: Video Essay, Explainer, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Documentary Feature, Corporate / Training, News Report / Reportage, Title Sequence / Opening Credits | genres: Political Thriller, Science / Educational, Psychological / Arthouse, True Crime, Historical / Biopic, Comedy, Verite / Observational  
**Avoid when:** Do not put a whole paragraph, long text goes unread and becomes decoration. And do not lay it over a busy moving image, a crowded background kills legibility.  
**Prompt:** `single clean sentence in large type over a slow static image, held five seconds, voice reading the same line underneath`  
**Yields to:** `doc.kinetic_quote` — When the line is somebody's words and needs attribution.  
`doc.text_overlay_thesis`

### The Chapter Card

**Also called:** section title card, part card  
**What it is:** A text card dividing the sections of the video: a number and a section name, usually on a plain background or a still frame, with silence or a short sound.  
**Effect on the audience:** It hands the viewer a map and renews their patience. Seeing part two of four tells them the video is organised and the end is reachable.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The long video essay, platform documentary and layered explainers. Name the chapter with an intriguing phrase rather than a dry heading, and place the card where the idea changes, never in the middle of an argument.  
**Best in:** formats: Video Essay, Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Explainer, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Corporate / Training, Title Sequence / Opening Credits, Kids Content | genres: Science / Educational, Political Thriller, Historical / Biopic, True Crime, Psychological / Arthouse, Mystery / Whodunit, Heritage (civilisation-focused)  
**Avoid when:** Do not drop a card every minute, over-chaptering breaks the flow. And do not hold it past two seconds, a card does not need a full performance.  
**Prompt:** `minimal chapter card, part number and a short title in clean type on black, held two seconds, single low sound`  
**Yields to:** `doc.landscape_breather` — When the section break should be felt, not labelled.  
`doc.chapter_card`

### The Receipt / On-Screen Evidence

**Also called:** showing the receipts, citation on screen  
**What it is:** At the exact moment you state a fact, its source appears on screen: a crop of the document, the quote in its original context, the source reference, the publication date. Unlike screen recording, this is a sourcing rule rather than a capture method.  
**Effect on the audience:** It removes doubt and raises the speaker's authority. The viewer does not have to trust you, they can see it, so the conversation shifts from trust to evidence.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The video essay, fact checking, true crime and any claim made against a person or an institution. Keep the source legible at a usable size for at least two seconds. In heritage work: the study or the museum the information or the object comes from.  
**Best in:** formats: Video Essay, News Report / Reportage, Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Explainer, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Heritage / Historical Documentary | genres: Political Thriller, True Crime, Science / Educational, Mystery / Whodunit, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused), Psychological / Arthouse  
**Avoid when:** Do not show a source too small to read, unreadable proof is decoration and deception. And do not clip a quote out of context, a truncated citation gets exposed and costs you your credibility.  
**Prompt:** `crop of the source document appearing beside the speaker at the moment of the claim, readable type, source and date labelled, held three seconds`  
**Yields to:** `doc.screen_recording` — When the source must be seen being opened.

### The Sit-Down Reaction

**Also called:** reaction video, watch-along  
**What it is:** You are filmed sitting and watching material, a clip, a match, a song, while commenting on it, and the material is shown to the viewer alongside your face or in alternation.  
**Effect on the audience:** It provides the pleasure of watching together. The viewer sees the material and a human reaction to it at the same moment, so the experience becomes social rather than solitary.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Content criticism, sports analysis and reviews. Keep the camera at eye height and close, and record the first reaction because it never repeats.  
**Best in:** formats: Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Video Essay, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Sports Broadcast / Sports Film, Explainer, Meme / Comedy Edit, News Report / Reportage | genres: Comedy, Sports Drama, Science / Educational, Psychological / Arthouse, Slice of Life, Musical, Verite / Observational  
**Avoid when:** Do not show the material in full, take only the passages you actually respond to. And if your face covers the material the viewer loses both.  
**Prompt:** `seated reaction shot at eye height with the watched clip in the corner of the frame, cutting between full clip and full face`  
**Yields to:** `doc.reaction_pip` — When the material must stay full screen.

### The Reaction Picture-in-Picture

**Also called:** face cam, webcam corner, PiP reaction  
**What it is:** A small image of the speaker's face sits in a corner of the screen over the displayed material, so the full material and the reaction are visible at once.  
**Effect on the audience:** It keeps the human presence without costing the viewer the material. A small face is enough to carry the reaction, which solves the choice between the two.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Gaming analysis, screen walkthroughs, clip reviews and sports commentary. Place the window in a corner with no information in it, shrink it when the material matters and enlarge it when the commentary is the point.

### The Outro End Card

**Also called:** end screen, subscribe card, next video card  
**What it is:** The last ten to twenty seconds carry a card with the next video or a call to follow, over a calm shot or one that sums up the subject. It differs from the advertising End Card, a short static frame with the logo and the action information: this one is a long card whose job is to move the viewer to the next video.  
**Effect on the audience:** It converts a single view into a series. The viewer who reached the end is the most likely to continue, which makes this the most valuable real estate in the video.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The end of any long-form platform video. Tie it to a line that opens the next video rather than a closing farewell. And keep the picture behind it still so it does not compete with the card.  
**Best in:** formats: Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Video Essay, Explainer, Corporate / Training, Documentary Feature, Documentary Series, Kids Content, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok) | genres: Science / Educational, Comedy, Political Thriller, Slice of Life, True Crime, Historical / Biopic, Heritage (civilisation-focused)  
**Avoid when:** Do not stretch it past twenty seconds, the viewer leaves before the end. And do not lay the card over a shot carrying movement or information.  
**Prompt:** `end card with the next video thumbnail on a calm static shot, held twelve seconds, music resolving underneath`  
**Yields to:** `doc.where_are_they_now` — When the ending must state outcomes, not sell the next video.  
`doc.outro_endcard`

### The Montage as Argument

**Also called:** evidence montage, argument by juxtaposition  
**What it is:** You gather a run of short clips from different sources saying the same thing or contradicting each other and cut them back to back at speed, so the repetition or the contradiction becomes the argument.  
**Effect on the audience:** It proves a pattern without asserting one. The viewer concludes for themselves that this recurs or that this person contradicts themselves, so the persuasion is internal.  
**Used for and where it works best:** The political, media and critical video essay. Order the clips from weakest to strongest, keep each to one or two seconds, and label every one with source and date. End on the longest clip because that is the one that settles in memory.  
**Best in:** formats: Video Essay, News Report / Reportage, Documentary Feature, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Explainer, Vlog / YouTube Long-Form, Meme / Comedy Edit | genres: Political Thriller, Comedy, Science / Educational, True Crime, Psychological / Arthouse, Mystery / Whodunit, Historical / Biopic  
**Avoid when:** Do not cut the clips in a way that changes their meaning, a misleading trim always surfaces. And do not extend the montage, after ten clips the point is made.  
**Prompt:** `rapid montage of ten one-second clips from different sources saying the same phrase, each labelled with source and date, ending on the longest`  
**Yields to:** `doc.side_by_side_comparison` — When two sources contradict and can run together.  
`doc.essay_montage_argument`

### The Map Zoom Locator

**Also called:** satellite zoom, earth zoom to location

### The Timeline Scrub

**Also called:** scrubbing the footage, playhead as evidence  
**What it is:** You show the video player itself with its timeline and move the playhead back and forth, stopping on the critical frame, so the exact time of the event is visible on screen.  
**Effect on the audience:** It gives the feel of live investigation. The viewer watches you work on the material rather than receive a conclusion, and trusts that nothing is hidden between frames.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Clip verification, sports analysis and exposing a manipulated edit. Scrub back over the same moment twice and freeze on the third pass, keeping the timecode visible throughout.  
**Best in:** formats: Video Essay, News Report / Reportage, Sports Broadcast / Sports Film, Documentary Feature, Explainer, Vertical Short-Form (Reels, Shorts, TikTok), Vlog / YouTube Long-Form | genres: True Crime, Political Thriller, Science / Educational, Mystery / Whodunit, Sports Drama, Psychological / Arthouse, Comedy

**Prompt:** `video player on screen with the playhead scrubbing back over the same two seconds twice then freezing on the key frame, timecode visible`  
**Yields to:** `doc.screen_clip_freeze_annotate` — When the frame needs marking, not locating.  
`doc.timeline_scrub`

### The Arm's-Length Selfie Walk

**Also called:** selfie walking shot, handheld self-shot  
**What it is:** You hold the camera at arm's length while walking and shoot yourself in a close frame, with the location crossing and changing behind you at every step.  
**Effect on the audience:** It carries personal presence and place in one shot. The closeness makes the talk intimate, like a message to a friend, and the moving background keeps boredom away.  
**Used for and where it works best:** Travel and daily vlogs: arriving somewhere, the first reaction, bridging two scenes. Keep the arm fully extended on a wide lens so the background enters, and record on a close mic because wind destroys this sound.
