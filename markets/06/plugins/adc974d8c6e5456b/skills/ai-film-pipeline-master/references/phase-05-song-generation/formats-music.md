# Formats — music and song

Entry point into the database. Each kit lists technique ids; open the matching `cat-*.md` for the full card.

Music formats break one assumption the rest of this database rests on: **the running time is fixed before the writing starts.** A song is 3:12 whether or not the story fits, and a music video is the length of the track. Everything here follows from that. **Every figure in a kit below — a length, a count, a rate, a fee — is a default or a reference, never an argument with the brief:** the brief's own length, format, aspect and count win, and the kit's craft is fitted to them.

## Music Video — Narrative

**Constraints:** Length is the track length, non-negotiable, typically 2:30 to 4:30. The audio already exists and cannot be re-cut for story. The artist must appear, usually within the first thirty seconds, and their appearance is contractual rather than dramatic. Dialogue is unavailable — everything is carried by image, performance and lyric. Budget is small relative to the runtime, and locations are few. The audience may have heard the song a hundred times or never.

**Grammar:** The story is cut to musical structure, not to dramatic structure — the turn lands on the chorus, the reversal on the bridge, the resolution on the final chorus or the outro. Because the audience cannot hear dialogue, every beat must be legible as a single image. Compression is extreme: a narrative that would take forty minutes is told in nine shots. Repetition in the music permits repetition in the picture, which is why the same image returning changed is the format's strongest structural device. The lyric and the picture should not say the same thing at the same time; the gap between them is where meaning lives.

**Palette:** 6–12 story beats · turn on the chorus · 2–4 locations · no dialogue · one image per beat · a returning image

**Rules:** Map the song's structure before writing a frame of story | Put the turn on the chorus, not where the story wants it | One idea per beat, legible as a single image | Never illustrate the lyric literally — set the picture against it *(narrative only; a teaching song inverts this — see below)* | Get the artist on screen early and get them out of the way | Build the whole thing so the last chorus can pay an image planted in the first verse | Assume the audience is not listening to the words *(narrative only; in a teaching song the words are the point)*

**If the song teaches something, you are in the wrong kit.** The two rules above that say *never illustrate the lyric literally* and *assume the audience is not listening to the words* are written for an audience that already knows the words. A song that teaches colours, numbers, letters or days inverts both: the literal illustration is the teaching, and the words are the entire point. Go to [Music Video — Teaching Song](#music-video-teaching-song) instead. Everything else in this kit — cutting to musical structure, the turn on the chorus, the returning image — still applies there.

**Examples:** narrative promos with a full story arc; performance-plus-story hybrids; single-location conceit videos

**Avoid:** Illustrating the lyric line by line, which is the most common and most deadening failure. A story with more beats than the track has sections. Dialogue scenes with no dialogue, which read as mime. Ending on the artist's face because the track ended.

## Music Video — Teaching Song

**This kit exists because the narrative kit above is exactly backwards for it.** *Music Video — Narrative* rules that you must never illustrate the lyric literally and that you should assume the audience is not listening to the words. For a song whose job is to teach colours, numbers, letters or days, the literal illustration **is** the teaching and the words are the entire point. Those two rules invert here. Everything else about working to a fixed track still holds.

**Constraints:** Length is the track length, as in every music format, and the track is short — a colour, counting or alphabet song usually runs 1:30 to 3:30, and is often built to loop. The audience is roughly two to six years old (where the brief's band straddles the tempo card's two bands, take the younger band's tempo) and cannot read, so the picture and the sung word are the only two channels there are. **The item list is fixed and ordered by the curriculum, not by the drama** — seven colours, ten numbers, twenty-eight letters, seven days — and it cannot be reordered for effect the way a story's beats can. The piece will be watched dozens of times by the same child, which changes what "boring" means: repetition is the product. Cutting has a hard floor at 1.5 seconds (`kids.no_fast_cuts`), and that floor is a comprehension rule rather than a taste, so it outranks any beat multiple that would break it. And the deliverable is external to the video: a child who cannot name the thing afterwards has watched something that failed, however beautiful it was. **When the item list, the ladder and the brief's runtime cannot all fit**, the brief's runtime and item list hold, and the song gives way in this order: fewer chorus returns first, then the answer silence down to its card's floor, then a faster recap; record what gave under `decisions`.

**Grammar:** The picture states the word. When the lyric sings *red*, the frame holds one red thing, alone, on a clean background, on the beat the word lands — not a beat later, and not beside a second object competing for the same attention. An arrival move — a train pulling in, a door opening — finishes before that downbeat, so the item is already alone in frame when the word is sung. That is the whole inversion: the narrative kit sets picture against lyric because the gap between them is where meaning lives for an audience that already knows the words, and a child learning the word has no gap to read, only a mismatch. So close the gap deliberately, every time, and put the craft somewhere else — into how the item arrives, what it does, and what it turns into. One item per section, never two. Each item gets three channels in order — sung, shown, then asked for, with the silence after the ask left unfilled — which is the repetition ladder doing the teaching. On a generated track that silence is made in the edit where the engine will not leave it: Suno documents no silent bar inside a lyric (`suno.instrumental_solo_tags`), so write the bar as an instrumental line to audition, and thin or cut it in the Song Editor or the edit where the take fills it. The chorus returns identically because the child is learning it rather than being entertained by it, and the variation an adult format would put into the chorus goes into the verses instead. Build to a recap: the last section names every item again in sequence, which is this format's equivalent of a final image and the place where the teaching is actually banked.

**Palette:** 1 item per section · 3 channels per item (sung, shown, asked) · a 1.5 s cut floor · 2–4 s average shot length · 1 new word per segment · an identical chorus · a full recap before the end · one fixed section order

**Rules:** Illustrate the lyric literally, on the beat the word is sung | One taught item per section; two in a frame teaches neither | Show the item alone on a clean background at the moment it is named | Sing it, show it, then ask for it — and do not fill the silence | Let the curriculum own the order, never the drama | Keep the chorus identical every time and put the variation in the verses | Hold the 1.5-second cut floor even when the beat is faster | Recap every item before the end | Write for the fortieth viewing, not the first | End on the child, not on a brand card

**Examples:** colour songs, counting and number songs, alphabet songs in any script, days-of-the-week and months songs, shapes, animal sounds, body parts, greetings and manners songs

**Avoid:** Metaphor, which is this format's defining failure — a colour song where red also means anger has taught nothing about red. The picture arriving a beat behind the word, which splits the child's attention at the only moment that matters. Ironic or contrapuntal imagery: the gap the narrative kit lives on is, here, simply a mismatch. More items than the song has sections, which produces a list nobody can hold. Cutting to a fast beat through the floor. A busy or textured background behind the item being named. Two subjects taught in one song. And the logo ending, which spends the recap slot on the one viewer who is not watching.

## Music Video — Concept and Performance

**Constraints:** A fixed runtime set by the track — the song's length is the film's length and it cannot be trimmed — and, unlike the narrative music video above, no story to carry it. The video runs on expression, texture, repetition and idea rather than on plot, so there is no value turn to build toward. Performance videos must sustain interest on the artist alone; concept videos must sustain it on a single idea stretched to three minutes.

**Grammar:** Development without conflict. The piece introduces a premise, deepens it, turns it, and reconciles — the four-part shape rather than the three-act one. Interest is maintained by escalation of the idea itself, not by stakes: each section must do more of the thing, or do it differently, and the audience's question is *how far will this go* rather than *what happens next*. The turn, when it comes, reframes everything before it.

**Palette:** 1 premise · 3–5 escalations · 1 reframe · no plot · no dialogue

**Rules:** Choose one idea and refuse the second | Escalate the idea, not the stakes | Place the reframe on the bridge | Never let a section repeat the previous one without adding something | If the concept can be understood in the first ten seconds, it must be able to surprise at ninety | End on the idea's furthest point, not on the artist

**Examples:** single-take conceits, one-location transformations, colour or object escalations, choreography-led pieces, tableau and portrait videos

**Avoid:** Two concepts. A concept that peaks at thirty seconds and then repeats. Cutting to the artist whenever the idea runs out, which the audience reads correctly as running out.

## Lyric Video

**Constraints:** The lyric is fixed and must be legible at the pace it is sung, on a phone, often muted-then-unmuted. Typography is the entire visual vocabulary. Budget is usually the lowest of any music format, and turnaround is short — frequently released with the single.

**Grammar:** Reading rate is the constraint that governs everything: a line must be on screen long enough to read before the next arrives, which means dense or fast lyrics must break across the screen rather than appear whole. Emphasis is created by isolating a word, changing its scale, or letting it persist after the rest has gone. The video's only structural decisions are which words get weight and which lines get a break — and those decisions are the interpretation.

**Palette:** 1 typeface family · 2–3 emphasis devices · a break on every section change · one word held per chorus

**Rules:** Set the reading rate first and design to it | Never put more words on screen than can be read before the next line | Break the line where the singer breathes, not where the grammar does | Isolate one word per chorus and hold it | Change something at every section boundary | Do not animate every line — variation only reads against stillness | Check it silent, because half the audience will watch it that way

**Examples:** typographic lyric videos, kinetic type pieces, handwritten and analogue lyric treatments

**Avoid:** Animating every word, which flattens emphasis to zero. Breaking lines on grammar instead of breath. A typeface that cannot hold the longest line in the song. Ending on a logo rather than on the last line.

## Song Lyric

**Constraints:** Sung, not read. Every line must be singable at the melody's stress pattern, and a line that scans on the page but fights the melody is unusable. The listener has one pass, no rewind, and is often doing something else. Rhyme and repetition are memory aids, not decoration. A chorus will be heard four or five times in three minutes and must survive that.

**Grammar:** Prosody governs everything: stressed syllables must land on strong beats, and a mismatch is audible even to listeners who cannot name it. The title line is the hook and belongs where the music is strongest, usually the chorus's first or last line. Verses carry specific detail; the chorus carries the claim. The bridge exists to say the thing the verses could not — it is the one section allowed to change perspective. Concrete images outlast abstractions across repeated listening.

**Palette:** 2–3 verses · 1 chorus heard 3–5 times · 1 bridge · title line placed once per chorus · concrete nouns over abstractions

**Rules:** Match stress to melody before worrying about meaning | Put the title line where the melody peaks | Verses are specific, the chorus is the claim | Write a chorus that survives five hearings | Give the bridge the thing the verses cannot say | Prefer a concrete noun to an abstract one every time | Never force a rhyme — change the line, not the word | Sing it aloud; the page lies

**Examples:** narrative songs, anthems, laments, list songs, character songs in musical theatre

**Avoid:** The filler line that exists to reach the rhyme. A chorus that states nothing. Abstractions in the verse, which have nowhere to go on a second listen. Writing to the page and discovering at the session that it cannot be sung.

## Concert and Performance Film

**Constraints:** The running order is the structure and it was set by someone else, usually months before, for reasons that have nothing to do with a film. Length is the show's length minus what can be cut — most concert films land between 85 and 120 minutes, a stadium film longer. The performance happened once, or three times, and coverage is whatever the camera plan caught; there is no reshoot and there is no second take of the moment the singer's voice broke. Audio is a multitrack mix made after the fact, which means the film's emotional grammar is being decided in an audio suite as much as in an edit. Where the brief raises rights, each song's publishing is its own question; this skill runs no clearance step (root `SKILL.md` §0). The writer's artefact is almost never a script — it is a running order, an interview plan, a structure document and a set of decisions about what the film is claiming about this artist.

**Grammar:** A concert film is not coverage of a concert, it is **an argument about what the performance means**, and the only place to make it is in what is placed between and around the songs. That is the entire writing surface: what comes before the first note, what the film cuts away to and when, whether the audience is a presence or a subject, and what happens after the last chord. The show's own arc is a ritual arc — arrival, offering, communion, release — rather than a dramatic one, and fighting it produces a film that feels edited against its own music. Withhold something: the wide shot, the crowd, the artist's face, the reverse of the room. A film that shows everything in the first song has nothing to give in the encore. Interview and backstage material buys context and costs momentum, so it is placed where the show's own energy is lowest, not where the story would like it. And the final image carries the claim — the thing the film has been arguing is what the audience is left standing in front of.

**Palette:** 85–120 minutes · 16–24 songs, minus cuts · 1 claim about this artist · 1 withheld reveal, spent late · cutaway material placed at the show's own troughs · a first note delayed · a held final beat

**Rules:** Find the claim the film is making before deciding what to cut | Work with the show's ritual arc instead of imposing a dramatic one | Withhold one thing — the wide, the crowd, the face — and spend it once | Delay the first note longer than feels comfortable | Put the interview and backstage material at the show's energy troughs | Treat the audience as a character or as furniture, and decide which before the first cut | Cut a song for shape rather than for quality, and never for both reasons at once | Clear the setlist before you edit to it | Let one performance run whole and unbroken somewhere in the film | Hold the last beat past the applause

**Examples:** *Stop Making Sense*; *The Last Waltz*; *Sign o' the Times*; *Shut Up and Play the Hits*; *Homecoming*; *Taylor Swift: The Eras Tour*

**Avoid:** A cut on every bar, which flattens twenty songs into one texture and leaves the film with no way to escalate. Backstage material inserted at the show's peak, which is where its own energy was already doing the work. An audience shown constantly, which trains the viewer to watch reactions instead of the performance. Every camera angle spent in the opening number. A song included because the artist wanted it, in a film whose shape does not have a place for it. And the ending that simply stops when the encore does, because the film had no claim to land.

## The Musical Number in Film and Theatre

**Constraints:** The number has a fixed length once the music is written, and the music is usually written to a lyric that was written to a scene — so the whole chain is locked by the last decision anyone made. Placement is conventionalised and the conventions are load-bearing: an opening number, an "I want" song inside the first fifteen minutes, an act-one finale before the interval, an eleven o'clock number roughly midway through act two, and a finale. On stage the number must also cover a scene change and get people on and off; on screen it must survive being watchable in isolation, because that is how most of the audience will meet it. Rehearsal and orchestration costs make cuts late and expensive. The performer's range and breath are hard constraints on the line. And the number is the most expensive minute in the production by a wide margin, which is why the standing test — does this lift the scene higher than speech could — is a budget question as well as a craft one.

**Grammar:** A number exists because **speech has run out**, and the moment where it runs out is the single most important thing the writer controls. If the character could have said it, they should have said it, and the song is decoration. So the entry beat is the whole craft: the pressure builds inside the spoken scene until it has nowhere to go, and the first sung line is the overflow. Inside the number, the same rule as any scene applies — a value has to turn, and it must turn on the music's own architecture, so the change lands on the bridge or on the last chorus and not wherever the lyric ran out of verses. Two characters singing is a scene with two wants, which is why the argument duet and the split line are the form's strongest dramatic instruments. And the number must exit differently from how it entered: the character who walks out of the song is not the one who walked in, or the story is exactly where it was three minutes ago and has paid a fortune to get there.

**Palette:** 2–5 minutes · 1 value turn, landing on the bridge or the last chorus · an entry beat where speech fails · an exit changed from the entry · "I want" inside 15 minutes · an act-one finale · an eleven o'clock number in act two

**Rules:** Write the spoken scene until speech fails, and start the song there | Make a value turn and land it on the music's architecture, not on the lyric's convenience | Change the character between the first line and the last | Never let a number restate what the scene before it established | Give a duet two wants, not two verses | Split the lines to the argument, not to the singers' billing | Keep the title line where the melody peaks | Reprise to show what has changed, never to remind | Check the number can be watched alone, because it will be | Cut the number that does not lift the scene above what speech could do, however good the tune is

**Examples:** *Hamilton*; *West Side Story*; *Les Misérables*; *Singin' in the Rain*; *La La Land*; *Sweeney Todd*

**Avoid:** A song that says what the scene has just said, which is the commonest and most expensive fault in the form. Music entering because it is time for a song rather than because speech has failed. A number with no value turn, which is a mood and not a scene. A duet where one character is accompaniment. A reprise used as a recap. And an eleven o'clock number placed where the plot climaxes rather than where the audience's energy needs rebuilding, which is a different job that the structure is expecting to be done.

## The Character Song and the "I Want" Song

**Constraints:** The "I want" song sits inside the first fifteen minutes or so of a musical, by a convention so consistent that breaking it is a structural decision rather than a stylistic one. Two to four minutes. It belongs to one character, is usually sung alone or to a single listener, and is written before the audience has any investment — so it has to generate its own. It must also be excerptable: this is the song that will be sung at auditions, in concerts and in a hundred bedrooms, so it has to work with none of the show around it. The character song, its broader family, has the same problem in a different place: it establishes or deepens one person, must fit a performer's range and a performer's breath, and is the number most likely to be cut in previews because it is the one that most often stops the story. Both are written to a voice that has already been cast, or to one that has not, and the difference changes every line.

**Grammar:** The song's engine is **the gap between what this person has and what they can see**, which means it is built on two things the writer has to establish in about ninety seconds: a concrete present, and a concrete elsewhere. Both must be objects, places and actions rather than states, because a want stated as a feeling gives the melody nothing to sit on and gives the listener nothing to picture. The character does not know they are singing an "I want" song, so the register is not aspiration but complaint, restlessness, or a daydream leaking out — the biggest versions of the form start small and are overtaken. The bridge is where the want is named plainly, having been circled for two verses, and that is why it is the section the whole song is built toward. And because the audience has no investment yet, the specific detail is the entire characterisation budget: one accurate object in the first verse does what a scene of biography cannot.

**Palette:** 2–4 minutes, inside the first 15 · 1 concrete present · 1 concrete elsewhere · the want named plainly on the bridge · 3–5 particular objects · 1 listener, or none · a last line that is a decision or a refusal to make one

**Rules:** Build the song on the gap between a place they are and a place they can see, and make both of them places | Open on the present, not on the dream | Use objects, never states | Let the character be complaining, remembering or daydreaming rather than declaring | Circle the want for two verses and name it on the bridge | Write the last line as a decision, or as a deliberate refusal to make one | Write to the voice if it is cast, and to a range if it is not | Make it work standing alone, because it will be performed that way a thousand times | Never let the song resolve the want it is establishing | Put one exact, ordinary object in the first eight bars

**Examples:** "Part of Your World" from *The Little Mermaid*; "Wouldn't It Be Loverly" from *My Fair Lady*; "Corner of the Sky" from *Pippin*; "My Shot" from *Hamilton*; "How Far I'll Go" from *Moana*; "Waving Through a Window" from *Dear Evan Hansen*

**Avoid:** A want stated as an abstraction — freedom, happiness, more — which has no image and therefore no second verse. A song that starts at the top of its range and its emotion, leaving the bridge and the last chorus nowhere to go. A character who knows they are singing a thesis about themselves. Resolution inside the song, which spends the engine the whole show was going to run on. And the version with three verses of atmosphere and no arrival, which is a mood piece wearing an "I want" song's placement.

## The Album as a Structure

**Constraints:** Anywhere from eight to sixteen tracks and roughly 35 to 60 minutes, though the historical constraint that produced those numbers — a vinyl side of around 22 minutes, a CD of 74 — no longer binds, and streaming has pulled both ways at once: albums sequenced for playlists have grown, albums sequenced as works have shrunk. The two-side split still shapes a great many records because writers were formed by it. Nothing guarantees the listener will hear the tracks in order, or hear all of them, or hear them once — which is the constraint that every other decision has to survive. Skip rates on the first thirty seconds of a track are measured and act as a commercial pressure on where anything slow can sit. Track order is frequently decided at the end, by a group of people, some of whom are not the writer. And interludes, segues and the gaps between tracks are part of the writing whether or not anyone budgeted for them.

**Grammar:** An album is a **sequence of separate things arguing the same claim**, which is the opposite of a story told in eleven parts, and the records that try to be the second usually fail at being either. What holds it is recurrence rather than plot: a returning image, a repeated line, a character's name, a phrase of melody that arrives in track two and again in track nine. Because order is not guaranteed, every track has to be complete on its own and the album's shape has to be something a listener assembles rather than follows — which makes the album closer to a short-story collection than to a novel. The sequence is still real, and its job is dynamic rather than narrative: where the record breathes, where it accelerates, where it puts the one that costs something. Point of view is the most underused instrument available — a record that changes who is speaking in track six has done something a single song cannot. And the last track is a verdict on the claim, not an epilogue to a plot.

**Palette:** 8–16 tracks · 35–60 minutes · 1 claim · 3–5 recurring elements — an image, a line, a name, a phrase · a two-side split if the record has one · every track complete alone · a last track that delivers a verdict

**Rules:** Write the claim first and let the tracks argue it from different positions | Make every track stand alone, because the listener's order is not yours | Carry the album on recurrence, not on plot | Plant three to five elements that return, and change one of them each time | Sequence for dynamics before narrative | Decide whether the record has a two-side break and honour it or ignore it deliberately | Put the second-strongest track second, and the one that costs something at the top of side two | Use the interlude as writing, not as filler | Change the point of view at least once if the record is more than nine tracks long | End on a verdict, not on a summary

**Examples:** *The Dark Side of the Moon*; *The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars*; *good kid, m.A.A.d city*; *Lemonade*; *Blood on the Tracks*; *Tommy*

**Avoid:** A story told across eleven songs that requires the listener to follow it in order, which almost none of them will. Tracks that only function in context, which strand anyone who arrived from a playlist. Interludes that explain the concept, which is a liner note pretending to be music. A record where the last track is simply the slowest one. Recurring elements that return identically, which reads as running out of material rather than as structure. And the concept applied after the songs were written, which listeners identify with an accuracy that surprises everybody involved.

## The Score-Led Sequence

**Constraints:** The sequence has no dialogue, or almost none, and its length is fixed by the cut before the music is written — the composer receives a locked picture with in and out points agreed at a spotting session, and every hit has a timecode. Cues are typically one to four minutes; anything longer needs internal structure of its own. The writer is usually not present at the spotting session and usually wrote the sequence long before it, which means the writing has to survive being interpreted by two departments. The audience's attention has nowhere to go: with no dialogue, a viewer notices everything, so a sequence that is a minute too long is visibly a minute too long. Temp music gets attached early and shapes what everyone hears, and it is often uncleared. And the sequence is expensive per second, which is why the question asked at the spotting session — what is this music doing for the story — is also a production question.

**Grammar:** The writing problem is that **a scene with no dialogue still needs a value to turn**, and the only instruments left are task, object, order and juxtaposition. So it is written as a sequence of actions with a shape: what is being done, in what order, and what the order proves. The commonest failure is a montage that accumulates rather than escalates — five things happening is not a sequence, it is a list — and the fix is structural, not musical. Give the whole thing one subject: one theme, one question, one change, because with no words the audience cannot hold two. Let the cut carry the reaction beat that a line would otherwise carry, and write it as a beat so it survives into the edit. Use time deliberately: a score-led sequence is the one place where three years or three seconds can pass with identical apparatus, and the writer has to specify which. And write the exit, because the hardest part of a wordless sequence is returning to speech without the return sounding like the film clearing its throat.

**Palette:** 1–4 minutes · 1 subject · 1 value turn · 5–9 actions in a proved order · in and out points that match a story beat · 1 recurring object or figure · a written exit back into speech

**Rules:** Give the sequence one subject and refuse the second | Make it turn a value, not accumulate images | Order the actions so the order itself is the argument | Write tasks, not moods | Carry the reaction on a cut and write that cut as a beat | State whether three seconds or three years are passing | Anchor it with one object or figure that recurs | Write the exit back into dialogue as deliberately as the entrance | Never delegate the emotion to the music and call it written | Time it on the page and then cut a third of it

**Examples:** the opening of *Wall-E*; "Married Life" in *Up*; the star-gate and bone-cut sequences of *2001: A Space Odyssey*; the oil-derrick sequence in *There Will Be Blood*; the intercut finale of *Dunkirk*

**Avoid:** A montage that is a list of images with no order that proves anything. Two subjects, which without dialogue the audience cannot separate. A sequence written as a mood and handed to a composer to supply the content. Temp music that everyone falls in love with and nobody can clear. An exit that lands in the middle of a line. And the sequence that has visibly outlasted its idea by forty seconds, which with no dialogue is unmissable.

## The Needle-Drop

**Constraints:** **Every needle-drop is two licences and both can refuse you.** The synchronisation right comes from the publisher of the composition; the master right comes from the owner of the recording — usually a label — and either can decline for any reason, including not liking the scene. Fees in 2026 practice run from nothing on a student film, through roughly $250 to $2,500 a cue for indie features and web series, to around $45,000 for an indie feature in distribution, to $100,000 and far beyond for a famous recording in a studio film or a global campaign; a featured drop costs multiples of the same song used as background. The four variables that set the price are term, territory, media and usage, and a licence bought for festivals does not cover streaming. Clearance takes weeks to months and estates are slower than labels. These facts are background for a brief that itself raises the rights to a named recording; this skill runs no clearance step (root `SKILL.md` §0). The craft that follows either way: **write the alternates into the script**, and build the beat on what the character does with the music, so the scene's meaning does not hang on one recording.

**Grammar:** A needle-drop is **a second voice entering the film with its own opinion**, and the entire craft is the angle between that opinion and the picture. Played with the scene, the song underlines what is already there and is usually a waste of money. Played against it, the gap becomes the meaning — and the further the distance, the more the scene is claiming, which is why the cheerful song over the violence became a cliché and why placing one now is a statement about your film's relationship to that cliché. A recording carries its own date, its own audience and its own memories, so a drop is also a piece of exposition: it locates the scene in a year and in a culture without a caption. The lyric competes with dialogue for the same channel, so the choice is almost always to give the song a stretch with no words in it. And the drop has to earn its entrance — a song that begins on a cut is a device; a song that begins from something inside the scene, a radio or a car or someone pressing play, is a scene.

**Palette:** 2 licences per drop · 4 price variables — term, territory, media, usage · a featured drop at several times a background rate · 1 written alternate per scripted song · 30–90 seconds of clear air with no dialogue · 1 in-world trigger where possible

**Rules:** Write the scene so it survives losing the recording | Name a second and a third choice in the script | Play the song against the picture, not with it | Give the lyric air, because it and the dialogue are on the same channel | Trigger the drop from inside the scene wherever you can | Use the recording's own date and audience as exposition and stop explaining the year | Never let a drop carry a value turn the scene has not earned | Budget the featured drop as a multiple of a background cue, because that is what it will cost | Check what your licence actually covers, since festivals, streaming and territories are priced separately | If the song is the idea, build the scene on what the character does with it

**Examples:** the *Goodfellas* "Layla" sequence; the ear scene in *Reservoir Dogs*; *Guardians of the Galaxy*'s Awesome Mix; the opening of *Trainspotting*; "The Sound of Silence" in *The Graduate*; the choreographed cues of *Baby Driver*

**Avoid:** Writing a scene whose meaning hangs on one recording's exact lyric with no alternate written, which is the most expensive rewrite available to a low-budget production. A drop that agrees with the picture, which is money spent on emphasis. Lyrics under dialogue, where neither is heard. The ironic cheerful song over violence used without an awareness that it is now a convention rather than a surprise. A licence cleared for one medium and a film that then sells to another. And the drop that does the emotional work a scene was supposed to do, which is a hole in the writing with a famous recording poured into it.

## Music in Advertising

**Constraints:** Six seconds, fifteen, thirty, sixty, and the vertical and social cuts of each, all built from the same piece of music. The brief comes from a client through an agency and carries a locked message, a legal line, a mandatory brand mention and often a fixed title or tagline the lyric must contain. Money moves in two directions and both matter: a bespoke track is commissioned and owned; a licensed one is a sync fee that in 2026 practice runs from a few hundred dollars for a regional digital campaign, through roughly $20,000 to $150,000 for national television with a twelve-month term, to $250,000 and well beyond for a famous recording in a global campaign — and the term, territory, media and exclusivity set the number more than the fame does. The music must work with dialogue over it, with a voiceover over it, and alone. It must survive being heard at low volume on a phone with the sound half off, and it must survive being heard several hundred times by the same person. And approval is by committee, which means the lyric will be read as a legal document before anyone hears it as a song.

**Grammar:** Advertising music has to do its job **before the listener has agreed to listen**, which is the opposite of every other kit in this file. So the hook sits inside the first two seconds, the brand is inside the melodic phrase rather than appended to it, and the whole idea is expressible in a fragment because a fragment is what most people will get. The lyric is not selling the product, it is stating the feeling the brand wants attached to it — the claim is almost always implicit, and writing it explicitly is what makes an ad sound like an ad. Repetition is the entire mechanism: the listener is not choosing to learn this, so it has to be learnable without attention, which is why a sub-five-word phrase with a falling melodic shape has outperformed everything cleverer for a century. Write the cutdowns as the same idea at four lengths rather than as one piece trimmed, because a six-second edit of a thirty-second track is a fragment and a six-second version is a piece. And every word is going through legal, so write the line you want and the line you will settle for at the same time.

**Palette:** 6 / 15 / 30 / 60 second versions · hook inside 2 seconds · brand inside the melodic phrase · a sung phrase of 5 words or fewer · 1 implicit claim · a mandatory line accommodated, not fought · a version that works under voiceover

**Rules:** Put the hook in the first two seconds and the brand inside the melody | Write the six-second version as a piece, not as a trim | State the feeling, never the claim | Build a phrase of five words or fewer that a person can learn without deciding to | Write it to survive several hundred hearings by the same listener | Make a version that works under a voiceover and a version that works alone | Take the client's fixed title as a constraint and solve it rather than arguing it | Write the alternate line for the one legal will cut | Agree the writer split on the day, in writing, before anyone leaves the room | Check the sync fee against the term and territory, not against how famous the song is

**Examples:** the Cadbury "Gorilla" use of "In the Air Tonight"; the Levi's campaigns that returned catalogue records to the charts; the John Lewis Christmas cover tradition; long-running brand jingles carried across decades of campaigns

**Avoid:** A lyric that describes the product, which is the fastest way to make a listener stop hearing it. A hook at eight seconds in a six-second cut. A licensed recording chosen for fame rather than fit, at four times the fee of one that fits. A track that disappears under a voiceover it was always going to have. A joke in the lyric that will be stale halfway through a twelve-month term. And the campaign whose music was cleared for one territory and one year, and whose renewal negotiation starts from the fact that the brand now needs it.
