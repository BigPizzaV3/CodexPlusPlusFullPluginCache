## Form, Continued

### Whose Words Before the Form

**Also called:** the lyric selector, whose words before the form, who has to get through these words
**What it is:** The card at the head of the `lyric.` shelf. It does not write a lyric and it does not pick a form. It asks the three questions the shelf's own pair graph asks over and over — **whose words are these when they are sung, do the words that return come back the same, and what is already fixed before the first word** — and hands back a named shortlist for each answer. The axes were not invented: they were read off the fifty `Yields to:` conditions the working cards carry and checked against their `Avoid when:` lines, and the conditions that repeated became the questions. Every card cited here is cited by id.
**Effect on the audience:** None. This card never reaches a listener. It exists so the form that does reach the listener was chosen against the people who have to sing it, the return the song makes and the thing the words must fit, rather than reached for because verse-chorus-bridge is what a song usually is.
**Used for and where it works best:** Answer the three questions **in order and out loud, one sentence each, before you open the shelf.** The first answer is the one the shelf cares about most and removes at least half of it; the second and third leave a shortlist of two to six. If you cannot answer question one — *who has to get through these words* — you are not ready to write the lyric, you are ready to decide what the song is for. Where two answers fight for one song — a number a character sings that the audience is also meant to sing back — the shelf has already ruled which wins: `lyric.written_for_the_voice` yields to `lyric.congregational_lyric` whenever the song *"is meant for anyone, not one singer"*. **One place on this map is contested and the card says so where it reaches it: the last chorus of a song a group sings back.** There the map shows you both sides and you decide.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Kids Song, Worship Song, Heritage Song, Musical Theatre, Lyric Video, Anthem | genres: all
**Avoid when:** **You are deciding the last chorus of a song a child, a class or a room sings back.** The package disagrees with itself there — the cards on each side are named under axis two — and this map deliberately puts cards on both sides of that cell. Using it to settle the question is using it for the one thing it refuses to do. Also avoid it when the melody or the track came first and the words are being fitted to it: `lyric.dummy_lyric_topline` says the melody has already chosen the stress, the vowels and much of the shape, so questions two and three have been answered by the music before you ask them. And **distrust axis one whenever your honest answer is its third value** — more than half the reachable cards live there, so on a performer's own song the first question has done almost nothing and axes two and three are carrying the whole map. Finally, axis two does not reach the cards that work inside a single line; it gives them a row of their own on purpose, and for those the answer comes from the song, not from them.
**Example:**
> Axis one: a character sings it, inside a show. Axis two: nothing returns — she ends somewhere else. Axis three: it sits in a running order that already exists.
> Shortlist: `lyric.i_want_song` · `lyric.character_song` · `lyric.eleven_oclock_number` · `lyric.argument_duet` · `lyric.act_one_finale` · `lyric.patter_song` — and the running order decides between the first, third and fifth.
> Axis one: a child sings it back. Axis three: nothing is fixed. Axis two: **contested** — read the note under axis two, decide, then take one row. Words that hold: `lyric.verse_chorus_bridge` · `lyric.verse_refrain_form` · `lyric.repeatable_chorus` · `lyric.congregational_lyric` · `lyric.anthem` · `lyric.protest_song`. Words that change: `lyric.kids_repetition_contract` · `lyric.double_chorus`.

**The axes**

**Axis one — Whose words are these when they are sung: a group's, a story's, or a performer's to someone who only listens?**
Asked first, because it is the condition the shelf repeats most and states most flatly. Fifteen of the fifty `Yields to:` lines on the shelf's working cards turn on a group having to sing the words back or learn them by ear: *"A crowd needs a big repeated chorus to shout"* (`lyric.aaba_form`), *"Song must be learnable, singable back, hook-driven"* (`lyric.through_composed`), *"A room must sing it back together"* (`lyric.refrain_mutation`), *"Children must sing along; rhyme perfectly, never break"* (`lyric.rhyme_scheme_break`), *"Kids' material; the landing must arrive on time"* (`lyric.delayed_rhyme`), *"Song must be learned by ear"* (`lyric.rhyme_free`), *"Line will be shouted or chanted by a crowd"* (`lyric.unstressed_ending`), *"A room or class sings it together"* (`lyric.syncopated_phrasing`), *"Piece must be performed to a room"* (`lyric.prose_poem`). Two more turn on whether the voice belongs to the singer or to a story — *"An artist's own single, not a story voice"* (`lyric.character_song`) and its mirror, *"Written for an artist's own record"* (`lyric.congregational_lyric`) — and that is the axis's second value.

**Axis two — Do the words that return come back the same?**
Eight conditions turn on the return, and they are stated as facts about the form, not feelings about it: *"One unbroken irreversible line; a returning chorus strands the narrator"* (`lyric.verse_chorus_bridge`), *"No section returns; there is no grid to hold"* (`lyric.syllable_lock`), *"Section heard once; spend the clever line there"* (`lyric.repeatable_chorus`), *"Release needs a lift; verse-refrain has no second gear"* (`lyric.verse_refrain_form`), *"The late number must arrive, not restate"* (`lyric.reprise`), *"The song has a real ending to land"* (`lyric.outro_tag`), *"Bedtime; prediction-plus-surprise wakes the child"* (`lyric.kids_repetition_contract`) and its reverse in `lyric.lullaby`. The `Avoid when:` lines agree: `lyric.double_chorus` stops where *"the bridge did not change anything"*. It is asked second because its answer depends on the first — **and because this is the axis on which the package disagrees with itself**, for exactly the songs axis one puts in its first row.

**Axis three — What is already fixed before the first word?**
Eight conditions are about what exists before the lyric and nothing else: *"The content is the commission; lyrics come first"* (`lyric.dummy_lyric_topline`), *"On a deadline with a defined brief"* (`lyric.object_writing`), *"Music is commissioned for the project; one owner"* (`lyric.music_clearance_constraint`), *"Original is wordplay or culturally locked; re-write instead"* (`lyric.singable_translation`), *"Diegetic source song rather than score"* (`lyric.spotting_session`), *"Instrumental or unread language; it behaves as score"* (`lyric.needle_drop_lyric`), *"The join would hide a track with no ending"* (`lyric.segue`), *"Streaming enforces no break; mark the half instead"* (`lyric.side_break`). `lyric.syllable_lock` names the same fork in its own `Avoid when:` — *"the arrangement is being built around the words rather than the reverse"*. It is asked last because it is the only axis that reaches the placement and process cards at all — the brief, the cut, the running order — which the first two questions cannot sort.

**⚠ The last chorus of a song a group sings back — the map reaches a live disagreement here and does not settle it.**
Axis one's first row meets axis two's first two rows, and the package gives two answers to *may the last chorus change its words when a child or a room is singing them back?*

- **The words hold.** `lyric.congregational_lyric` rules out `lyric.refrain_mutation` by name — *"a congregation cannot follow a changing line"* — and lists Kids Song and Preschool among its own formats. `lyric.repeatable_chorus` builds a chorus that deepens by context and never by rewording, and lists kids song too. `lyric.verse_refrain_form` says the refrain's words *"never move"*, and that pairing it with `lyric.refrain_mutation` means giving up singability. Off the shelf, `end.last_chorus` says that on a song built to be sung back the sung line of the last chorus stays identical and the change goes into the arrangement, the voice, the tempo or a tag after the chorus; it carries an **Outranks** line over the Kids' Song format kit, and that kit now yields to it.
- **One element of the words changes, so the song ends.** `lyric.refrain_mutation` names kids song and heritage song as its strongest ground; its own fence is *"the audience needs to sing along"*, illustrated only by *"a congregation or a crowd"* — whether a child singing along stands behind that fence, the card does not say. `lyric.kids_repetition_contract` builds its whole song on one changing element, and does not say the changing stops before the last pass. `lyric.double_chorus` requires the second pass to *"differ in one element"* — *"a changed final line, an added ad-lib, a dropped word"* — and names Faith, Sports and Live Event, which are rooms that sing.

**What is settled and what is not.** The precedence written on `end.last_chorus` covers the format kit and nothing else; no line on any of these four `lyric.` cards defers to it or to each other, and `lyric.double_chorus` is not addressed by anyone. `end.last_chorus` itself draws the line on axis one's boundary — *sung back* against *listened to* — and lets a listened-to kids' song mutate, which is why the question is really two: **does this child sing it back, and if so, do its words move at the end?** The first is yours to answer on axis one. **The second is yours too, and this card will not answer it.** Both rows stay populated in the map, and the example above shows both shortlists. The argument continues off the shelf in the same shape — `emo.song_emotional_beat` wants *"exactly one thing"* different in the last chorus, `arc.lyric_arc` says *"change one word of the final chorus at most, and preferably none"*, and in the neighbouring `lyr.` family `lyr.opening_image_returns` wants the final chorus *"fundamentally transformed"* while `lyr.repetition_as_feature` repeats it verbatim.

**The default, and the one against it**

**The default is `lyric.verse_chorus_bridge` — and it is measured by distinct conditions, not by edges.** Four fields send a reader to it for three different reasons: a group must sing it back (`lyric.aaba_form`, *"A crowd needs a big repeated chorus to shout"*; `lyric.through_composed`, *"Song must be learnable, singable back, hook-driven"*), the release needs a lift (`lyric.verse_refrain_form`, *"verse-refrain has no second gear"*), and there is no turn to justify anything else (`rhyme.pivot_rhyme_transition`, from the neighbouring shelf, *"Simple pop song with no thematic pivot"*). It is also the form the shelf's named failures presuppose — `lyric.chorus_peaks_early`, `lyric.second_verse_repeat`, `lyric.all_setup_no_arrival` and `lyric.empty_chorus` are all failures of a verse-chorus-bridge song, and a failure list is a map of what the writers assumed. On the other side of axis two the shelf falls back just as firmly to `lyric.narrative_song`, also reached for three reasons — *"The song has a real ending to land"* (`lyric.outro_tag`), *"The lyric carries plot"* (`lyric.melisma_placement`, and `rhyme.non_lexical_vocables` on the same ground), *"The lyric is lecturing; show one person instead"* (`lyric.protest_song`). When the words return, fall back to the first; when they must not, to the second.

**The trap this shelf sets.** Four cards are reached more often than the default, and none of them is a default. `lyric.congregational_lyric` takes five fields and **all five fire on one condition** — a group sings it. `lyric.through_composed` takes five and all five fire on one condition — nothing returns. `lyric.kids_repetition_contract` takes five, four of them on *a child sings along* and one of those a repair edge from `lyric.second_verse_repeat`. `lyric.anthem` takes five, four of them on *a crowd or a room*. **Those are doors out of the one-voice song, not places the shelf falls back to.** Counted by edges, the default would have been one of these four — and each is exactly wrong for every song its one condition does not describe.

**The one against it is `lyric.verse_refrain_form`.** Nothing in the package routes to it. It does the default's one job — bringing a line back — without the default's one mechanism, a separate chorus that arrives with the lights up. The title lands at the tail of each verse on the verse's own tune; the register never changes; the song comes back to the same sentence the way a person does when they cannot get past it. Where the default makes the return an event, this makes it a habit, and it is the form of folk, hymnody, testimony and heritage song, where a big chorus *"would break the spell of one person talking"*. Its only stated cost is the one the default was built to avoid — *"verse-refrain has no second gear"*, and a long one in a genre that expects dynamics reads as unfinished — which a short heritage song simply pays. Its near cousin against the grain is `lyric.counter_lyric`, reached only by `lyric.duet_split`: the one card on the shelf that can *"state two opposed truths without ranking them"*, which is the opposite of a chorus that exists to be home.

**And the shape of that list is the finding, not its length. Every card that builds a chorus out of its parts is on it** — `lyric.title_line_placement`, `lyric.hook_placement`, `lyric.prechorus_lift`, `lyric.post_chorus`, `lyric.intro_line_cold_open` and `lyric.repeatable_chorus`. The pair graph reaches the *form* — verse-chorus-bridge, AABA, through-composed — and never the parts a form is assembled from, because a pre-chorus is not an alternative to a post-chorus: they sit in the same song, one before the chorus and one after it. They carry pair fields and behave like companions. **So the shelf holds a complete hook-building kit that no reader is ever routed into.** The boundaries that would govern it are scattered across three `Avoid when:` lines — a bridge is wrong *"under two minutes"* (`lyric.bridge_turn`), a pre-chorus is wrong when *"the chorus is quiet"* (`lyric.prechorus_lift`), a post-chorus is wrong for *"one voice and a guitar"* (`lyric.post_chorus`) — and no card gathers them. The card this shelf wants is the section budget: *which of the chorus's parts can a song of this length, for this singer, afford?*

The second thing the list shows: **positions and procedures are unreachable by construction.** `lyric.act_one_finale`, `lyric.album_sequencing`, `lyric.segue`, `lyric.side_break` and `lyric.i_want_song` are places in a running order; `lyric.object_writing`, `lyric.rewrite_pass_order`, `lyric.cowrite_split` and `lyric.dummy_lyric_topline` are ways of working. A record needs an opener *and* a side-two opener; nothing routes to one position as an alternative to another. This is the region of the shelf most likely to become a second decision if more placement cards are written — the *where does this song go* decision next to the *what shape is it* one — and it is why axis three exists.

The third: **the two cards whose whole job is to change the words of a return — `lyric.refrain_mutation` and `lyric.reprise` — are both unasked**, and `lyric.rhyme_scheme_break`, which breaks a trained pattern once, is unasked with them. Every edge on the shelf that touches a returning line points toward the room. **That is a fact about where readers were sent, not a verdict on the last-chorus argument** — an unasked card is one nobody was sent to, not one nobody should use, and the card this map names against the grain is unasked too.

`lyric.whose_words_before_the_form`

### Verse-Refrain (Tag) Form

**Also called:** verse-with-refrain, AAA form, tag form, the folk form, refrain-at-the-end
**What it is:** There is no separate chorus section. Every verse ends on the same one or two lines — the refrain — sung to the same melody as the rest of the verse, so the title lands at the tail of each verse rather than in a section of its own.
**Effect on the audience:** The return arrives without the song ever changing register. It feels like a person telling you something and coming back to the same sentence because they cannot get past it, which is far more intimate than a chorus arriving with the lights up.
**Used for and where it works best:** Folk, hymnody, country narrative, heritage songs, and any lyric where a big chorus would break the spell of one person talking. The craft rule: the refrain must be a complete, self-contained sentence that stays true after every verse, and it must mean something different each time only because the verse changed — the words themselves never move. Test it by reading the refrain immediately after verse 3 with verses 1 and 2 covered; if it now sounds like a non-sequitur, you have a chorus, not a refrain. Pair it with `lyric.refrain_mutation` only if you are prepared to give up singability.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Poetry, Musical Theatre, Live Event, Podcast | genres: Heritage, Faith, Drama, Testimony, Historical
**Avoid when:** The release needs a lift for radio or short-form, or the arrangement has a big instrumental chorus with nowhere for words to go. The honest limit: verse-refrain has no second gear. A four-minute verse-refrain song in a genre that expects dynamics reads as unfinished rather than restrained.
**Example:**
> V1: They moved the road in 'sixty-one, they moved it for the lorries.
> V1 refrain: *The house is where the house has always been.*
> V2: They moved the school, they moved the shop, they moved my brother's name.
> V2 refrain: *The house is where the house has always been.*
> (same eight syllables, same tune as the verse — no section change anywhere)

**Yields to:** `lyric.verse_chorus_bridge` — Release needs a lift; verse-refrain has no second gear.  
`lyric.verse_refrain_form`

### The Post-Chorus and the Drop Lyric

**Also called:** the second hook zone, pop-drop, vocal break, the attached post-chorus, the tail hook
**What it is:** Four to eight bars immediately after the chorus carrying a second, smaller hook — usually a fragment of the chorus, a vocable ("oh-oh", "na-na", "ay"), or one repeated phrase — that extends the chorus's energy instead of advancing the lyric.
**Effect on the audience:** Delivers a payoff a second time before the listener has finished the first one. Because the words are almost nothing, the ear stops decoding and just rides, which is why a post-chorus is what people hum when they cannot remember the song's actual words.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any release competing on a feed or a playlist, and any track where the production drops rather than resolves after the chorus. The craft rules: write no new information here — a post-chorus that introduces a fact will be sung over by the arrangement and lost; take the post-chorus phrase *from* the chorus so the two reinforce each other rather than compete; keep it to three or four words plus a vowel you can hold; and place the title in it only if the title is short enough to survive being chanted. For the pop-drop variant, the lyric's job is to stop — leave the bar empty and let the drop be the drop. A post-chorus is not a substitute for `lyric.prechorus_lift`; the lift raises pressure before, the post-chorus spends what is left after.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Music Video, Reels/Shorts, Lyric Video, Advertising | genres: Popular Music, Kids, Lifestyle, Advertising
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2028-06. The post-chorus as a near-obligatory section is a mid-2010s-onward streaming-era convention tied to short attention windows and drop-based production; the section has existed far longer but its current mandatory status will move with the platforms.
**Avoid when:** The song is narrative, liturgical, or performed live by one voice and a guitar, where a vocable tail is audibly a production artefact with nothing behind it. Real failure mode: the post-chorus that exists because the genre expects one, adding eight bars of "oh" to a song that had already said everything — pure length, no lift.
**Example:**
> Chorus (last line): ...and I am not the one who locks the door.
> Post-chorus: Not the one, not the one — *oh-oh* —
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Not the one, not the one — *oh-oh* —
> (four words total, all lifted from the chorus; the held "oh" is where the drop lands)

**Yields to:** `lyric.double_chorus` — Liturgical or one voice live; strip rather than vamp.  
`lyric.post_chorus`

### The Double Chorus and the A Cappella Last Chorus

**Also called:** the back-to-back chorus, the stripped last chorus, the naked chorus, the final-chorus treatment
**What it is:** Two structural moves at the end of a song. The double chorus plays the chorus twice with no verse between, usually after the bridge. The a cappella (or near-a cappella) last chorus removes the arrangement so the words arrive bare, often on the first half only before the band returns.
**Effect on the audience:** Doubling reads as insistence — the singer will not let the point go. Stripping reads as truth-telling, because removing the music removes anything the listener could have been persuaded by. Together they are the most reliable ending escalation in popular song.
**Used for and where it works best:** After a bridge that revealed something, where the chorus now means more than it did and needs to be heard rather than merely repeated. The craft rules: a double chorus must differ in *one* element on the second pass — a changed final line, an added ad-lib, a dropped word — or it is a copy-paste and the listener checks out at bar four. When a room sings it back, a congregation (`lyric.congregational_lyric`) or children (`end.last_chorus`), the words stay and the one difference goes into the arrangement or the voice. For an a cappella chorus, check the lyric survives naked: every weak line the arrangement was hiding becomes audible, so strip the section in rehearsal before you commit to it, and put the lyric's plainest, most concrete line at the front of it. This is `end.last_chorus` executed in words.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Live Event, Musical Theatre, Music Video | genres: Popular Music, Faith, Drama, Underdog, Sports
**Avoid when:** The bridge did not change anything. Two identical choruses after an inert bridge do not build — they confirm the song ended eighty seconds ago. Also avoid stripping a chorus whose power is rhythmic; some lines only work on top of a groove and sound like a rehearsal without it.
**Example:**
> Chorus 3 (full band): I'm not asking you to stay. I'm asking you to say it.
> Chorus 4 (a cappella, one voice, first two lines only):
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;I'm not asking you to stay.
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;I'm asking.
> (band returns on the next line; the dropped three words are the whole ending)

**Yields to:** `lyric.modulation_moment` — Chorus power is rhythmic; lift by key, not stripping.  
`lyric.double_chorus`

### The Intro Line and the Cold Open

**Also called:** the opening line, the pickup line, hook-first opening, the pre-verse
**What it is:** The first words of the song, before the verse proper — either a short intro line that sets the frame, or a cold open that drops the listener straight into the chorus, the hook, or the middle of a sentence with no orientation at all.
**Effect on the audience:** Decides in about four seconds whether the listener is being invited in or asked to wait. An intro line orients; a cold open commits. Neither is neutral — silence over an eight-bar intro is also a choice, and on a feed it is usually a bad one.
**Used for and where it works best:** Cold-open when the chorus is the strongest thing you have and the platform is unforgiving — this is `open.first_second_hook` and `open.cold_open` in sung form. Use an intro line instead when the song needs one fact before anything else makes sense: a place, a year, a relationship. The craft rule for both: the first line must contain a concrete noun or a verb of action, never a mood word. "Tonight I'm feeling" is not an opening; "She left the engine running" is. Second rule: whatever the first line promises, the song must deliver — an opening that sets up a story and then delivers a mood piece loses the listener at the chorus, not at the start.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Reels/Shorts, Music Video, Lyric Video, Trailer, Title Sequence | genres: Popular Music, Drama, Heritage, Advertising, Kids
**Avoid when:** The song is a slow reveal whose ending depends on the listener not knowing where they are — a cold open onto the hook spends the payoff at second one. Also avoid an orienting intro line in a live set, where the audience already has the context and the line is dead air.
**Example:**
> Cold open (chorus first, no intro): Nobody's driving. Nobody's driving this car.
> Intro line (one fact, then the verse): *Nineteen eighty-four. The border was a field.*
> Weak opening of either kind: Tonight I'm thinking of the times we had.

`lyric.intro_line_cold_open`

### The Outro Tag and the Fade-Out Lyric

**Also called:** the tag, the coda lyric, the vamp, the repeat-and-fade, the outro hook
**What it is:** The words at the very end — a repeated tag line, a fragment of the chorus vamped over the last bars, or the final line held over a fade — where the lyric's job shifts from saying to leaving.
**Effect on the audience:** The last thing heard is the thing remembered. A tag repeated under a fade installs a phrase in the listener's head after the song is gone; a hard ending on a single line leaves them with a statement instead.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any song where the last chorus said everything and there are still sixteen bars of arrangement. The craft rules: take the tag from the song, never write a new line for it — a new idea at the fade is information the listener has no room for. Choose the *shortest* true phrase, and prefer one that is grammatically incomplete, because an unfinished phrase keeps turning over after the track stops. If the piece ends cold instead, write the last line to be heard in silence and put the most important word last, which is `rhythm.end_weight` and `end.last_line` applied to a sung ending. And know that fades are a mix decision you may not control — write a tag that also works if someone cuts it clean.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Music Video, Live Event, Brand Film, Podcast | genres: Popular Music, Faith, Heritage, Drama, Romance
**Avoid when:** The song has a real ending to land — a narrative song, a lament, a theatre number. Fading out of a story is an admission you could not write the last line. Failure mode: the four-minute song with ninety seconds of tag, which is not an outro but an arrangement problem being solved with the singer's mouth.
**Example:**
> Last chorus ends: ...and the harbour keeps the light on for the boats.
> Tag under the fade (from the chorus, cut short, never resolved):
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;keeps the light on — keeps the light on — keeps the light —
> (the missing "on" is what the listener supplies for the next hour)

**Yields to:** `lyric.narrative_song` — The song has a real ending to land.  
`lyric.outro_tag`

### The Reprise

**Also called:** the dark reprise, the return, the reprise lyric, reprise rewrite
**What it is:** A song heard earlier returns later, usually shorter, with its lyric partly or wholly rewritten, so the same melody now carries what the story has done to the character since.
**Effect on the audience:** The audience recognises the tune before they parse the words, which puts them a half-second ahead — and that half-second is where the change lands. It is the most efficient emotional device in musical theatre because the setup was paid for an hour ago.
**Used for and where it works best:** Musicals, animated features, concept albums and episodic series with a recurring theme song. The craft rules that decide whether it works: keep the melodic shape and the *position* of the title phrase identical, because that is the anchor; change the smallest number of words that inverts the meaning — often just the pronoun or the object ("if I can't love her" becoming "if she can't love me" is the whole rewrite); and make it shorter than the original, always. Hardest rule: a reprise must be *dramatically necessary*, not merely available. If you cannot say in one sentence what has changed between the two hearings, cut it. The mechanism is `theme.thematic_motif` operating across an evening, and the flip in meaning is `end.callback` with a second set of words.
**Best in:** formats: Musical Theatre, Animation Feature, Animated Series, Opera, Limited Series, Feature Film | genres: Musical, Drama, Family Drama, Tragedy, Coming of Age, Kids
**Avoid when:** The show is running long and a reprise is the convenient cut — it usually is, and that tells you it was never load-bearing. Also avoid a reprise of a song whose first version the audience did not like; repetition does not rehabilitate a number. Real failure mode: the lazy reprise, the same lyric sung slower with a key change, which announces that the writers wanted the feeling without doing the work.
**Example:**
> Act I (the daughter, hopeful): *You'll write me every Sunday and I'll know the house is fine.*
> Act II (the same eight bars, the same melody, four words changed):
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*I'll write you every Sunday. You won't know the house is gone.*
> (title position identical; pronouns and one noun carry the entire reversal)

**Yields to:** `lyric.eleven_oclock_number` — The late number must arrive, not restate.  
`lyric.reprise`

### The Modulation Moment

**Also called:** the key change, the truck driver's gear change, the final-chorus lift, the gear shift
**What it is:** The song shifts key — typically up a semitone or a tone before the last chorus — and the lyric has to justify the sudden new altitude or it sounds like a gimmick.
**Effect on the audience:** A physical lift the listener feels before they can name. Done with a reason, it reads as resolve. Done without one, it reads as the song buying energy it did not earn, which is why the device has a mocking nickname.
**Used for and where it works best:** When the character has decided something and the last chorus is the decision being acted on rather than restated. The craft rules: put the lyric's *turn* at the modulation, not before it and not after — the first line in the new key must be the line that changed, whether that is a switched pronoun, a moved tense, or the first time the singer addresses someone directly. Second rule: give the top of the new key an open vowel, because everything is now a tone higher and `lyric.vowel_casting` stops being optional. Third: if the lyric is identical in the new key, either write one changed line or drop the modulation. A gear change with unchanged words is an arrangement covering for a lyric that ran out.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Musical Theatre, Live Event, Animation Feature | genres: Popular Music, Musical, Faith, Underdog, Sports
**Avoid when:** The song is ambivalent, unresolved or a lament — modulation asserts, and assertion is wrong for a song that does not know the answer. Also avoid in anything a crowd sings: `lyric.anthem` rules apply, and a key change puts the last chorus above the range of the room, which is how a stadium goes quiet at the exact moment it should be loudest.
**Example:**
> Final chorus in the old key: I keep the ring inside the drawer.
> Modulation — first line in the new key, one word changed:
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;I *kept* the ring inside the drawer.
> (tense flip on the downbeat of the lift; the whole chorus now means the opposite)

**Yields to:** `lyric.anthem` — A crowd sings it; the key change outruns the room.  
`lyric.modulation_moment`

## Rhyme and Sound, Continued

### The Held or Delayed Rhyme

**Also called:** the suspended rhyme, long-range rhyme, the withheld resolution, distant rhyme
**What it is:** Setting up a rhyme sound and then not paying it for several lines — or paying it in the next section entirely — so the ear carries an open expectation across a stretch of song.
**Effect on the audience:** Unresolved tension the listener cannot locate. They know something is outstanding; when it lands four lines later, the closure is far larger than a couplet's because the debt was bigger.
**Used for and where it works best:** Long verses that must not feel like a list of couplets, and any section where you want the chorus to *complete* the verse rather than follow it. The craft rules: the held sound must be distinctive enough to be remembered — a fat open vowel, not a schwa; the rhyme must land on a stressed syllable at a strong beat or nobody registers the payment; and it works exactly once per song, because a second held rhyme reads as randomness rather than design. The strongest version rhymes the last line of the verse with the first line of the chorus, so the chorus is heard as the answer to a question the verse asked. Compare `lyric.rhyme_scheme_break`: that withholds a rhyme permanently, this one pays late.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Musical Theatre, Poetry, Novel | genres: Popular Music, Drama, Literary, Romance, Tragedy
**Avoid when:** The audience needs to sing along or the tempo is fast — the ear cannot hold a sound across four bars at 140 bpm. Also avoid in kids' material, where `lyric.kids_repetition_contract` demands the landing arrive on time.
**Example:**
> He told me that the ferry runs at **nine**. (A — set)
> He told me it was cheaper in the winter.
> He told me that the crossing isn't rough.
> He told me lots of things.
> Chorus (four lines later, A paid, on the downbeat): And none of them were **mine**.

**Yields to:** `lyric.kids_repetition_contract` — Kids' material; the landing must arrive on time.  
`lyric.delayed_rhyme`

### Multi-Syllable and Compound Rhyme

**Also called:** multis, polysyllabic rhyme, compound rhyme, stacked rhyme, chain rhyme
**What it is:** Rhyming two or more syllables at once, across word boundaries if necessary — *battlefield / rattle steel*, *not a saint here / got a painter* — so the match is a whole phrase rather than a single ending.
**Effect on the audience:** Audible virtuosity. The listener hears pattern where speech normally has none, and the density itself reads as intelligence, control, and in battle contexts, dominance.
**Used for and where it works best:** Rap verses above all, plus patter songs, comic lyrics and any section where the writer's skill is part of the content. The craft rules: build them by breaking a target phrase into syllables and hunting a rhyme for each syllable separately, then discarding the ones that force the sense — the drill is allowed to produce nonsense, the finished bar is not. Second rule: the stressed syllables must align, or the rhyme is technically present and audibly absent — *mándatory / hánd for me* works, *mandátory / hand for mé* does not; this is `lyric.stress_alignment` operating inside the rhyme. Third: place the multi where the beat is open, and let the surrounding bars be plain — wall-to-wall multis flatten into texture and the listener stops hearing any of them.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Musical Theatre, Live Event, Poetry, Reels/Shorts | genres: Popular Music, Comedy, Satire, Political, Underdog
**Avoid when:** The lyric is about something that should sound unplanned. Multis are the sound of preparation, which is exactly wrong for grief, confession, or a child's voice. The real failure mode is the bar that is clearly reverse-engineered from the rhyme — see `lyric.forced_rhyme`, which multis make easier to commit and harder to hide.
**Example:**
> Plain end rhyme: They said the shop was closing down / I had to leave the town.
> Three-syllable multi, stresses aligned (**BOLD** = stressed):
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;They **shut** the **shut**ters, **called** it a **mer**-cy **clos**-ing,
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;my **fa**ther **kept** the **keys** like they were **worth** sup**pos**-ing.
> (*mercy closing / worth supposing* — vowel chain and stress pattern match across three syllables)

**Yields to:** `lyric.slant_rhyme` — Grief or confession must sound unplanned.  
`lyric.multisyllabic_rhyme`

### The Rhyme-Free Lyric

**Also called:** unrhymed lyric, blank lyric, prose lyric, the spoken-sung line
**What it is:** A lyric that uses no end rhyme at all, holding the lines together with parallel syntax, repeated openings, matched syllable counts and consonant echoes instead.
**Effect on the audience:** Removes the sound of "song" and leaves the sound of speech set to music. Listeners describe it as honest or unguarded, which is a reaction to the absence of pattern, not to the content.
**Used for and where it works best:** Testimony, documentary songs, liturgical and heritage material, and character songs where the person singing would never be witty. The craft rules: rhyme is a structural load-bearing element, so you must replace it, not just remove it — use anaphora (`rhythm.anaphora`) or a repeated closing word (`rhythm.epistrophe`) to mark line ends, and lock the syllable counts so the ear still hears a grid. Second rule: end every line on the strongest word, since there is no rhyme to mark the ending and `rhythm.end_weight` is now doing all of it. Third: repeat one image rather than one sound.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Poetry, Opera, Audio Drama, Musical Theatre | genres: Testimony, Heritage, Faith, Literary, Social Drama, Migration
**Avoid when:** Anyone has to learn the song by ear — rhyme is a memory device before it is an ornament, and congregations, children and crowds all rely on it. Honest limit: much of what is praised as a rhyme-free lyric is a rhymed lyric with slant rhymes so loose they go unnoticed. Removing rhyme *entirely* is rarer, harder, and unforgiving of a weak line.
**Example:**
> I was carrying the small bag.
> I was carrying the papers in the small bag.
> I was carrying my mother, who weighed nothing by then, in a photograph, in the papers, in the small bag.
> (no rhyme anywhere; anaphora plus one accumulating image holds all three lines together)

**Yields to:** `lyric.perfect_rhyme` — Song must be learned by ear.  
`lyric.rhyme_free`

### Alliteration and Consonance as Texture

**Also called:** sound texture, consonant patterning, sonic palette, phonetic colour
**What it is:** Choosing words for the consonants they repeat rather than for the rhymes they make — hard stops, sibilants, liquids, nasals — so a line carries a physical texture underneath its meaning.
**Effect on the audience:** Operates below conscious notice. Clustered plosives make a line feel like an argument; liquids and nasals make the same content feel like a hand on the shoulder. Listeners report the mood and credit the melody.
**Used for and where it works best:** The two or three lines of a song that must land physically — the top of a chorus, the first line of a bridge, the last line before silence. The craft rules: pick one consonant family per section and stop, because two competing textures cancel; keep alliteration to two or three hits, since four is a tongue-twister and audiences hear the joke rather than the line; and put the alliterating syllables on stressed beats or the pattern is inaudible. For fast delivery, sibilants and stops cut through a mix where nasals disappear, which is why patter and rap lean hard on them. This is `rhythm.consonant_texture` in a sung line, and it is the one sound device that survives a bad PA.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Poetry, Musical Theatre, Trailer, Audio Drama, Picture Book | genres: Popular Music, Comedy, Myth, Epic, Kids, Heritage
**Avoid when:** The register is plain-spoken realism. Texture is an authorial fingerprint, and a character song built on conspicuous alliteration tells the audience the writer is in the room. Failure mode: the line that means nothing and merely sounds good, which survives drafting precisely because it is pleasant to say.
**Example:**
> Stops and hard consonants (a decision): **C**ut the **c**ord, **k**ick the **c**rate, **c**lose the **d**oor behind me.
> Liquids and nasals, same event (a loss): **L**et it **l**ie, **l**eave the **l**inen, **l**ock is **l**ow and **s****l**ow.
> (identical content, opposite physical feeling — no rhyme involved in either)

`lyric.sound_texture`

### The Feminine (Unstressed) Line Ending

**Also called:** feminine ending, unstressed ending, the trailing syllable, weak ending, falling ending
**What it is:** Ending a line on an unstressed syllable — *WON-der*, *LEAV-ing*, *O-ver* — instead of on a stressed one, so the line falls away rather than landing.
**Effect on the audience:** Softens closure. A stressed ending is a full stop; an unstressed ending is a comma the listener does not consciously see. Alternating them is how a section breathes without changing line length.
**Used for and where it works best:** Conversational verses, and any section where you want forward motion without resolution — the pre-chorus especially. The craft rules: put the unstressed ending on the lines that carry the question and the stressed ending on the line that answers it, so the sound of the section mirrors its argument. Second, and this is the one people miss: an unstressed ending needs a weak beat under it. If the melody puts a long note or a downbeat on that trailing syllable, the singer is forced to stress an unstressable syllable and the line sounds foreign — the same failure `lyric.stress_alignment` names. Third: a run of four falling endings makes a section sound listless; break it with a hard masculine ending when the character decides something.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Musical Theatre, Poetry, Opera, Animation Feature | genres: Popular Music, Musical, Romance, Drama, Coming of Age
**Avoid when:** The line must be shouted, chanted, or sung by a crowd — `lyric.anthem` needs stressed endings, because a crowd cannot land together on a weak syllable and the section falls apart. Also avoid in comedy: a punchline on an unstressed ending does not punch.
**Example:**
> Falling, falling, falling, then landing (last syllable **BOLD** = stressed):
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;I wasn't really *listen*-ing
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;and you weren't really *ask*-ing
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;and nobody was *leav*-ing
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;until I said your **name**.

**Yields to:** `lyric.anthem` — Line will be shouted or chanted by a crowd.  
`lyric.unstressed_ending`

## Prosody, Continued

### Parallel Syllable Lock Across Verses

**Also called:** syllable matching, verse-to-verse lock, metrical parallelism, the grid
**What it is:** Verse 2 and verse 3 carry the same syllable count, the same stress positions and the same line lengths as verse 1, so a single melody fits all of them without the singer negotiating.
**Effect on the audience:** Invisible when right; when wrong the listener hears a singer cramming or stalling, and reads it as a bad performance rather than a bad lyric.
**Used for and where it works best:** Every strophic song — which is nearly all of them — and non-negotiable for anything sung by a choir, a congregation, a classroom, or a dubbed cast. The craft rule: write verse 2 against a marked-up copy of verse 1, with the stressed syllables in capitals, rather than against the melody in your head. Note the *stress positions* as well as the count; nine syllables with the stress in a different place is a different line, and that is the error most writers miss because the count matches. Where verse 2 genuinely needs one more syllable, use a pickup note before the bar rather than compressing inside it — and record the change, because a singer will otherwise fix it silently and differently on every take. `lyric.singable_translation` inherits this whole card: a translation that breaks the lock is unusable regardless of how accurate it is.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Kids Song, Musical Theatre, Opera, Preschool, Live Event | genres: Kids, Faith, Musical, Popular Music, Heritage, Preschool
**Avoid when:** The song is through-composed (`lyric.through_composed`) or the arrangement is being built around the words rather than the reverse. And you may break the lock deliberately for one line in a final verse, to make a narrator sound like they are running out of room — but only once, and only after the grid is established.
**Example:**
> V1: i KEPT the LET-ters IN the KITCH-en DRAW-er [10, stresses on 2-4-6-8-10]
> V2 correct: i BURNED the PHO-tos IN the GAR-den FIRE [10, same stress map]
> V2 broken: i BURNED ev-ery-thing that WE had ev-er COL-lect-ed [12, stresses in new places — singer must cram; the melody now fits nobody]

**Yields to:** `lyric.through_composed` — No section returns; there is no grid to hold.  
`lyric.syllable_lock`

### Melisma and the Stretchable Word

**Also called:** melismatic setting, the run, riffing, syllable stretching, vocal runs
**What it is:** One syllable sung across several notes, rather than one note per syllable. As a lyric decision it is the choice of *which* word is allowed to stretch, and the vowel that word ends up sitting on.
**Effect on the audience:** Heightened emotion with the words suspended. While the run happens the listener receives no new information, only feeling, which is exactly why the word underneath it must already be the most important one in the line.
**Used for and where it works best:** Gospel, soul, R&B, worship and any big-voice performance; also one-off, at the single peak of an otherwise syllabic song. The craft rules the lyricist owns: choose the stretch word before the singer does — if you do not, the run will land on whatever falls at the peak, and the audience will remember "and" for four bars. Second, that word must end on an open, sustainable vowel: *stay*, *go*, *home*, *know*. Nothing with a closed vowel or a final consonant cluster can be stretched, and the singer will silently substitute a word that can. Third, mark it in the lyric sheet so the melisma is a written decision, not an accident of performance.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Live Event, Opera, Musical Theatre, Music Video | genres: Faith, Popular Music, Romance, Drama, Testimony
**Avoid when:** The lyric carries plot or facts. Every melisma is a bar in which nothing is said, and a narrative song (`lyric.narrative_song`) cannot afford it. Also wrong for children's material, where the child must be able to sing the line back, and for anything with burned-in or on-screen text, where a four-second run on one word leaves the caption stranded.
**Example:**
> Syllabic line: I told you I would go and then I went.
> Marked for one stretch at the section's peak:
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;I told you I would **go-o-o-o**  — and then I went.
> (the run sits on an open "oh" and on the line's true subject; "and" and "then" stay one note each)

**Yields to:** `lyric.narrative_song` — The lyric carries plot; a run says nothing.  
`lyric.melisma_placement`

### Syncopation and the Pushed Line

**Also called:** pushing the beat, anticipation, laying back, off-beat phrasing, the early entry
**What it is:** Placing a line — or one word inside it — ahead of or behind where the bar expects it, so the vocal rhythm no longer coincides with the pulse.
**Effect on the audience:** Pushing forward reads as eagerness, urgency, or interruption; laying back reads as ease, exhaustion or defiance. The listener hears character, not rhythm, and a section of perfectly on-the-beat singing reads as stiff or childlike by comparison.
**Used for and where it works best:** Conversational verses, any groove-based genre, and the exact moment a character loses patience. The craft rules: push the line by starting it on the *and* before the downbeat, and put an unimportant syllable there — a pickup word like "and", "so", "but" — so the stressed word still lands on the beat and `lyric.stress_alignment` survives the displacement. Second rule: syncopate against a stable reference, so the section needs at least one straight line for the pushed one to be heard against. Third: write the pickup into the lyric sheet, because "and-I-was" versus "I was" is a different line and any dub, translation or choir will need to know which one it is.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Music Video, Reels/Shorts, Musical Theatre, Live Event | genres: Popular Music, Comedy, Romance, Slice of Life, Drama
**Avoid when:** A crowd, a congregation or a class has to sing it. Groups sing behind the beat by nature, and a syncopated line plus a lagging crowd is a mess. Also avoid in dubbed or subtitled songs where the caption timing is locked to the bar (`dial.subtitle_constraint`).
**Example:**
> On the beat (stiff): | I NEV-er said that | ONCE in eight-een | YEARS |
> Pushed (pickup word before the bar, stress still on the downbeat):
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;and-I | NEV-er said that | ONCE in eight-een | YEARS |
> (three extra syllables, no extra bar; the line now sounds interrupted rather than recited)

**Yields to:** `lyric.congregational_lyric` — A room or class sings it together.  
`lyric.syncopated_phrasing`

### Writing the Duet Split

**Also called:** the split lyric, two-voice writing, line assignment, the divided verse
**What it is:** Deciding, line by line, which of two singers says what — and whether they alternate lines, take whole sections, finish each other's sentences, or sing together.
**Effect on the audience:** The split itself is the drama. Who speaks first, who gets the last line, who is allowed to finish a sentence and who is cut off tells the audience the power balance before a word of content registers.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any song with two characters, in theatre, animation, kids' material or a featured-artist single. The craft rules: give each voice a distinguishable vocabulary and sentence length, because on audio alone the listener has only the words and the timbre to tell them apart — this is `dial.signature_vocabulary` inside a lyric. Second: decide who owns the title line, and give it to the character the song is *about*, not the one who sings most. Third: use the shared line sparingly; if they sing together from the first chorus you have no move left for the end. Fourth: the split must not break the breath — a swap mid-phrase needs both singers to breathe where the phrase allows.
**Best in:** formats: Musical Theatre, Song Lyric, Animation Feature, Animated Series, Kids Song, Opera | genres: Musical, Romance, Kids, Family Drama, Drama
**Avoid when:** One character has nothing at stake. A duet where the second voice exists to echo is a solo with a guest, and audiences hear the imbalance as a featured artist wasted. Also avoid splitting a lyric whose power is that one person is alone with it.
**Example:**
> HER: I said I'd meet you at the gate.
> HIM: You said a lot of things.
> HER: I said I'd —
> HIM: — meet me at the gate. I know. I waited.
> BOTH (first shared line, and the last line of the song): *We are both still standing at the gate.*

**Yields to:** `lyric.counter_lyric` — Second voice only echoes; write a harmony line.  
`lyric.duet_split`

### The Counter-Lyric and Harmony Line

**Also called:** counterpoint duet, the partner song, quodlibet, the overlapping lyric, the second text
**What it is:** Two different lyrics sung at the same time over the same harmony — each self-contained, each intelligible on its own, colliding in the overlap.
**Effect on the audience:** The audience cannot hear both at once, and that is the effect: they receive two positions simultaneously and resolve them as a single, contradictory feeling. It is the only device in song that can state two opposed truths without ranking them.
**Used for and where it works best:** Musical theatre arguments, ensemble finales, and any moment where two characters are talking past each other rather than to each other — the sung version of `dial.talking_past`. The craft rules: each line must have been heard alone first, so build the counter-lyric out of material already established earlier in the show; stagger the entries so the audience gets one line of clear air before the collision; and design the *collision points* deliberately — pick the two or three bars where both lyrics land the same word or opposite words, and let everything else be texture. Accept the arithmetic: at most one line in three will be understood, so no plot may live here. For harmony lines beneath a lead, keep the words identical or reduce them to a single repeated phrase; a harmony singing different words at the same volume just sounds like a mistake.
**Best in:** formats: Musical Theatre, Opera, Animation Feature, Live Event, Song Lyric | genres: Musical, Drama, Comedy, Family Drama, Tragedy, Political
**Avoid when:** The audience needs the information, the piece will be subtitled, or the release is audio-only for a second-language audience. Two simultaneous lyrics cannot be captioned and cannot be translated live. Failure mode: the counter-lyric that is merely the first lyric paraphrased — two voices, one idea, nothing gained but volume.
**Example:**
> SHE (established in Act I): The house is paid for, the house is paid for, and nobody is leaving.
> HE (new text, same harmony, entering one bar later): I signed it over Tuesday, I signed it over Tuesday, I'm sorry —
> Collision bar, both on the same beat: SHE: *nobody* — HE: *nobody* —
> (one shared word is the whole point; everything around it can be a blur)

**Yields to:** `lyric.duet_split` — Audience needs the words; subtitled or translated.  
`lyric.counter_lyric`

## More Song Types

### The Duet as Argument Song

**Also called:** the quarrelling song, the argument duet, the confrontation number, the two-hander song
**What it is:** A song in which two characters disagree and the disagreement is the structure — each verse a new tactic, the chorus each one's unchanged position, and the end a win, a loss or a stalemate that the story then acts on.
**Effect on the audience:** The only kind of song that has suspense in the way a scene does. The audience is not waiting for the chorus, they are waiting to see who folds.
**Used for and where it works best:** Musicals and animated features at the act break, and any concept piece where a relationship changes on record. The craft rules: give each character a *different* argument, not a different volume — one argues from money, the other from loyalty, and neither is stupid (`anta.wins_argument` is the test). Second: each verse must be a new tactic, not a restatement, which is `scene.tactic_change` written in verses. Third: something must be different at the end; a sung argument that ends where it started is a scene the show could cut. Fourth: keep the lines short. Real arguments interrupt, and long symmetrical stanzas make both characters sound like they are waiting politely.
**Best in:** formats: Musical Theatre, Animation Feature, Opera, Song Lyric, Stage Play, Limited Series | genres: Musical, Drama, Family Drama, Romance, Comedy, Tragedy
**Avoid when:** The characters agree, or the outcome is predetermined and the audience knows it. A rigged argument in song is worse than in dialogue because the loser still has to sing eight bars of it. Also avoid where one party has all the power; that is not an argument, it is an instruction with a tune.
**Example:**
> HIM: The rent, the roof, the van, the loan — I am not being cruel, I'm being *accurate*.
> HER: My mother is not a line on your page.
> HIM: She is if I'm the one who pays it.
> HER: Then stop paying.
> HIM: (nothing — eight bars, no words, and he starts the last chorus in her key)

**Yields to:** `lyric.duet_split` — Two voices but no genuine disagreement.  
`lyric.argument_duet`

### The Protest Song

**Also called:** the topical song, movement song, resistance song, the picket-line song
**What it is:** A song that names a specific injustice, attaches it to a specific actor, and gives a crowd something to sing back — argument and chant in one structure.
**Effect on the audience:** Turns a private opinion into an audible majority. The person singing discovers they are not alone, which is the actual political function; persuasion of opponents is rare and mostly a myth about the form.
**Used for and where it works best:** Live protest, advocacy campaigns, heritage and testimony pieces. The craft rules: be factual and be specific — a named place, a named year, a named number is what separates a protest song from a mood, and the specificity is also what protects you when the claim is challenged. Second: show, do not lecture; the strongest protest lyrics are narrative songs about one person that let the listener draw the conclusion (`theme.irony_as_argument` outperforms `theme.explained_theme` by a wide margin here). Third: the chorus must be chantable without a lyric sheet — six or seven syllables, hard consonants, no subordinate clauses. Fourth: make the title the chorus line, so the song is findable under the words people will actually shout.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Live Event, Music Video, Reels/Shorts, Podcast | genres: Advocacy, Political, Social Drama, Heritage, Testimony, Migration
**Avoid when:** You do not know the facts, or the cause is not yours to speak for — both are detectable and both discredit the song and the movement it claims. Also avoid the abstract protest song, which is `lyric.empty_chorus` with a conscience: "stand up, rise up, the time is now" names no one and threatens nobody.
**Example:**
> Verse (specific, factual, one person):
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;They shut the ward on the fourteenth of June.
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sixty-one beds and a letter of thanks.
> Chorus (seven syllables, chantable, no clause):
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*Give us back the fourteenth of June.*

**Yields to:** `lyric.narrative_song` — The lyric is lecturing; show one person instead.  
`lyric.protest_song`

### The Congregational Lyric

**Also called:** the worship song, hymn text, the congregational song, corporate singing lyric
**What it is:** A lyric written to be sung by a whole room of untrained voices on a first or second encounter, where the test is not whether it is moving to hear but whether a hundred strangers can actually get through it together.
**Effect on the audience:** Participation rather than reception. A congregation that can sing a line owns it; a congregation that cannot goes silent and listens to the band, which is a different and much weaker experience.
**Used for and where it works best:** Worship, memorials, communal heritage events, and school or classroom singing. The craft rules, and they are hard constraints: keep the melody inside about an octave from middle C so ordinary voices can reach both ends; keep phrases short enough for one unhurried breath, since an untrained room breathes early and often; keep the syllable count symmetrical between lines so nobody has to cram; and write in the plural or in a second person the whole room can mean, because a lyric a person cannot honestly sing is a lyric they will mouth. Two more that matter: no proper nouns or private images the room does not share, and no `lyric.refrain_mutation` — a congregation cannot follow a changing line. A children's song sung in a classroom still keeps the one guessable slot `lyric.kids_repetition_contract` changes per verse, a count or a letter the sequence hands every child, while its refrain stays fixed. Vocabulary should be plain and the theology or claim unmistakable, since the room is agreeing to it out loud.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Live Event, Kids Song, Preschool | genres: Faith, Heritage, Kids, Testimony, Advocacy
**Avoid when:** The song is written for an artist's own record. Congregational constraints strip out the range, the rhythmic freedom and the personal detail a solo recording lives on — and a song tailored to one voice (`lyric.written_for_the_voice`) is exactly what a room cannot sing. Real failure mode: the worship single pitched for a soloist with a wide leap in the chorus, at which point the room stops singing and the leader mistakes silence for reverence.
**Example:**
> Unsingable by a room (range leap, long phrase, private image):
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And in the hour that I was broken on the floor of my father's empty kitchen, You were there
> Congregational (eight syllables, one breath, shared, plural):
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*You were there, and You are here.*
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*We were lost, and we were found.*

**Yields to:** `lyric.written_for_the_voice` — Written for an artist's own record.  
`lyric.congregational_lyric`

### The Comedy Patter Song

**Also called:** patter, the list-and-rattle number, the tongue-twister song, the Major-General number
**What it is:** A comic song delivered at high speed with one note per syllable, where the humour comes from the sheer quantity of words fitted into the time and the fact that the singer is visibly at the edge of managing it.
**Effect on the audience:** Delight at competence under pressure. The audience is not only laughing at the jokes, they are laughing at the feat — which is why a patter song gets applause even when individual lines are missed.
**Used for and where it works best:** Musical theatre and comic opera, comic animation, and any character defined by self-important fluency. The craft rules: strictly syllabic setting, one syllable per note, no melisma anywhere; perfect rhyme only, because at speed a slant rhyme registers as a mistake (`lyric.perfect_rhyme` is mandatory here and `lyric.slant_rhyme` is forbidden); front-load the vowel-heavy words and avoid consonant clusters across word boundaries, since *-sts str-* at speed is unsayable; and build the rhyme density upward so the last eight bars are the tightest. Structurally it is usually `lyric.list_song` at tempo, so the final item must break the pattern. Give the singer two planned breathing bars or the number cannot be performed twice in a week.
**Best in:** formats: Musical Theatre, Opera, Animation Feature, Animated Series, Live Event, Comics | genres: Comedy, Musical, Satire, Kids, Dark Comedy
**Avoid when:** Anything is being translated or subtitled — patter is the least translatable lyric form there is, because speed, rhyme and meaning are welded together. Also avoid when the audience needs the content: at patter tempo perhaps half the words land, so the song must be funny in shape even to someone who catches one line in three.
**Example:**
> (the clerk, defending his filing system, bars 17–20)
> I have cata-logued the cata-logue, I've indexed every index,
> I've a system for the systems and a memo on the memos,
> I have filed the filing cabinet in a cabinet for filing —
> and I cannot find your name.

**Yields to:** `lyric.list_song` — The number will be translated or subtitled.  
`lyric.patter_song`

### The Jingle and the Sub-Fifteen-Second Hook

**Also called:** the sonic logo, audio logo, the brand hook, the sting, the mnemonic
**What it is:** A complete sung idea in five to fifteen seconds, carrying a brand or product name inside a melodic phrase built to be recalled without the listener ever choosing to learn it.
**Effect on the audience:** Unconsented memorisation. An audio mnemonic attaches to the brand name far more durably than a spoken claim, and it is recalled unprompted, which is the whole commercial argument for the form.
**Used for and where it works best:** Radio and streaming audio ads, pre-roll, smart-speaker and short-form video, and channel or podcast idents. The craft rules: the name goes in the melody, not around it, and it goes on the strongest beat of the phrase; four to eight lines maximum and usually two; one claim only, in words a seven-year-old could repeat; the last note must land on an open vowel that the name itself supplies, so choose the phrasing that ends on the brand. Write the five-second cut-down first and grow it to fifteen — the reverse never works, because a trimmed jingle loses the resolution that made it stick. The register rules of `voice.advertising_register` apply, and the claim itself is `theme.implicit_ad_claim`.
**Best in:** formats: Advertising, Brand Film, Reels/Shorts, Podcast, Title Sequence, Live Event | genres: Advertising, Lifestyle, Comedy, Kids
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-12. The current dominant length and shape follow from streaming pre-roll, smart-speaker and vertical-video ad slots; the sub-six-second sonic logo displaced the thirty-second sung jingle over roughly a decade and the next slot format will move it again.
**Avoid when:** The brand is in a serious category — grief services, medical, disaster relief — where a memorable tune reads as trivialising. Also avoid when the name is long, cluster-heavy or hard to stress; some names simply cannot be sung, and no amount of melody fixes a three-consonant ending.
**Example:**
> Five-second cut (brand name on the downbeat, open vowel last):
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Two days. One box. **MAR**-lo.
> Fifteen-second version, same landing:
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Order on a Monday, wear it on a Wednesday,
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;nothing left to think about —
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Two days. One box. **MAR**-lo.

`lyric.jingle_micro_hook`

### The Boast and the Diss Verse

**Also called:** braggadocio, the battle verse, the diss track, flexing, the takedown
**What it is:** A verse whose content is the speaker's own superiority or an opponent's inferiority, built as a chain of setups and punchlines rather than as a narrative, and judged on wit and rhyme density rather than on sincerity.
**Effect on the audience:** Sport. The listener keeps score — a good line draws the same reaction as a good shot, and the pleasure is competitive rather than emotional. A crowd will rewind a punchline the way it replays a goal.
**Used for and where it works best:** Hip-hop verses, battle formats, competitive or comic theatre numbers, and any character who wins by talking. The craft rules: keep the setup short — two to six words that plant an expectation — and put the payload word last, because a punchline whose funny word is buried mid-line does not land (`comedy.funny_word_last`). Second: escalate. Bars must get harder, not merely continue, so save the two best lines for the close (`beat.escalation_step`). Third: attack a specific, checkable thing — a boast or a diss about nothing in particular is noise, and the specific claim is what makes it quotable. Fourth: multis and internal rhyme belong here (`lyric.multisyllabic_rhyme`, `lyric.internal_rhyme`) because density is part of the argument.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Live Event, Reels/Shorts, Musical Theatre, Comics, Animated Series | genres: Comedy, Satire, Political, Underdog, Sports, Popular Music
**Avoid when:** The target is a private individual, or the attack is on something a person did not choose — the form rewards wit against a chosen public position and punishes cruelty against an unchosen trait, and the audience turns fast (`comedy.punch_direction`). Also avoid a boast verse in a song about vulnerability; the register collision reads as insecurity rather than range.
**Example:**
> You brought a lawyer to a rap battle,
> I brought the receipts and the man who signed them;
> you rented the car that you rap about renting —
> I know, I was standing behind you in line.

`lyric.boast_diss_verse`

### The Lullaby

**Also called:** the cradle song, sleep song, the rocking song, nursery lullaby
**What it is:** A song written to move a child from waking to sleep: small melodic range, heavy repetition, a rocking rhythm, and a lyric of almost no incident — plus, in most traditions, a strain of adult sorrow the child is not meant to understand.
**Effect on the audience:** Regulation rather than entertainment. The predictability is the mechanism: a child who can anticipate every line has nothing left to attend to, and attention is what has to fall. The adult singing it is soothed by the same repetition.
**Used for and where it works best:** Kids' and preschool releases, heritage recordings, and any scene where a character must be shown caring without saying so. The craft rules: keep the vocabulary tiny and concrete — objects in the room, not concepts; repeat whole lines rather than varying them, which is the opposite of the adult rule and why `lyric.refrain_mutation` is wrong here; end lines on long open vowels and soft nasals so the voice can go quiet without going sharp; and let the song *decelerate* — shorter lines and longer gaps toward the end, so the last verse is mostly silence. The one permitted complexity is emotional: lullabies across traditions carry the singer's own weariness or grief under a calm surface, and that double layer is what makes them survive as songs for adults too.
**Best in:** formats: Kids Song, Preschool, Picture Book, Song Lyric, Animation Feature, Audio Drama | genres: Kids, Preschool, Heritage, Family Drama, Faith, Romance
**Avoid when:** The song has to teach or activate. A counting or alphabet structure (`lyric.kids_repetition_contract`) wakes a child up by design — prediction plus surprise is the wrong mechanism at bedtime, because the surprise is the part that keeps them up. Also avoid a bright, wide-range melody, however pretty; range is arousal.
**Example:**
> The lamp is off. The coat is on the door.
> The lamp is off. The coat is on the door.
> The road is quiet, the road is quiet,
> and I will not be going anywhere.
> (verse 3, decelerating): The lamp is off. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The coat. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The door.

**Yields to:** `lyric.kids_repetition_contract` — The song has to teach or activate.  
`lyric.lullaby`

## Process and Craft

### Object Writing

**Also called:** sense-bound writing, the ten-minute timer, sense memory gathering, free-writing to an object
**What it is:** A timed pre-writing exercise, not a song: pick one real object, set ten minutes, and write about it through all seven senses — sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, plus the organic sense (heartbeat, nausea, tension) and the kinesthetic sense (the body's relation to space, the feeling of falling or swaying). No rhyme, no metre, no sentences required. Stop the instant the timer goes.
**Effect on the audience:** Indirect but decisive. Sense-bound language makes the listener supply their own memory, which is why a specific detail feels universal and an abstract statement feels like nobody's. It is the raw material `emo.specific_detail` is built from.
**Used for and where it works best:** Every morning before writing, and specifically when a lyric has gone abstract and you cannot say why. The craft rules that make it work rather than waste ten minutes: use a real object you have actually encountered, not a symbol; go for speed and depth over quality and do not edit while writing; deliberately reach for the organic and kinesthetic senses, because those two are where the non-obvious lines come from and they are the two everybody skips; and stop dead on the timer so you finish hungry. Afterwards, mine it — the usable yield is usually two or three lines out of ten minutes, and the rest is fuel. Do it daily; it is a gym, not a technique you deploy in a song.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Poetry, Novel, Musical Theatre, Picture Book, Podcast | genres: Popular Music, Literary, Heritage, Drama, Romance, Testimony
**Avoid when:** You are on a deadline with a defined brief — object writing generates, it does not solve, and it will hand you ten minutes of beautiful material about the wrong subject. Honest limit: the output is not a lyric and should never be pasted into one wholesale; undigested object writing is how a song ends up dense, image-crammed and impossible to sing (`lyric.page_lyric`).
**Example:**
> Object: *a kettle*. Ten-minute pass, raw, sense by sense:
> sight — limescale ring like a tide mark / sound — that click before it gets going, then the roar / touch — handle sticky where the plastic has gone soft / smell — old steam, the wallpaper above it / organic — my chest does something when it clicks off / kinesthetic — I lean on the counter waiting, always the same hip.
> Mined into a lyric line: *I still lean on the same hip waiting for the click.*

**Yields to:** `lyric.writing_to_brief` — On a deadline with a defined brief.  
`lyric.object_writing`

### Point of View Choice and the Deliberate Switch

**Also called:** POV in lyric, person choice, the pronoun decision, I/you/he, the address
**What it is:** Choosing who speaks and who is addressed — first person to a "you", first person about a "he/she", second person addressing the listener, or third-person narration outside the song — and deciding whether that choice changes anywhere in the song.
**Effect on the audience:** Point of view sets the listener's distance from the events. First person to a "you" is the closest and most claustrophobic; third person puts the events at arm's length and lets the listener judge; second person addressing the listener implicates them. The same story in different persons is a different song, not a different arrangement.
**Used for and where it works best:** Decide it before the first line, and write the decision at the top of the page. The craft rules: pick the person that makes the hardest line possible to say — if the confession is unsayable in first person, that is usually the sign it belongs there. Second: where "I" and "you" are both present, the pronouns take stress, so their position is a prosody decision as well as a narrative one. Third, on switching: a deliberate late switch is one of the strongest moves in the form — a story told in third person for two verses that turns to "I" in the bridge reveals the narrator was in it all along. But it works once, it must land on a section boundary, and everything before it must be re-readable as true. This is `pov.contract` and `pov.handoff` at song scale; the failure version is `lyric.pronoun_drift` below.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Musical Theatre, Poetry, Novel, Audio Drama, Animation Feature | genres: Popular Music, Drama, Heritage, Testimony, Romance, Migration
**Avoid when:** The song is congregational, anthemic or for children — those need one stable person for the whole song, usually plural, and a switch leaves a room singing a sentence that is no longer true of them. Also avoid second person addressing the listener in advertising unless you mean the accusation; "you need this" reads as instruction, not invitation.
**Example:**
> Same event, three persons:
> 3rd: *She kept the letters. She never opened one.*
> 1st to a you: *You kept my letters. You never opened one.*
> 1st: *I kept your letters. I never opened one.*
> The switch, used once, at the bridge: two verses of *she* — then: *— and I am the one who put them in the drawer.*

**Yields to:** `lyric.congregational_lyric` — Congregational, anthemic or children's; one stable person throughout.  
`lyric.point_of_view_choice`

### The Rewrite Pass Order

**Also called:** the revision pass, editor's hat, the rewrite checklist, the lyric audit, the rewrite pass order, one problem per pass, the prose revision sequence — this card is filed under `lyric` because its example is a lyric, but the method is general and transfers whole to prose and to script
**What it is:** Rewriting a lyric in fixed, separate passes — one problem per pass — rather than trying to improve everything at once from the top.
**Effect on the audience:** They never see it, and that is the measure. A lyric that has been passed properly has no line the listener stumbles on and no line they forget.
**Used for and where it works best:** Every finished draft, and especially the one you like. An order that works, and the reason for it: **1. Sing it** — aloud, at tempo, standing; mark every stumble before you read a word, because a lyric that looks finished on screen can be unsayable (`lyric.page_lyric`, `rhythm.read_aloud_test`). **2. Structure** — does verse 2 move the song on, does the bridge reveal, is the title in the right place. **3. Truth** — does every line mean something you would defend; cut filler here (`lyric.filler_line`). **4. Specificity** — replace abstractions with objects, one per section minimum. **5. Prosody** — stress positions, syllable lock between verses, breath points. **6. Rhyme** — last, always, because fixing rhyme first is what produces `lyric.forced_rhyme`; and when a rhyme will not come, rewrite the *earlier* line. **7. Sing it again.** The two rules that do the most work: separate the writing hat from the editing hat and never wear both in the same hour, and never fix the second line of a couplet first.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Musical Theatre, Kids Song, Lyric Video, Poetry | genres: Popular Music, Musical, Kids, Faith, Heritage, Drama
**Avoid when:** The draft is one day old and still hot — you will edit the life out of it. Leave it, then pass. Also stop rewriting when the passes start reversing each other; a lyric can be sanded until nothing is left but correctness, which is how you arrive at a technically flawless song nobody remembers.
**Example:**
> Draft line: I just really need you here with me tonight
> Pass 1 (sing) — stumbles on "really need you here"; four unstressed syllables in a row.
> Pass 3 (truth) — "just really" is filler; the true thing is that he is not coming.
> Pass 4 (specificity) — import an object from verse 2: the second chair.
> Result, same syllable count: *I moved the second chair against the wall tonight.*

`lyric.rewrite_pass_order`

### The Co-Write Split

**Also called:** the split sheet, songwriting splits, the Nashville rule, credit division
**What it is:** The agreement — written, on the day — recording what percentage of a song each writer owns. Not a legal footnote but a craft condition: the split decides who can say what in the room.
**Effect on the audience:** None directly. It decides whether the song gets finished, whether it gets released, and whether the writers ever work together again, which is why it belongs in a craft database rather than a legal one.
**Used for and where it works best:** Every session with more than one writer in it, including the producer who wrote the topline and the artist who changed four words. The craft rules: agree the split before anyone leaves the room and write it down, because US copyright defaults to equal shares among co-authors unless a written agreement says otherwise, and memory is the least reliable document in music. The pragmatic default — equal shares to everyone who was in the room — is used precisely because it removes the negotiation that poisons the next session; the alternative is a contribution-based split agreed in advance of writing, not after the song turns out to be good. Splits must total exactly 100%. The working rule for the lyricist: never trade credit for speed, and never leave a session with a shared file and no sheet. If you brought the title and the concept, say so on the day; nobody will remember by Friday.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Musical Theatre, Kids Song, Advertising, Music Video | genres: Popular Music, Musical, Kids, Advertising, Faith
**Avoid when:** — There is no "avoid"; there is only *how*. But do avoid the two common traps: the vague equal split on a song where one writer brought a finished lyric and the other changed a preposition, and the handshake with no sheet, which becomes a dispute the moment there is money. Note the honest limit: these are conventions and defaults, not law in every territory, and a real release needs a real agreement.
**Example:**
> Written on a phone, in the room, before anyone leaves:
> *"Coat Behind the Door" — 15 Sept. Y. — lyric + title, 50%. D. — melody + chords, 30%. A. — topline in chorus 2, 20%. Total 100%. Agreed: Y / D / A."*
> That note, dated and sent to all three, is the whole instrument.

`lyric.cowrite_split`

### Writing to a Brief and to a Client's Title

**Also called:** the sync brief, writing on assignment, the commissioned lyric, the given title
**What it is:** Someone else supplies the constraint — a theme, a scene, a campaign, sometimes a fixed title — and the lyric has to satisfy it while still sounding like something a person meant.
**Effect on the audience:** They will hear whether the song came from the brief or from the writer. Commissioned lyrics fail audibly in one specific way: they describe the emotion the brief named instead of showing a situation that produces it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Sync, advertising, film and animation songs, kids' educational commissions, heritage and institutional pieces. The craft rules: start from your own true version of the brief's emotion and only then check it against the brief, rather than the reverse — this is the whole difference between a song and a deliverable. Second: keep the *situation* specific and the *statement* broad, because a supervisor needs the song to fit a scene they have not described to you; a song about one bus station can score a hundred departures, while a song about "new beginnings" scores none. Third, on a given title: find the reading of it nobody expects, then write toward that — take the title's syllable count and stress shape as fixed (it has to remain the title) and change what it means by what you put around it. Fourth: deliver a clean, slow, lyric-forward version too, because sync usage often needs the words intelligible under dialogue.
**Best in:** formats: Advertising, Brand Film, Song Lyric, Animation Feature, Trailer, Title Sequence, Kids Song | genres: Advertising, Lifestyle, Kids, Faith, Heritage, Romance, Underdog
**Avoid when:** The brief asks for a feeling you do not believe in, or for a claim you would not make in your own name. That song will be finished, delivered, paid for and hollow, and a listener hears it — `theme.purpose_washing` and `emo.borrowed_feeling` name what the result becomes. Also refuse a brief whose subject belongs to a community you are outside of unless you have a way in.
**Example:**
> Brief: "uplifting, fresh start, 30 seconds, must contain the title *Open Road*."
> Weak (writes the brief): *Open road, open sky, anything is possible tonight.*
> Same title, specific situation, broad statement:
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;I sold the second car. I kept the map that came with it.
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*Open road* — and nobody in the passenger seat to argue.

`lyric.writing_to_brief`

## Further Named Failure Modes

### The Second Verse That Repeats the First

**Also called:** second verse curse, second verse hell, the restated verse, the parallel verse
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** Verse 2 says what verse 1 said with different words — same time, same place, same position, same information — so the song has two openings and no middle.
**Effect on the audience:** The listener's attention drops at the first line of verse 2 and does not fully return. They cannot name why; they simply start doing something else, and the song becomes background before the bridge.
**Used for and where it works best:** — It is legitimate in one place only: a strophic congregational or children's song built on a fixed frame, where the whole design is a repeated slot (`lyric.kids_repetition_contract`). It hurts most in narrative and single-track songwriting, where verse 2 is the only place the song can move.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always, outside that exception. Diagnose it by covering the choruses and reading the two verses back to back: if swapping their order changes nothing, you have one verse written twice. The fixes, in order of usefulness: move verse 2 forward in time; change the location; change who is present; or keep the situation and change the narrator's position on it — same facts, new verdict. A useful working habit is to write verse 2 immediately after verse 1, before the chorus, so you are still in the verse's voice and the story is still moving.
**Example:**
> V1: You said you'd call me Sunday from the station.
> V2 (the failure — same moment, new adjectives): You told me you would ring me from the platform.
> V2 (fixed — time moves, position moves): It's Thursday. I have stopped checking the phone. I have started checking the papers.

**Yields to:** `lyric.kids_repetition_contract` — Strophic kids or congregational song on a fixed frame.  
`lyric.second_verse_repeat`

### The Accidental Pronoun Shift

**Also called:** pronoun drift, person slip, the you/he wobble, POV leak
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** The song addresses "you" in the chorus and describes "him" in verse 2, or slides from "I" to "we" to "you" without a decision, so the listener loses track of who is being sung to.
**Effect on the audience:** Low-level confusion that presents as the song being "hard to connect with". Listeners almost never identify the pronoun as the problem; they report that the song did not land, which is why this failure survives so many drafts.
**Used for and where it works best:** — Only as a deliberate, marked switch on a section boundary, which is a different technique entirely (`lyric.point_of_view_choice`). Unmarked drift has no use. It hurts most in sung material, where the listener cannot go back and check (`rhythm.listener_no_rewind`), and worst of all in congregational and kids' songs, where a room is saying the words out loud about themselves.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always. The diagnostic takes two minutes: circle every pronoun in the lyric sheet and write the person in the margin, section by section. You are either talking *to* someone for the whole song or *about* them; any change must be one change, at a section boundary, that you can justify in a sentence. The common cause is drafting order — a chorus written to "you" and a verse written months earlier about "her" — so run the check after any transplant of material between songs.
**Example:**
> Drifting: *You never learned to swim* (chorus, to her) → *She was frightened of the harbour* (V2, about her) → *We were always frightened* (bridge, both of us)
> Fixed, one person throughout: *You never learned to swim. You were frightened of the harbour. You are still frightened, and so am I.*

**Yields to:** `lyric.point_of_view_choice` — A marked switch at a section boundary.  
`lyric.pronoun_drift`

### The Chorus That Peaks Before the Bridge

**Also called:** the early ceiling, nowhere left to go, the maxed chorus, premature climax
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** Chorus 1 is written at maximum — top of the range, biggest words, full statement — so the bridge and the final chorus have no available altitude and the song's second half is a plateau.
**Effect on the audience:** The song is at its best ninety seconds in and never again. Listeners often love the first chorus and abandon the track before the end, which shows up in skip data as a strong open and a collapsing second half.
**Used for and where it works best:** — Deliberately, in a sub-thirty-second cut-down or a jingle, where there is no second half to protect. It hurts most in singles and theatre numbers, where the entire architecture assumes the last chorus is the biggest thing in the song.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Any song with a bridge or a final chorus. The test: write the last chorus and the bridge *before* you finalise chorus 1, then check you left yourself somewhere to go — a word you have not used, a person you have not addressed, a fact you have not admitted. The fixes: hold one image back from chorus 1 entirely; write chorus 1 a note lower and let the arrangement grow instead of the lyric; or take something *away* from the last chorus (`lyric.double_chorus`) so it lands by contrast rather than by size. Note the related error: a bridge cannot lift a chorus that already said everything, which is why a dead bridge is usually a symptom of this card, not a cause.
**Example:**
> Chorus 1 (maxed — "everything", "never", "always" all spent): I would give you everything I have, I would never let you go, I would always be the one —
> Bridge and chorus 3 now have no larger words available. The fix is to spend less early:
> Chorus 1: *I would give you Tuesday. That's what I have.*
> Chorus 3 (the raise, finally earned): *I would give you everything I have, and it is Tuesday, and it is all of it.*

**Yields to:** `lyric.double_chorus` — Let the last chorus land by contrast, not size.  
`lyric.chorus_peaks_early`

### All Setup, No Arrival

**Also called:** the song that never lands, the unpaid setup, the deferred point, the missing verdict
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A lyric that spends every section establishing — the place, the weather, the history, the atmosphere — and never states or dramatises what the song is *for*. There is no verdict, no turn, no thing the singer finally admits.
**Effect on the audience:** A pleasant, competent song that leaves nothing behind. The listener finishes it feeling the absence of a payoff without being able to say which line was missing, because the missing line was never written.
**Used for and where it works best:** — Genuinely useful as an atmosphere bed in sync, where a song that says nothing specific can score anything. Even there it is a product, not a song. It hurts most in narrative songs, laments and theatre numbers, all of which are defined by the arrival.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always, outside a sync bed. The diagnostic is one question answered in one sentence: *what does the singer know at the end that they did not know at the start?* If there is no answer, the song has no arrival, and no amount of imagery will supply one — this is `end.unpaid_setup` and `scene.value_turn` failing together. The fix is almost never to add a section; it is to cut a verse of scenery and put the admission in the bridge. Related tell: the writer describes the song's subject fluently in conversation and cannot point to the line where it is said.
**Example:**
> All setup: V1 the harbour in winter / V2 the boats and the men who left / bridge the weather turning / chorus *and the water keeps on moving* — beautiful, and nothing happened.
> With an arrival, bridge rewritten: *My father's name is on the third boat from the end. I have never once gone down to look at it. I went last Tuesday.*

**Yields to:** `lyric.bridge_turn` — Cut scenery; put the admission in the bridge.  
`lyric.all_setup_no_arrival`
