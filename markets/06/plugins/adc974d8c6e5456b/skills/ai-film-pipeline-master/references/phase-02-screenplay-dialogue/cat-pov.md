## The Basic Positions

### The Owner Before the Distance

**Also called:** the viewpoint selector, owner before distance, who is holding this before how close it stands
**What it is:** The card at the head of the `pov.` shelf. It recommends no position. It asks the three questions the shelf's own pair graph asks over and over — **who owns the viewpoint and how many owners there are, what fixes the distance and whether the distance may move, and what the deliverable will let a viewpoint do** — and hands back a named shortlist for each answer. The axes were not invented. They were read off the forty-five `Yields to:` conditions the cards carry, cross-checked against every `Avoid when:` on the shelf, and the conditions that repeated became the questions. Every card cited here is cited by id.
**Effect on the audience:** None. This card never reaches a reader. It exists so that the position that does reach the reader was chosen against the material, the form and the length, rather than inherited from the last thing the writer read.
**Used for and where it works best:** Answer the three questions **in order, out loud, in one sentence each, before you write sentence one**. The first eliminates most of the shelf; the second and third leave a shortlist of three to seven. If your deliverable is not a page — a screen, a panel, a controller, a microphone, a stage, a song — **answer question three first and let it overrule the other two**, because the form decides what a viewpoint can be before you get a vote. Where two answers to question one fight for one scene, that is not a hard choice, it is a multi-viewpoint book and the card for it is `pov.multi_pov_architecture`.
**Best in:** formats: all | genres: all
**Avoid when:** **Your honest answer to axis three is its first value.** Twenty-eight of the fifty live under *long prose*, so on a novel this map has only two questions left and is doing two-thirds of the work it does on a song or a game. Also avoid it as a way of choosing between a position and an instrument: **ten cards on this shelf answer their own `Avoid when:` with *nothing*, *never*, or a stage of drafting rather than a property of the story** — they are conditions of the work, not options in it, and this map stores them in the last value of all three axes without sorting them at all. And **do not run it on a draft that already has a viewpoint**: there the question is not which position but whether the one you have is being held, which is `pov.distance_audit`'s work — and nothing on this shelf will ever send you there, which is the map's largest blind spot and is recorded below rather than hidden.
**Example:**
> *One owner, inside the story. Fixed at the skin. Long prose.*
> → `pov.first_person_central` · `pov.close_third` · `pov.free_indirect_style` · `pov.child_pov` · `pov.nonhuman_pov` · `pov.divergent_cognition` · `pov.reader_surrogate`

**The axes**

**Axis 1 — Who owns the viewpoint, and is it one owner or many?**
Asked first, because it is the condition the shelf repeats most: sixteen of the forty-five `Yields to:` lines turn on the number of consciousnesses and on whether the audience is allowed to know more than the one it is in — *"Plot needs events the narrator cannot attend"* (`pov.first_person_central`), *"Witness has no reason to be at key scenes"* (`pov.peripheral_narrator`), *"Reader must know what the character cannot"* (`pov.close_third`), and its exact reverse, *"Suspense depends on the reader not knowing"* (`pov.omniscient`), *"One character's ignorance is the point"* (`pov.multi_pov_architecture`), *"Two equally live consciousnesses in one scene"* (`pov.free_indirect_style`), *"Two POVs share every scene; give each territory"* (`pov.handoff`), *"A fixed rotation pre-assigns the owner"* (`pov.scene_ownership`), *"Observer wants nothing; reassign by stake"* (`pov.inert_viewpoint`), *"Someone with a real stake is in the room"* (`pov.reader_surrogate`).

**Axis 2 — What fixes the distance, and may it move?**
Fifteen conditions turn on this, and they are stated as facts about the material rather than as feelings about the reader: *"Moment-to-moment suspense; a climax needs interior"* (`pov.distant_third`), *"Distance is deliberate; the filter verb is working"* (`pov.filter_word_removal`), *"'will she survive' is the live question"* (`pov.retrospective_distance`), *"Mid-book jeopardy must survive; living retrospect"* (`pov.posthumous_narrator`), *"The story's turn is internal, unfilmable"* (`pov.objective_camera`), *"A sustained action sequence needs live scene"* (`pov.found_document`), *"Want transmission without a frame read twice"* (`pov.nested_tellers`), *"Insistent internal audience in third; build the frame"* (`pov.narratee`), *"Precision is the deliverable"* (`pov.oral_narration`), *"Short or functional scene; hold a two-rung band"* (`pov.distance_slide`).

**Axis 3 — What will the deliverable let a viewpoint do, and is there room to teach it?**
Fifteen conditions turn on the form and on how much room it gives, and this is the only axis that reaches the non-prose region of the shelf at all: *"Interiority the camera cannot photograph, when VO earns it"* (`pov.screen_no_interiority`), *"Protagonist must act against the player's will — author the cutscene"* (`pov.playable_pov`), *"Simultaneous action; a narrating teller must carry it"* (`pov.audio_pov`), *"The song is for a congregation, crowd or classroom"* (`pov.song_pov`), *"Single-protagonist vehicle; own scenes, not strands"* (`pov.episode_ownership`), *"No room to establish a contract; sentence one is it"* (`pov.contract`), *"Long and event-driven; 'you' cannot sustain it"* (`pov.second_person`), *"Testimony or heritage; a real teller's authority"* (`pov.ai_narration`), *"Prologue needs a different order of voice"* (`pov.prologue_pov`). It is asked last on a page and **first everywhere else**.

**The default, and the one against it**

**The default is `pov.close_third`, with its rung on `pov.psychic_distance_ladder` chosen rather than drifted into — and it is measured, not asserted.** Five cards send a reader to it, and they send from five different regions of the shelf for five different reasons: `pov.distant_third` (*"Moment-to-moment suspense; a climax needs interior"*), `pov.omniscient` (*"Suspense depends on the reader not knowing"*), `pov.objective_camera` (*"The story's turn is internal, unfilmable"*), `pov.multi_pov_architecture` (*"One character's ignorance is the point"*) and `pov.found_document` (*"A sustained action sequence needs live scene"*). **Two cards look like rivals on raw in-degree and neither survives the count of distinct conditions.** `pov.multi_pov_architecture` also takes five, but three of the five arrive from `pov.scene_ownership`, `pov.handoff` and `pov.same_event_twice` — techniques that already live inside a multi-viewpoint book, which makes those edges a way back to the architecture rather than a reason to adopt it, leaving two genuine entry conditions. `pov.omniscient` takes four, and **two of the four are repairs from named failures** — `pov.head_hopping` and `pov.impossible_observation` both point at it, which measures how often limited viewpoint breaks, not how often omniscience is chosen; its two remaining edges give the same condition twice, *more than one mind's knowledge is needed in one place*. Close third is the only card on this shelf reached from five directions for five separate reasons, and that is the contract's own definition of a fallback.

**The one against it is `pov.objective_camera`, and it is the default's exact inverse.** Close third's entire value is that a reader is inside one skull; objective camera refuses every interior including the one it is following. **Nothing on this shelf yields to it.** The single edge it touches runs outward — *"The story's turn is internal, unfilmable"* sends a reader from it to the default — so the shelf knows how to leave this position and has no route in. Its `Avoid when:` is the shortest boundary here, one sentence, *a camera cannot film a man deciding, only a man standing still*, which is a narrow and payable cost for the only position on the shelf where the audience's own inference is the content: a reader who has worked out what a character feels holds that reading in a way a reader who was told it never does. Its near cousin against the grain is `pov.found_document`, likewise the target of no `Yields to:` field, likewise a narration made of record rather than voice — and the two of them are the only cards here that withhold the interior without ever promising it, which is why neither can be accused of teasing.

### First Person Central

**Also called:** I-narration, autodiegetic narration, first person protagonist
**What it is:** The protagonist tells her own story in her own grammar. Everything the reader gets has passed through one skull.
**Effect on the audience:** Instant intimacy and instant suspicion. The reader bonds fast because there is no visible narrator standing between them and the character — and then starts testing the account, because there is only one account.
**Used for and where it works best:** Reach for it when the interest of the story is the *interpretation*, not the events: crime seen by the criminal, illness seen from inside it, a con, a confession, a coming-of-age. The craft rule that makes it work is that the narrator must be wrong about something the reader can independently see. A first person narrator who is right about everything is just a third person narrator wearing a pronoun. This is the natural home of `info.unreliable_narrator` and `info.naive_narrator`, but it does not require either — plain honest first person still buys you a voice.
**Best in:** formats: novel, short story, memoir, podcast | genres: crime, literary, horror, romance, coming-of-age
**Avoid when:** The plot needs events the narrator cannot attend. Every workaround — the overheard conversation, the letter that arrives, the friend who explains it later — costs credibility, and three of them in one book reads as machinery. Also avoid when the character's voice is not interesting enough to live in for 90,000 words; first person cannot be turned down.
**Example:**
> I told them everything. I have always been honest about that night, and I have told it the same way every time, which is how you know it is true.

**Yields to:** `pov.multi_pov_architecture` — Plot needs events the narrator cannot attend.  
`pov.first_person_central`

### Peripheral First Person

**Also called:** the witness narrator, the Nick Carraway, first person observer, the Watson
**What it is:** A minor character narrates someone else's story. The "I" is present, has skin in the game, but is not the engine of the plot.
**Effect on the audience:** The subject stays mysterious and slightly larger than life, because the reader only ever sees him from outside, through a mind that is already impressed or appalled.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when your most interesting character would become smaller if we heard his thoughts — the genius, the monster, the charismatic fraud. The craft rule: the witness must have a stake that changes. If the narrator ends where he began, the book has a hole in the middle of it; the reader will ask why this person told the story, and there will be no answer. Also useful when you want a legitimate, non-cheating source of `info.restricted_narration` — the witness genuinely does not know.
**Best in:** formats: novel, novella, series, documentary | genres: literary, crime, historical, biography
**Avoid when:** The witness has no reason to be present at the key scenes. Detective fiction solved this with a live-in companion; if your structure has no equivalent, you will be inventing errands for your narrator all book.
**Example:**
> He was the only honest man I ever met, and I helped ruin him, and I have been dining out on the story for eleven years.

**Yields to:** `pov.collective_we` — Witness has no reason to be at key scenes.  
`pov.peripheral_narrator`

### Close Third Person

**Also called:** third limited, limited third, tight third, over-the-shoulder
**What it is:** "He" and "she", but the narration is locked to one character's perception, knowledge and priorities for the duration of the scene. The reader sees only what that character could see.
**Effect on the audience:** Nearly the intimacy of first person with none of its constraints on prose. The reader identifies without being trapped in the character's diction.
**Used for and where it works best:** The default position of modern commercial and literary fiction, and it is the default for a reason: it takes the discipline of first person and gives back the freedom to write a sentence the character could not write. The craft rule is the *permission test* — before any line of narration, ask whether the POV character could perceive, know or plausibly infer it. If not, cut it or give it to someone else's scene. Pairs directly with `char.what_they_notice`: in close third, the inventory of noticed detail *is* the characterisation.
**Best in:** formats: novel, short story, serialised fiction, graphic novel | genres: thriller, fantasy, romance, literary, SF
**Avoid when:** You need the reader to know something the character does not — sustained `info.dramatic_irony` is expensive in close third, because you have to smuggle it in through things the character sees but misreads. If you want the bomb under the table and a character who cannot know, either add a POV or move out to omniscient deliberately.
**Example:**
> Distant third: *She left at six, before the others arrived.*
> Close third: *Six o'clock. If she went now she would not have to hear what any of them had decided about her.*

**Yields to:** `pov.omniscient` — Reader must know what the character cannot.  
`pov.close_third`

### Distant Third Person

**Also called:** cool third, wide third, third person with authorial distance, the long lens
**What it is:** Third person narration that stays outside the character's interior, reporting behaviour, context and consequence in a voice that is clearly the book's rather than the character's.
**Effect on the audience:** Judgement space. The reader watches rather than inhabits, and gains the room to disapprove, to see a pattern, to feel time passing quickly.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it for summary, for the passage of years, for the opening page that establishes a world before it establishes a person, and — most powerfully — for the moment of maximum pain, where pulling back hurts more than leaning in. The craft rule: distance is a tool of *contrast*, so it only reads as cold if you have earned warmth somewhere nearby. A whole novel at this distance is a different book, not a defective one, but know which you are writing.
**Best in:** formats: novel, short story, literary essay | genres: historical, literary, satire, war, epic
**Avoid when:** You need moment-to-moment suspense. At this range a knife to the throat is information, not threat. Do not sit here through a climax by accident.
**Example:**
> The funeral was on a Tuesday. Twelve people came. Her son stood at the back with his hands in his pockets and left before the end, and that was the last time the family was all in one room.

**Yields to:** `pov.close_third` — Moment-to-moment suspense; a climax needs interior.  
`pov.distant_third`

### Omniscience

**Also called:** omniscient narrator, the god narrator, authorial narration, third person omniscient
**What it is:** A single narrating intelligence that stands above the story, can enter any mind, knows the future, and has a personality and vocabulary of its own that belongs to no character.
**Effect on the audience:** Scale, authority, and a specific pleasure — the sense of being told a story by someone who has already read it. Handled well, it feels generous. It can also be very funny, because it can compare two people's private opinions of the same conversation.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it for large casts, long time spans, communities, and any book whose real subject is a pattern no single character can see. The craft rule that separates it from a defect: **the narrator must be a consistent entity with an audible voice of its own**, and it must go where the *narration* wants to go, not where a character drags it. If your "omniscient" passages read in the diction of whichever mind they are inside, you are not omniscient, you are head-hopping — see `pov.head_hopping`.
**Best in:** formats: novel, kids story, picture book, narrated film | genres: historical, literary, comic, fantasy, magical realism, fable
**Avoid when:** You are writing suspense that depends on the reader not knowing. An omniscient narrator who conspicuously avoids one fact draws attention to the avoidance — that is `info.withheld_pov` and it plays as coy unless the withholding is itself thematic.
**Example:**
> They agreed the marriage was a mistake. He thought so for thirty years and never said it; she said it twice a week for thirty years and never thought it; and in this, as in most things, they were better matched than either of them believed.

**Yields to:** `pov.close_third` — Suspense depends on the reader not knowing.  
`pov.omniscient`

### Objective Camera-Eye

**Also called:** third person objective, dramatic POV, the fly on the wall, behaviourist narration, Hemingway's iceberg mode
**What it is:** Narration that reports only what a camera and microphone could record — action, dialogue, surface — and never enters any mind, including the one you are following.
**Effect on the audience:** Active reading. The audience has to infer every feeling from behaviour, and the emotion they build themselves hits harder than one they are handed.
**Used for and where it works best:** Superb for scenes where the characters are concealing from each other and from themselves: negotiations, breakups conducted through logistics, violence. The craft rule is that objective POV demands *loaded behaviour* — every gesture must be selectable as evidence. It fails not from coldness but from underwriting. Note the deliberate trade: you lose the interior and must pay for it in `dial.on_the_nose` avoidance, because the dialogue now carries the whole subtext.
**Best in:** formats: short story, screenplay, stage play, literary novel | genres: literary, crime, horror
**Avoid when:** The story's turn is internal — a change of belief, a decision made in silence. A camera cannot film a man deciding, only a man standing still.
**Example:**
> "Fine," she said. She picked up the receipt, folded it twice, and put it in her coat pocket. "It's fine." She did not put on the coat.

**Yields to:** `pov.close_third` — The story's turn is internal, unfilmable.  
`pov.objective_camera`

### Second Person

**Also called:** you-narration, the imperative mode, apostrophic narration
**What it is:** The narration addresses a "you" who performs the story. That "you" may be the reader, a stand-in for the narrator's own past self, or a specific absent person the narrator is talking to.
**Effect on the audience:** Implication. The reader is conscripted, and feels both closer and more uneasy than in first person, because the story is telling her what she is doing.
**Used for and where it works best:** Three jobs it does better than anything else. One: dissociation — a narrator describing his own trauma or addiction as something happening to a "you" he cannot admit is himself. Two: the second person as *accusation*, a letter to someone who left. Three: procedure and instruction as satire. The craft rule is to decide early who "you" actually is and never wobble; a reader who cannot tell whether she is being addressed or watching someone be addressed will drop out by page three.
**Best in:** formats: short story, novella, interactive fiction, game narrative, advertising | genres: literary, experimental, horror, satire
**Avoid when:** The story is long and event-driven. Sustained second person is a high-attention mode; at novel length the device becomes the subject whether or not you want it to be. Also avoid when the "you" would have to do things a reader will refuse to imagine doing — the moment you write *you strike your daughter*, half the readership steps out of the pronoun.
**Example:**
> You do not call her back. You tell yourself you will call her on Sunday, because Sunday is a day when people call each other, and then it is Sunday and you clean the kitchen instead.

**Yields to:** `pov.first_person_central` — Long and event-driven; "you" cannot sustain it.  
`pov.second_person`

---

### First Person Plural

**Also called:** the collective we, choral narration, communal narrator, the town voice
**What it is:** A group narrates as one — a neighbourhood, a class, a family, a workforce — using "we" and claiming a shared memory, shared judgement and often shared guilt.
**Effect on the audience:** The subject becomes an object of communal attention, studied and never reached. The reader also becomes aware of the *group* as a character with its own bad faith.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when the real subject is the watchers, not the watched — a town's treatment of an outsider, boys' mythologising of girls, an institution closing ranks. The craft rule: the "we" must at some point fracture, or admit a gap in the record. One "we did not know then" or one dissenting member gives the collective a conscience and a plot. A pure unanimous "we" for 200 pages goes inert.
**Best in:** formats: novel, short story, voiceover script | genres: literary, gothic, historical, satire
**Avoid when:** You need a protagonist whose choices drive the plot. Collectives observe; they rarely act, and when they act they act badly, which is a theme, not a structure.
**Example:**
> We agreed later that we had always known. This was not true, but it was easier than the alternative, which was that we had watched her every day that summer and seen nothing at all.

**Yields to:** `pov.first_person_central` — You need a protagonist whose choices drive plot.  
`pov.collective_we`

## Psychic Distance and Its Controls

### The Psychic Distance Ladder

**Also called:** narrative distance, authorial distance, Gardner's five levels, the zoom
**What it is:** The measurable gap between the reader and the character's interior, running from a long exterior shot of the world down to the character's unmediated sensation. Gardner's classic demonstration steps it in five: *It was winter of the year 1853. A large man stepped out of a doorway* → *Henry J. Warburton had never much cared for snowstorms* → *Henry hated snowstorms* → *God how he hated these damn snowstorms* → *Snow. Under your collar, down inside your shoes, freezing and plugging up your miserable soul.*
**Effect on the audience:** It sets, moment by moment, how much the reader is watching versus experiencing. Most reader complaints of "I didn't care" and "it dragged" are distance problems, not plot problems.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use the ladder as a diagnostic on every scene: mark the rung each paragraph sits on, then ask whether the scene's function wants that rung. Setup and transit want rungs 1–2; the value turn of `scene.value_turn` almost always wants 4–5. The craft rule is that the rung is set by *word choice*, not by pronoun — "damn snowstorms" is close third and "Henry J. Warburton" is far third, in the same grammatical person.
**Best in:** formats: novel, short story, memoir, documentary | genres: all
**Avoid when:** Nothing — every prose narrator sits somewhere on it whether the writer chose or not. The only misuse is unconscious use.
**Example:**
> Rung 2: *Mary Hallam had never liked the sea.*
> Rung 5: *Salt in the mouth again. Don't look at the water, look at the horizon, they always say look at the horizon, as if the horizon has ever helped anyone.*

`pov.psychic_distance_ladder`

### Sliding the Distance Within a Scene

**Also called:** the narrative zoom, pulling focus, distance modulation, in-scene camera move
**What it is:** Deliberately moving up or down the ladder inside a single continuous scene — opening wide, closing to the interior at the pressure point, pulling back for the exit.
**Effect on the audience:** It reads as rhythm. The reader does not notice the move, only that the scene "builds" and then "lands".
**Used for and where it works best:** The standard shape is wide → close → close at the turn → one rung back for the last line, so the scene exhales. The craft rule everyone learns late: **move one rung at a time**. A jump from rung 1 to rung 5 mid-paragraph reads as a mistake even when it is intentional, because the reader has no time to re-seat. The exception is the *deliberate* jump-cut as a shock device, used once, at a moment violent enough to justify it. This is the finest-grained control you have over an MRU sequence in `scene.mru`: motivation is usually written a rung out, reaction a rung in.
**Best in:** formats: novel, short story, long-form non-fiction | genres: literary, thriller, horror, romance
**Avoid when:** The scene is short and functional. A 200-word errand scene that zooms in and out twice reads as fussy. The same holds across a whole short story: there the full wide-close-wide arc is too much for the form, and the slide stays inside the two-rung band `pov.short_form_pov` sets.
**Example:**
> The hall had been repainted since the last inspection. Forty-one candidates sat at forty-one desks. Reza found his name on the third row and sat down and read the first question and read it again and the letters were fine, the letters were letters, he simply could not make them say anything.

**Yields to:** `pov.short_form_pov` — A short story, or a short functional scene; hold a two-rung band.  
`pov.distance_slide`

### Free Indirect Style

**Also called:** free indirect discourse, FID, erlebte Rede, style indirect libre, the intimate third
**What it is:** Third person narration that takes on the character's idiom, judgements and syntax without quotation marks or a "she thought" tag, so the narrator's voice and the character's voice run in the same sentence.
**Effect on the audience:** Double vision. The reader is inside the character's opinion *and* can feel the narrator's slight distance from it, which is where nearly all literary irony comes from.
**Used for and where it works best:** The workhorse of close third since Austen, and the single most useful technique in this file. Reach for it whenever a thought would be flattened by tagging. The craft rules: drop the tag and the filter verb; keep the past tense and the third person but let the *deixis* go present ("tomorrow", "this", "now"); and let rhetorical questions and exclamations through, since they are the clearest markers. It is also the cheapest source of irony you have, because the narrator's judgement lives in the gap between the character's phrasing and the observable facts.
**Best in:** formats: novel, short story, comics, long-form non-fiction | genres: literary, comedy of manners, romance, satire, crime
**Avoid when:** The character's judgement and the narrator's are identical and correct. Then FID has no gap to work in and just reads as flat narration. Also avoid in scenes with two equally live consciousnesses in close quarters — that is where writers slide into `pov.head_hopping` without noticing.
**Example:**
> Tagged: *She thought the party had been a disaster and that Meera had been unforgivably rude.*
> Free indirect: *A disaster, the whole evening. And Meera — well. Some things you did not do to a friend, not in front of people, not twice.*

**Yields to:** `pov.omniscient` — Two equally live consciousnesses in one scene.  
`pov.free_indirect_style`

### Cutting the Filter

**Also called:** deep POV, removing filter words, unmediated perception, dropping the perceiver
**What it is:** Deleting the verbs that report perception — saw, heard, felt, noticed, realised, watched, seemed, decided — so the perception arrives directly instead of being announced.
**Effect on the audience:** The reader stops watching a character perceive and perceives alongside her. It is the single fastest way to gain a rung of closeness without rewriting anything structurally.
**Used for and where it works best:** Run it as a pass, not as a rule, over any scene that feels distant when you wanted it close. The craft rule: keep the filter verb exactly when the *act of noticing* is the event — when a character realises something, "she realised" may be the beat. Delete it in all the other ninety cases. This is also the cleanest fix for a scene that has good material and reads flat.
**Best in:** formats: novel, short story, game narrative | genres: thriller, horror, romance, YA, literary
**Avoid when:** You are in distant third or omniscient on purpose. There, the filter verb is doing honest work of holding the reader out; stripping it collapses your chosen distance without permission.
**Example:**
> Filtered: *She noticed that the back door was open and she felt a rush of cold and she realised someone had been inside.*
> Cut: *The back door stood open. Cold moved through the kitchen, along the floor, over her bare feet. Someone had been inside.*

**Yields to:** `pov.distant_third` — Distance is deliberate; the filter verb is working.  
`pov.filter_word_removal`

### The Retrospective Narrator

**Also called:** distance in time, the older self, narrating I versus experiencing I, hindsight narration
**What it is:** A first person narrator telling events from a point long after them, so two consciousnesses are present: the one living it and the one who now knows how it ended.
**Effect on the audience:** Built-in dramatic irony and built-in elegy. Every scene is shadowed by the fact that someone survived to narrate it, and by what they now think of themselves.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when the *meaning* of the events was not available at the time — childhood, war, a marriage, a crime. The craft rule is to decide how wide the gap is and how often you cross it. A narrator who interrupts every page with "of course, I know now" kills suspense; a narrator who never does has wasted the frame. Three or four deliberate crossings in a novel, placed at turns, is usually the right dose. It is the cleanest legitimate route to `info.dramatic_irony` inside a single POV.
**Best in:** formats: novel, memoir, short story, narrated film, podcast | genres: literary, historical, war, coming-of-age, crime
**Avoid when:** The book's engine is *will she survive*. Retrospect answers that on page one. Also avoid when the older self is simply wiser and kinder than the younger — that flatters and bores. The older self should be implicated.
**Example:**
> I was fourteen and I thought my father was a coward. I want to be careful here, because I have had thirty years to be fair to him and I have used almost none of them.

**Yields to:** `pov.tense_as_distance` — "will she survive" is the live question.  
`pov.retrospective_distance`

## Architecture and the Contract

### The POV Contract

**Also called:** the rules of the telling, viewpoint discipline, the narrative pact
**What it is:** The set of promises your first twenty pages make about who can narrate, how close, how reliably, and with what access to other minds — promises the reader then uses to interpret everything after.
**Effect on the audience:** Invisible when kept, loud when broken. A reader cannot articulate the contract but detects a violation within a sentence and registers it as the writing getting worse.
**Used for and where it works best:** Write the contract down before drafting: number of POVs, person, tense, distance range, who gets interiority, whether the narrator addresses the reader, whether the narrator knows the ending. The craft rule that matters most: **establish every element of the contract early and cheaply**. A second POV that first appears on page 300 reads as a rescue; the same POV introduced on page 40 and left dormant reads as design. `open.character_hook` is where most of the contract is actually signed, before the reader knows it is being signed.
**Best in:** formats: novel, series, screenplay, game, serialised fiction | genres: all
**Avoid when:** Never avoid having one. Avoid only the version that is unwritten and therefore inconsistent by accident.
**Example:**
> Page 3, contract signed in one line: *I did not see the fire start. I have had to build that part from what the neighbours told the inquest, and the neighbours lied.*

**Yields to:** `pov.short_form_pov` — No room to establish a contract; sentence one is it.  
`pov.contract`

### The Licensed Break

**Also called:** the earned violation, the single exception, the authorised POV break
**What it is:** Deliberately breaking your own POV rule once, at a maximum-pressure moment, having engineered the break to carry meaning.
**Effect on the audience:** A shock of form. Because the reader has internalised the contract, the break itself becomes content — it says *this moment is outside the world you have been reading*.
**Used for and where it works best:** The classics: a single omniscient paragraph in a tightly limited novel at the death of the protagonist; one chapter in second person in a third person book; the first-person narrator who admits, once, that he has been lying about the form itself. The craft rules are strict — **once per book, at a turn, and the break must be about the same thing the story is about.** A POV break that is merely convenient is not licensed, it is `pov.head_hopping` with a good publicist.
**Best in:** formats: novel, film, literary short story, limited series | genres: literary, horror, speculative, crime
**Avoid when:** You have broken the contract elsewhere by accident. The licensed break only reads as authored against a spotless record. Also avoid in genre where the contract is part of the product — a cosy mystery that suddenly enters the killer's head has spoiled its own game.
**Example:**
> For four hundred pages we have been only in Ana's head. Then: *She did not see the second car. In eleven days her sister would stand in this road and count the skid marks and get the number wrong, and go on getting it wrong for the rest of her life.*

**Yields to:** `pov.multi_pov_architecture` — Contract not spotless; build the viewpoint in.  
`pov.licensed_break`

### Multi-POV Architecture

**Also called:** ensemble POV, rotating viewpoints, braided narration, multiple third
**What it is:** A book built from two or more viewpoint characters, each owning whole scenes or chapters, arranged in a deliberate pattern of rotation and weight.
**Effect on the audience:** Breadth, and a particular engine of suspense: the reader assembles a picture none of the characters has. Cutting away from a hot thread also creates the appetite that pulls a reader through the cold one.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when the story's question cannot be answered inside one life — a war, a conspiracy, a marriage from both sides, a city. The craft rules, in order of how often they are broken: (1) every POV must have its own want and its own arc, not merely a camera position; (2) weight the rotation — a lead POV with 60% of the pages and two at 20% reads better than a democratic split, which flattens hierarchy and tells the reader no one matters most; (3) return to a POV before the reader has forgotten its stakes, roughly within two chapters; (4) each POV needs a distinguishable perceptual signature, best built from `char.what_they_notice` rather than from dialect. The reader's real question at every switch is *why am I here now*, and the answer must be that this POV knows or wants something the last one could not.
**Best in:** formats: novel, series, limited series | genres: fantasy, thriller, historical, literary, SF, romance (dual POV), epic
**Avoid when:** One character's ignorance is the point. Adding the second POV to "show the other side" is the most common way writers destroy their own suspense: the moment the reader has both halves, `info.restricted_narration` is gone and the book becomes a waiting game.
**Example:**
> End of chapter 9, Marek's POV: *He decided he would tell her on Sunday.*
> Start of chapter 10, Hana's POV: *On Friday she began packing the flat while he was at work.*

**Yields to:** `pov.close_third` — One character's ignorance is the point.  
`pov.multi_pov_architecture`

### Scene Ownership

**Also called:** whose scene is it, viewpoint assignment, the POV audition
**What it is:** The per-scene decision about which available character narrates, made on what the scene needs rather than on whose turn it is.
**Effect on the audience:** It determines what the scene is *about*. The same events narrated by the other person in the room are a different scene with a different meaning.
**Used for and where it works best:** Audition every significant scene. The three questions, in priority order: **who has the most to lose here** — usually decisive; **who will be changed by this** — because a scene should turn a value for its owner, per `struct.mckee_scene`; and **whose ignorance is most useful**, because putting us in the head of the person who does not know keeps the reader in suspense rather than in dread. The strongest counterintuitive move in this file: give the big revelation scene to the character *receiving* it, not the one delivering it — the deliverer knows already, so there is nothing to watch.
**Best in:** formats: novel, screenplay, series, comics | genres: all narrative
**Avoid when:** You are rotating on a fixed pattern for structural reasons (alternating dual POV romance, for instance). Then ownership is pre-assigned and your job is to make sure the scene you place there is one that character can actually carry — move the *scene*, not the POV.
**Example:**
> Wrong owner: the detective's POV as she announces the arrest — she has known for forty pages.
> Right owner: the brother's POV as he works out, one sentence too late, what the detective's questions have been for.

**Yields to:** `pov.multi_pov_architecture` — A fixed rotation pre-assigns the owner.  
`pov.scene_ownership`

### The POV Handoff

**Also called:** the viewpoint switch, the POV signal, chapter-break transition
**What it is:** The mechanics of moving from one viewpoint character to the next — where the seam falls and how fast the reader is re-oriented.
**Effect on the audience:** Done well, it is frictionless and the reader never consciously registers a change of mind. Done badly, it costs a paragraph of confusion at exactly the moment a new scene needs momentum.
**Used for and where it works best:** Put the seam at a chapter or section break, never mid-scene. Then **name the new POV character in the first sentence, and anchor them in the first three** — name, place, and one perception only they would have. Resist the fashionable cold open that withholds who we are with for half a page; it buys mystery you did not need and spends clarity you did. Named chapter headers ("HANA") are a legitimate tool and no admission of failure, but they do not excuse a vague opening line — a reader who has to check the header has already stumbled.
**Best in:** formats: novel, series, comics, screenplay | genres: thriller, fantasy, romance, literary
**Avoid when:** Two POVs share almost every scene. Then the handoffs come so often that the breaks fragment the book; collapse to one POV or restructure so each owns distinct territory.
**Example:**
> Weak: *The room was cold. Someone had left the window open again.* (Who is this?)
> Strong: *Idris had asked them twice about the window. It was open again.*

**Yields to:** `pov.multi_pov_architecture` — Two POVs share every scene; give each territory.  
`pov.handoff`

### The Same Event Twice

**Also called:** the Rashomon structure, contested account, replayed scene, double vision
**What it is:** Narrating one event from two or more viewpoints, so the reader compares incompatible accounts of the same hour.
**Effect on the audience:** Epistemological unease — the reader stops asking what happened and starts asking who is capable of seeing what. Also, at a smaller scale, pure delicious irony when the second account reveals how badly the first one misread a gesture.
**Used for and where it works best:** Two very different uses. The full structure — a whole novel or film built on competing accounts — is for stories about testimony, memory and guilt, and it needs the accounts to differ in *interpretation*, not just in facts, or the reader simply solves it. The miniature version is more broadly useful: replay only ninety seconds of a scene from the other side, once, to detonate a misunderstanding. The craft rule for the miniature is that the second pass must add a turn, never merely re-describe; cut everything the reader already has and start at the first divergence. Note this is the honest way to spend `info.delayed_decoding` across two POVs.
**Best in:** formats: novel, film, limited series, short story cycle | genres: literary, crime, courtroom, historical, romance
**Avoid when:** Momentum matters more than depth. Every replay stops the clock, and in a thriller's third act that is fatal. Also avoid when the accounts differ only trivially — the reader feels the repetition as a tax.
**Example:**
> First pass, his POV: *She would not look at him. Fine. He could do silence too.*
> Second pass, hers: *If she looked at him she was going to cry in front of his mother, and she had promised herself she would not do that again.*

**Yields to:** `pov.multi_pov_architecture` — Momentum matters; show the other side forward.  
`pov.same_event_twice`

## POV Outside Prose

### POV Without Interiority

**Also called:** screenwriting POV, the exterior contract, filmable viewpoint
**What it is:** In a screenplay, the writer has no access to thought. Viewpoint is built from what the camera is with, what the audience is allowed to know, and what characters do — not from narration.
**Effect on the audience:** The audience infers interiority from behaviour and therefore believes it more than any narrated version.
**Used for and where it works best:** Build screen POV from three levers. **Attachment** — whose scenes exist at all; if we never see a scene without Marie, we are in Marie's POV whether or not a camera is on her shoulder. **Access** — does the audience learn a fact before, with, or after the protagonist; that choice alone creates suspense, surprise or mystery. **Alignment** — reaction shots, since whoever the camera returns to for reaction is who the scene belongs to. The craft rule for the page: never write a thought a camera cannot photograph. *He remembers the war* is unfilmable; *He rolls his sleeve down over the scar before the waiter comes back* is the same information, shot.
**Best in:** formats: screenplay, teleplay, stage play, feature film, storyboards | genres: all screen
**Avoid when:** Never avoided, but resist solving the problem with voiceover by default. Voiceover is legitimate — it is simply a different POV device with its own contract, and it must do something the images cannot rather than narrate them.
**Example:**
> Unfilmable: *SARA realises he has been lying to her for months.*
> Filmable: *SARA looks at the receipt. She looks at the date on it. She puts it back in his jacket, exactly where it was, and smooths the pocket flat.*

**Yields to:** `expo.character_voiceover` — Interiority the camera cannot photograph, when VO earns it.  
`pov.screen_no_interiority`

### Caption Against Panel

**Also called:** comics POV, the caption box, image-text counterpoint
**What it is:** In comics, two POV channels run at once: the drawn panel, which is an external camera, and the caption box, which can carry a character's interiority. The relationship between them is the technique.
**Effect on the audience:** When caption and panel agree, the reader gets reinforcement. When they disagree, the reader gets irony, dread or comedy instantly, with no authorial comment required.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use counterpoint deliberately: a narrator's confident caption over a panel that shows him wrong is the most efficient unreliable-narrator device in any medium. The craft rules: keep one caption voice per sequence or the reader loses the speaker; let the panel carry all information it can carry, and caption only what the drawing cannot say; and remember the panel border is a POV instrument — a close-up of hands *is* a closer psychic distance. Comics also have a true first-person camera (the POV panel drawn from behind or through a character's eyes), which is powerful and, like its film equivalent, exhausting past a few panels.
**Best in:** formats: comics, graphic novel, webtoon, picture book | genres: superhero, memoir, horror, literary
**Avoid when:** The caption merely describes the panel. That is the medium's version of on-the-nose dialogue and it deadens the page.
**Example:**
> Caption: *I had it under control.*
> Panel: the same man asleep in a parked car at noon, three phones lit up on the passenger seat.

`pov.comics_caption_panel`

### The Playable Viewpoint

**Also called:** game POV, first-person versus third-person camera, player-character distance, ludic POV
**What it is:** In games, POV is split in two: the *camera* (first person, over-the-shoulder, isometric, top-down) and the *authorship* of the protagonist (silent avatar the player projects onto, versus a named character with fixed opinions).
**Effect on the audience:** The camera sets embodiment; the authorship sets whether the player is *being* someone or *directing* someone. Mismatch them and the player feels a low-grade friction they will call "the writing".
**Used for and where it works best:** Choose along one axis: how much do you need the player to *be* this person. A first-person camera on a silent protagonist maximises identification and is the only configuration where a horror game's fear is genuinely the player's. A third-person camera with a fully voiced character buys you drama, a face, and reaction shots, at the cost of identification. The craft rule unique to the medium: **the player's knowledge and the character's knowledge desynchronise constantly** — the player has replayed the level eleven times — so write reveals that survive repetition, and never make a scene depend on the protagonist being surprised by something the player solved an hour ago.
**Best in:** formats: video game, VR, interactive fiction, immersive theatre | genres: horror, adventure
**Avoid when:** You want the protagonist to do something the player would refuse. In a cutscene a character can betray a friend; in player control, a player will sit there and decline, and the authored POV breaks.
**Example:**
> Silent avatar: the radio says *"Talk to me, are you still alive down there?"* and the silence that follows is the player's own.
> Voiced character: *"I'm alive. Mostly. Tell my sister that if you find her before I do."*

**Yields to:** `pov.screen_no_interiority` — Protagonist must act against the player's will - author the cutscene.  
`pov.playable_pov`

### The Reporter's Stance

**Also called:** documentary POV, nonfiction viewpoint, the observer's I, reportage distance
**What it is:** In documentary and journalism, the choice of whether the maker appears — as an on-screen presence, an "I" in the copy, an audible question off-camera — or is effaced behind an apparently neutral record.
**Effect on the audience:** It sets who the audience trusts. An invisible narrator offers authority; a visible one offers honesty about the frame and invites the viewer into the process.
**Used for and where it works best:** Declare the stance in the first two minutes or the first three paragraphs and hold it. The craft rule: **effacement is a rhetorical choice, not the absence of one** — an invisible reporter is still selecting, and when the selection later becomes visible the audience feels manipulated. So make yourself visible whenever your presence changed what happened, whenever you have a stake, or whenever the subject's willingness to talk to you is part of the story. Stay out of it when the subject's testimony is stronger unmediated, and when your presence would be the most interesting thing on screen, which is usually a sign you have the wrong subject.
**Best in:** formats: documentary, journalism, podcast, essay film, heritage video | genres: true crime, biography, travel, personal essay
**Avoid when:** The subject is grief or atrocity and your reactions would recruit the audience's attention away from the people it belongs to. A first person that turns a mass grave into a passage about the writer's feelings is the profession's oldest failure.
**Example:**
> Effaced: *The factory closed in March. Four hundred people lost their jobs.*
> Visible: *I asked him what he did the morning the factory closed. He said he got up at five anyway, the way he had for nineteen years, and I understood that he was still doing it.*

`pov.reportage_stance`

### POV in Translation

**Also called:** translated viewpoint, pronoun drift, FID across languages
**What it is:** The way a translation can shift narrative distance without shifting a single plot point, because the target language handles pronouns, honorifics, tense and reported thought differently from the source.
**Effect on the audience:** Readers of the translation may experience a systematically warmer or colder book than the original, without anything identifiable being wrong.
**Used for and where it works best:** Attend to it whenever you are translating fiction, writing for translation, or adapting across languages. The specific traps: free indirect style relies on a tense-and-deixis trick that languages implement differently, and a translator who tags the thought ("she thought that...") to disambiguate has silently moved the book a rung outward for its entire length. Pro-drop languages let a narrator stay closer with fewer pronouns; forcing explicit subjects in English adds distance. Honorific systems encode the observer's social position, and flattening them removes a POV signal the source used for characterisation. The craft rule: translate *distance* as a target to hit, the same way you would translate register — decide the rung first, then choose the constructions that land on it.
**Best in:** formats: novel, subtitles, dubbing | genres: literary
**Avoid when:** Nothing to avoid; the risk is not noticing. Where you must trade, protect distance over literal syntax — readers forgive an unliteral clause and never forgive a book that feels cold.
**Example:**
> Close (FID preserved): *And why should she apologise? Let them wait.*
> Distanced by over-tagging: *She wondered why she should apologise and thought that they could wait.*

`pov.translation_shift`

## Unusual Owners

### The Child Viewpoint

**Also called:** child narrator, the innocent eye, juvenile POV
**What it is:** A story narrated or focalised through a child, whose vocabulary, priorities and causal model differ systematically from the adult reader's.
**Effect on the audience:** Constant unforced irony. The child reports accurately and concludes wrongly, and the reader does both jobs — receiving the data and supplying the meaning the child cannot.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it for subjects that would be unbearable or sentimental head-on: abuse, war, divorce, poverty, death. The craft rules: (1) the child's *perception* may be adult-sharp, but the child's *interpretation* must stay age-honest — that gap is the whole technique and it overlaps with `info.naive_narrator`; (2) never let the vocabulary be cute; a child narrator written for charm is a puppet; (3) keep the child's actual priorities — a nine-year-old at a funeral is thinking about whether there will be food, and that is truer and sadder than grief would be; (4) decide whether an older self is also present, per `pov.retrospective_distance`, since child-narrated-now and child-remembered-later are different books.
**Best in:** formats: novel, short story, film, memoir, picture book | genres: literary, war, historical, horror, coming-of-age
**Avoid when:** The plot requires competent agency across an adult world. A child protagonist who negotiates, investigates and travels freely stops being a child and the irony evaporates.
**Example:**
> My mother was asleep on the kitchen floor again, so I made the toast myself and I did not burn it, and when she woke up I told her and she cried, which I did not expect, because I had done it right.

**Yields to:** `pov.retrospective_distance` — Plot needs adult competence; use the older self.  
`pov.child_pov`

### Non-Human Viewpoint

**Also called:** animal POV, object narrator, the it-narrative, machine POV
**What it is:** The viewpoint belongs to a dog, a tree, a coin, a house, an AI — an entity whose perceptual apparatus and value system are not human.
**Effect on the audience:** Defamiliarisation. Human behaviour described by something that does not share human assumptions is exposed, and often devastating or very funny.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when the target is human habit itself, or when you want grief and cruelty seen without the consolations of human framing. The craft rule that separates the good from the whimsical: **commit to a different sensorium and a different value hierarchy, and let the language be the only concession.** A dog narrator who sees the world in smell and ranks events by proximity to its person is a POV; a dog narrator with human opinions and a paw-based vocabulary is a costume. Objects need a governing constraint — a coin sees only transactions, a house sees only what happens indoors — and that constraint is what makes the book a book rather than a stunt.
**Best in:** formats: novel, short story, picture book, animation, advertising | genres: literary, fable, SF, children's, satire
**Avoid when:** The story needs the audience to feel a character's moral responsibility. Non-human POV tends to suspend blame, which is sometimes the point and sometimes the problem.
**Example:**
> The man came back three times that week, which was unusual, and each time he smelled less like the house and more like the other place, and on the third time he did not take off his coat.

**Yields to:** `pov.first_person_central` — Audience must feel moral responsibility.  
`pov.nonhuman_pov`

### The Institutional Viewpoint

**Also called:** POV of a place, corporate narrator, the building's eye, systemic narration
**What it is:** The viewpoint belongs to a place or an organisation — a hospital, a village, a company, a river — reporting in the register that institution uses about itself, with its characteristic blind spots.
**Effect on the audience:** The reader feels the shape of a system that no individual inside it can perceive, and the specific horror of decisions with no author.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when the antagonist is a process rather than a person: medicine, bureaucracy, colonisation, war logistics, a family's unwritten rules. The craft rule: **borrow the institution's own genres.** The minutes, the incident report, the tour script, the annual review, the rota. The gap between the institution's vocabulary and the human event it is documenting does all the work without a single line of commentary. Close cousin of `pov.collective_we`, but colder — a "we" has memory and shame; an institution has only records.
**Best in:** formats: novel, short story, documentary, stage play | genres: literary, satire, horror, historical, speculative
**Avoid when:** You want the reader to love someone. Institutions cannot love and cannot be loved, and a whole book at this altitude is admired more often than it is finished. Most writers should use it for one chapter, in counterpoint.
**Example:**
> Bed 14 was cleaned and remade within forty minutes, which is within target. The family were shown to the relatives' room, which is also within target, and were offered tea, and the audit records that they declined.

**Yields to:** `pov.collective_we` — You need memory, shame, someone to love.  
`pov.institutional_pov`

---

### The Found Document

**Also called:** epistolary, dossier novel, the discovered manuscript, archival frame
**What it is:** The narrative is presented as existing artefacts — letters, diaries, transcripts, emails, case files — with the implication that someone gathered them.
**Effect on the audience:** Authenticity and dread in equal measure. The reader knows the documents survived, does not know who assembled them, and reads every gap as meaningful.
**Used for and where it works best:** Excellent for horror (records end abruptly), for crime (contested testimony), and for any story where the *act of documentation* is part of the subject. The craft rules: every document must have a plausible reason to exist and a specific intended reader, because who a letter is addressed to controls what it can say — this is the form's engine, and the built-in reason each account is partial. Vary the document types so the reader can date and place each one instantly. And decide who compiled the file: an unsignalled compiler is a wasted layer, a signalled one — a preface, a footnote, a final archivist's note — recontextualises everything at a stroke. Naturally hospitable to `info.unreliable_narrator`, since a letter-writer is performing for a known audience.
**Best in:** formats: novel, short story, podcast, game, ARG, film (screenlife) | genres: horror, crime, romance, speculative, historical
**Avoid when:** You need a sustained action sequence. Nobody writes a letter mid-chase, and the form's standard fix — writing it up afterwards — drains the tension it is supposed to be reporting.
**Example:**
> *Voice memo, 02:14, recovered from the handset.* — "I'm going to say this quickly because the battery's — look, if you're listening to this, don't come to the house. Don't. Go to my sister's and tell her I said the word we agreed."

**Yields to:** `pov.close_third` — A sustained action sequence needs live scene.  
`pov.found_document`

## The Failure Modes

### Head-Hopping

**Also called:** POV bouncing, head jumping, mid-scene viewpoint switching
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A limited POV that changes owner mid-scene without a break or a governing narrator, so the reader is dropped from one interior into another and back.
**Effect on the audience:** Seasickness and, more damagingly, a collapse of investment: if the reader can have anyone's thoughts for the asking, no one's thoughts are worth much. Editors register it within a page as an unpublished writer's tell.
**Used for and where it works best:** It doesn't. The confusion to clear up is that omniscience is *not* head-hopping. Omniscience has one narrator with one voice who reports many minds from above, consistently, from the first page. Head-hopping is close third that keeps falling over — the giveaway is that the narrating voice changes to match whichever head it is in. If you want multiple interiors in one scene, you have three legitimate options: pick an owner and infer the rest from behaviour, break the scene, or write genuine omniscience and establish it in chapter one.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always. The one place it survives is older category romance, where a house style permitted alternating interiors within a love scene; even there it now reads as dated.
**Example:**
> Head-hopping: *She hated how he chewed. He wondered if she'd noticed the ring box in his pocket. The waiter, bored, thought they looked like a couple in their last month.*
> Owned: *She hated how he chewed. He kept one hand in his jacket pocket the whole meal, and when the waiter came he snapped at him, which he never did.*

**Yields to:** `pov.omniscient` — Many interiors in one scene, done legitimately.  
`pov.head_hopping`

---

### The Impossible Observation

**Also called:** POV slippage, camera drift, the unearned detail
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** In a limited POV, a single detail the viewpoint character could not have perceived or known — an expression behind her, the temperature of another room, what a stranger is thinking, her own face.
**Effect on the audience:** A hairline crack. One instance is barely conscious; a pattern of them tells the reader the writer is not actually inside the character, and the whole POV goes provisional.
**Used for and where it works best:** It doesn't. Run the permission test on every descriptive line of a close-third or first-person scene. The usual offenders, in order: characters watching their own faces without a reflective surface; "unbeknownst to her"; a reaction shot behind the POV character's back; and weather or geography that is really the author's establishing shot. The fix is almost always to convert the fact into an inference the character makes, which is better writing anyway because inference characterises and description does not.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always in limited POV. It is not an error in omniscience — but "I meant it as omniscient" is only true if the rest of the book agrees.
**Example:**
> Impossible: *She walked out, her face pale with fury. Behind her, Daniel smiled to himself.*
> Legal: *She walked out. She could feel her own face doing something she would not have chosen. Behind her, nothing — no chair, no footstep, not even the decency of him getting up.*

**Yields to:** `pov.omniscient` — You want the unearned detail; commit to it.  
`pov.impossible_observation`

---

### The Mirror Self-Description

**Also called:** the reflection dump, the mirror cliché, appearance inventory
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A first person or close third narrator inspects herself in a mirror, window or spoon so the writer can deliver hair, eye colour, height and build in one block.
**Effect on the audience:** An immediate signal of an inexperienced hand, and worse, a lie about attention: nobody catalogues their own face except in a specific and rare mental state. The reader feels the author reaching past the character.
**Used for and where it works best:** It doesn't — but the *need* behind it is real, and the fixes are known. Let appearance arrive through friction (sleeves too short, a stranger's assumption about where she is from, the nurse asking her to step on the scale), through other people's reactions, or through what she does to change it. And ask whether the reader needs the inventory at all; readers build faces fine from three chosen details, and a mirror gives them a police description that erases the picture they had already made.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always, with one earned exception: the mirror scene in which the point is *not* the description but the looking — dissociation, illness, ageing, a face after violence. Then the mirror is the event, and no inventory is needed.
**Example:**
> Dump: *I looked in the mirror at my long red hair and green eyes and the freckles I've always hated.*
> Earned: *The bathroom mirror had my mother in it again. Round the mouth mostly. I have been finding her there since about March.*

**Yields to:** `char.physical_signature` — The reader needs a body, not an inventory.  
`pov.mirror_description`

---

### The Inert Viewpoint Character

**Also called:** the passive POV, the tourist protagonist, the camera on legs
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A viewpoint character who is present at the story's events but neither causes them nor is changed by them — chosen for access rather than for stake.
**Effect on the audience:** Flatness that readers misdiagnose as slow plot. Things are happening; the reader does not care, because the mind they are inside does not need anything.
**Used for and where it works best:** It doesn't. It is the standard consequence of assigning scenes by convenience — "he's the one who would be in the room" — instead of by `pov.scene_ownership`. Diagnose it by asking, for each POV scene: what does this character *want in this scene*, what do they do to get it, and what is different for them at the end. Three blanks means the wrong owner. Note the distinction from `pov.peripheral_narrator`, which is a legitimate design: the witness narrator does not drive the plot, but he has a stake in the subject and he is changed by watching. Remove the stake and the witness becomes furniture.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always. If you cannot give the observer a want, give the scene to someone who has one, or cut the scene — an event nobody in it needs is usually not a scene.
**Example:**
> Inert: *Yusuf watched them argue about the money. It went on for a long time. Eventually Aram left.*
> Staked: *Yusuf watched them argue about money that was, although neither of them had said so yet, his. Every minute they spent on it was a minute nobody had to ask him where it came from.*

**Yields to:** `pov.scene_ownership` — Observer wants nothing; reassign by stake.  
`pov.inert_viewpoint`
