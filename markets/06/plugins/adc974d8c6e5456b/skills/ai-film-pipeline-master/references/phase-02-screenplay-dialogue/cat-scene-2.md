## Openings and closings by type

### The Orientation Beat

**Also called:** the anchoring line, the one establishing move you are allowed
**What it is:** A single line — of action, of description, of sound — placed at the top of a scene whose only job is to tell the audience where they are, who is present and roughly when. One line. Not a paragraph, not an establishing sequence.
**Effect on the audience:** Free attention. An audience that is quietly asking "where are we?" cannot listen, and the cost of answering that question is one line against the entire scene.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any work that changes location often — prose with many settings, series television, anything cutting between timelines. It is the necessary counterweight to `scene.late_entry`: cutting every arrival is right, cutting every anchor is how readers get lost. The craft rule is that the anchor must also do a second job. "The kitchen, four in the morning, the good plates still out from yesterday" locates and characterises in the same breath, and costs nothing more than "INT. KITCHEN - NIGHT".
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Feature Film, Audio Drama | genres: all narrative
**Avoid when:** The location is unchanged from the previous scene, or the audience can infer it from the first line of dialogue. An anchor that confirms what the audience already knows is the slowest possible opening, and in audio drama it is the difference between orientation and narration.
**Example:**
> **Flat:** The kitchen. It is very early. Nadia is making tea.
> **Working:** Four in the morning and the good plates are still on the table from yesterday. Nadia puts the kettle on anyway.

**Yields to:** `scene.late_entry` — Location unchanged or inferable.  
`scene.orientation_beat`

### Opening on an Object

**Also called:** the object-first scene opening, the cold prop
**What it is:** The scene opens on a thing rather than a person — a form half filled in, a suitcase by a door, a plate of food nobody has touched, a phone face down — and the people enter the scene second.
**Effect on the audience:** They read the situation before they are told it, which makes them feel clever and buys goodwill. An object also sets tone faster than a face, because it carries no performance to interpret.
**Used for and where it works best:** Scenes whose content is a decision already made off-page. The object states the outcome and the dialogue can then be about anything else, which is the cheapest route to subtext in the craft. Choose an object that would be unremarkable anywhere else in the story; the meaning must come from its position in this scene, not from the object announcing itself. In prose it also solves the paragraph-of-description problem: one object beats three sentences of room.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Short Film, Novel, Short Story, One-Hour Drama, Comics, Vertical Micro-Drama | genres: Drama, Crime, Family Drama, Thriller, Heritage, Psychological, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** Used as a default. A draft where every third scene opens on a significant object develops a rhythm the audience starts predicting, and the objects begin to feel curated rather than found. Also avoid when the object's meaning only becomes clear pages later — an opening image the audience cannot read at all is atmosphere, not information.
**Example:**
> Two cups. One has been drunk. The other has gone cold with the spoon still in it.
> Then his voice from the other room: "Did you find it?"

**Yields to:** `scene.opening_on_reaction` — The object would be habit, or is illegible.  
`scene.opening_on_object`

### Opening on the Reaction

**Also called:** the reaction-first entry, showing the blast not the bomb
**What it is:** The scene opens on somebody's response to an event the audience has not seen — a woman coming out of a door and sitting down on the step, a man laughing alone, a room of people not speaking — and the event is reconstructed afterwards or never.
**Effect on the audience:** Immediate forward lean. A visible reaction with an invisible cause is the smallest complete mystery available, and it resolves within the scene, so it costs no patience.
**Used for and where it works best:** The scene after something enormous. It is almost always stronger than dramatising the enormous thing itself, because a reaction is specific to a person while an event is only specific to a plot. It is the right choice whenever you cannot afford the big scene — a verdict, a death, a wedding — and it is the standard economy of low-budget and AI-generated production, where the crowd you cannot stage is the crowd you cut to a face outside. Related to `scene.entering_on_the_answer`, but this one uses behaviour rather than dialogue, so it works in silence.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Short Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Vertical Micro-Drama, Reels/Shorts, Animation Feature | genres: Drama, Tragedy, War, Family Drama, Crime, Heritage, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The audience needed to witness the event to believe the reaction, or when the event is the story's one promised spectacle. Skipping the thing you spent an act promising reads as a budget decision even when it is an artistic one.
**Example:**
> She comes out of the consulting room, walks past the lifts, past reception, out to the car park, and starts putting coins in the ticket machine before she remembers she came by bus.

**Yields to:** `scene.set_piece_entrance` — The event is the promised spectacle.  
`scene.opening_on_reaction`

### The Button

**Also called:** the out line, the capper, the scene button
**What it is:** The scene's last line or last action, written to snap the scene shut — short, unanswered, and usually given to the character who has just lost. Nothing follows it.
**Effect on the audience:** Punctuation. A button tells the audience the scene is complete without the characters having to agree that it is, and it makes the cut feel authored rather than arbitrary.
**Used for and where it works best:** Comedy, where it is mandatory, and any dramatic scene ending on a reversal. Two rules make buttons work. The first is length: the last line is the shortest line in the scene. The second is who gets it — give it to the person with less power and the scene closes on resistance rather than on victory, which is more interesting and leaves the conflict alive. Pair with `scene.early_exit`; the button is what you keep when you cut the wind-down.
**Best in:** formats: Half-Hour Comedy, One-Hour Drama, Feature Film, Vertical Micro-Drama, Reels/Shorts, Stage Play, Comics | genres: Comedy, Crime, Drama, Thriller, Romance, Social Drama, Family Drama
**Avoid when:** The scene is a sequel, a grief scene or a held ending — see `scene.held_exit`. A button on a death scene is a joke about a death. Also avoid buttoning every scene in a drama: a script where each scene ends on a zinger reads as a series of sketches, and the audience stops believing anyone means anything.
**Example:**
> "So that's a no."
> "That's a no."
> "Then I'll ask someone who liked him."

**Yields to:** `scene.held_exit` — where the scene is a sequel, a grief scene or a held ending. A button on a death scene is a joke about a death.  
`scene.button`

### Closing on the Question

**Also called:** the open close, the handed-forward scene
**What it is:** The scene ends on the moment its own question becomes urgent rather than on the answer. The last beat is a question asked, a door opening, a name said, a phone still ringing — and the answer arrives, if at all, in a later scene.
**Effect on the audience:** The gap does the work. Attention is held by the unanswered, and a scene that closes open is carried into the next one by the audience for free.
**Used for and where it works best:** Serialised work above everything: episodic television, vertical micro-drama, chaptered prose, anything with a break where the audience decides whether to continue. The craft rule that separates it from a cheat: the question must be one the scene raised honestly and could have answered. Withholding an answer the scene never had is not suspense, it is nothing. Track this across the draft with the scene-question audit in `scene.scene_question`, and with `seq.sequence_question` at the level above.
**Best in:** formats: Vertical Micro-Drama, Limited Series, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Reels/Shorts, Podcast, Game Narrative | genres: Thriller, Crime, Family Drama, Romance, Psychological, Social Drama, Science Fiction
**Avoid when:** Two or three scenes in a row have already closed open. Unanswered questions accumulate, and past about three live ones the audience stops tracking and starts waiting, which feels identical to boredom from the inside.
**Example:**
> "Who told you I was here?"
> She looks at him. The kettle clicks off.
> **[end of episode]**

**Yields to:** `scene.button` — Three open closes in a row.  
`scene.close_on_question`

### The Wind-Down Close

**Also called:** the handshake ending, the mutual agreement close
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** The scene turns, and then continues for half a page while the characters acknowledge the turn, agree on what it meant, arrange when to speak next, and say goodbye. The last quarter of the scene contains no new information and no new pressure.
**Effect on the audience:** Deflation, and a learned habit of stopping listening. Once an audience discovers that the ends of scenes are safe, they disengage at the first sign of agreement, and they take that habit into the scenes that need them.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere as a default. The one legitimate relative is the deliberately held ending, which is a different technique with a different purpose — see `scene.held_exit`. Everything else this produces is a scene that ends twice.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always, with one exception: a scene whose subject is the aftermath itself, written deliberately long — see `scene.held_exit`. The test is whether the extra material is characters *doing* something or characters *confirming* something. Confirmation is the failure. The repair is mechanical: find the value turn, keep one beat past it, delete to the end of the scene, and check whether anything you deleted was load-bearing. Ninety per cent of the time nothing was.
**Example:**
> **Written:** "You knew." / "I knew." / "Why didn't you say something?" / "I wanted to." / "Okay." / "Are you going to tell Mum?" / "I don't know yet." / "Call me tomorrow." / "I will."
> **Kept:** "You knew." / "I knew."

**Not a choice:** this is a **named failure**, not an option. It is reached by the **Avoid when** line of the card you did choose, never by a selector, because a selector that listed it would be offering to fail. It sits on the `scene.` shelf and is not of it.  
**Yields to:** `scene.held_exit` — the single exception, where the aftermath itself is the subject and the extra length is characters *doing* rather than *confirming*.  
`scene.wind_down_close`

## The montage, the crosscut and the parallel scene

### The Montage With a Turn

**Also called:** the dramatic montage, the montage that is a scene
**What it is:** A montage built so that a value stands at one charge in its first image and the opposite charge in its last — the training works, the marriage cools, the business fills up and then empties. The sequence of images is not a list; it is a single scene compressed.
**Effect on the audience:** Time passes and a dramatic value changes, felt simultaneously. A montage without a value turn can still be a valid declared process or time summary, but the audience experiences it as connective information rather than as a compressed dramatic scene.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any span the story must cross but not dramatise — recovery, courtship, decline, preparation, an occupation, a year. The rule that makes it work is to write the first and last images before any of the middle ones, and to make them the same frame with one thing different: the same shop counter full and empty, the same two people at the same table closer and further apart. See `seq.montage_sequence` for the sequence-level version and `time.montage` for its treatment as a time device.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Animation Feature, One-Hour Drama, Reels/Shorts, Documentary, Vertical Micro-Drama, Short Film | genres: Coming of Age, Romance, Sports, Drama, Social Drama, Heritage, War
**Avoid when:** The span contains the story's real decisions. A montage cannot hold a choice, because a choice needs a person weighing, and a montage has no room for weighing. Also avoid in prose, where the equivalent is summary — see `time.summary_duration` — and where a list of images reads as a writer skimming.
**Example:**
> **First image:** he counts the till and there is not enough, so he puts in his own money.
> **Last image:** he counts the till, there is not enough, and he closes it and turns off the light.

**Yields to:** `time.summary_duration` — in prose, where the equivalent of a montage is summary and a list of images reads as skimming.  
`scene.montage_value_turn`

### One Theme Per Montage

**Also called:** the single-idea rule, montage focus
**What it is:** Every image in a montage serves one idea. If the montage is about money running out, no image is about the marriage; if it is about the marriage, no image is about money.
**Effect on the audience:** Legibility at speed. A montage gives each image about two seconds, which is not long enough for the audience to sort two ideas, so a mixed montage reads as noise and they disengage until the music stops.
**Used for and where it works best:** Every montage, but it is most needed in the ones that cover a long span, because long spans tempt the writer into covering everything that happened. The discipline is to name the idea in four words before writing a single image, then to write eight images and cut the three that serve a different idea — they are usually the three you liked best. If two ideas genuinely need covering, that is two montages with something between them, not one montage with both.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Reels/Shorts, Animation Feature, Documentary, One-Hour Drama, Vertical Micro-Drama | genres: all narrative
**Avoid when:** The montage's subject is precisely the multiplicity — a day in a city, a family of many people, the breadth of a movement. There the images are meant to be plural, and the unifying element becomes place, time or a repeated action rather than a single idea.
**Example:**
> **Idea: he stops being trusted.**
> Keys taken off his ring. His name crossed off the rota. The stockroom code changed while he watches.
> *Cut the shot of his wife on the phone. It belongs to a different montage.*

`scene.montage_single_theme`

### The Montage That Hides a Scene

**Also called:** the avoidance montage, montage as skip button
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A montage placed over material that is actually dramatic — the argument, the courtship, the decision, the investigation — because the writer could not find the scene. The music covers it, and the audience is shown the outside of an event whose inside was the story.
**Effect on the audience:** A hole where a scene should be, usually experienced later rather than at the time: the audience believes the relationship, the recovery or the betrayal is unearned, and they cannot say why. They cannot say why because it was never dramatised, only summarised with a good song over it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere. A montage is a legitimate instrument for spans that contain no choices — see `scene.montage_value_turn` — and this is what happens when it is asked to cover a span that does. The two are told apart by one question, not by how the montage is written.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always. The diagnostic is one question: does anyone in this montage make a choice? If yes, it is not a montage, it is a scene you have compressed to avoid writing. The most common offender is the falling-in-love montage in a romance, which places the entire emotional argument of the film behind a wall of images. The repair is to keep the montage and extract one moment from it — the one where a choice is made — and give that moment thirty seconds of actual scene.
**Example:**
> **The hole:** three months of the investigation as a montage of pinboards and coffee, then he announces he has found the pattern.
> **The repair:** the same montage, interrupted once — sixty seconds in which he decides not to tell his partner what he has found.

**Not a choice:** this is a **named failure**, not an option. It is reached by the **Avoid when** line of the card you did choose, never by a selector, because a selector that listed it would be offering to fail. It sits on the `scene.` shelf and is not of it.  
**Yields to:** `scene.montage_value_turn` — The span contains no choice.  
`scene.montage_as_avoidance`

### The Crosscut Pair

**Also called:** parallel scenes, intercutting, cross-cutting at scene level
**What it is:** Two scenes in different places, each of which would work alone, broken into fragments and alternated so the audience experiences them as one scene happening in two rooms.
**Effect on the audience:** Comparison they perform themselves. Once two things are cut together, an audience will insist they are about each other, which is a meaning-making reflex the writer gets for free and cannot switch off.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any two scenes that share a clock, a subject or an outcome — a negotiation and a raid, a wedding and a funeral, a mother waiting and a son arriving. The structural rule most drafts miss: both strands must be able to stand as scenes on their own before you cut them together. Crosscutting is a multiplier, and two weak scenes cut together produce confusion at twice the speed. On the page, establish each location as a full scene first and only then intercut — a reader who meets a location in fragments never builds it. See `seq.crosscut_sequence` for the larger unit and `time.parallel_now` for simultaneity as a time device.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Novel, Reels/Shorts, Game Narrative, Animation Feature | genres: Thriller, Crime, War, Drama, Social Drama, Heritage, Science Fiction
**Avoid when:** The two strands carry different levels of tension. Cutting from a life-and-death strand to an administrative one does not raise the administrative strand, it kills the other one — the audience waits through the weaker material, which converts suspense into impatience.
**Example:**
> Her side: the taxi at the second checkpoint, engine off.
> His side: the hall filling, the chairs in rows, his mother keeping the seat beside her.
> Neither strand ever mentions the other.

**Yields to:** `scene.storyline_handoff` — Strands of unequal tension.  
`scene.crosscut_pair`

### The Ironic Parallel

**Also called:** the contrast cut, cutting against the content
**What it is:** A crosscut whose two strands say opposite things at the same moment — a celebration and a discovery, a promise and its breaking, a prayer and a theft — so the meaning is produced by the join rather than by either scene.
**Effect on the audience:** Dread or bitterness, depending on the order. The audience holds both strands and sees the collision coming before the characters do, which is `info.dramatic_irony` delivered structurally instead of through dialogue.
**Used for and where it works best:** Endings of acts, and any theme you refuse to state out loud — see `theme.explained_theme` for why you should refuse. It is the most efficient thematic device available to a screenwriter, because it argues without anyone in the film holding the opinion. The craft rule is restraint at the join: do not let either strand comment on the other, and do not add a line that notices the irony. If a character notices, the device is dead.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Novel, Short Film, Documentary | genres: Tragedy, Crime, War, Social Drama, Political, Drama, Heritage
**Avoid when:** The contrast is obvious and moral — the rich man's dinner cut against the poor man's hunger. Obvious irony reads as the writer instructing the audience, and an audience being instructed stops feeling — in a piece that refuses to state its theme; where the piece is openly arguing, as political film, social documentary and charity advertising do, the single hard cut between the two worlds is `scene.rich_to_poor_contrast`, used once. The strongest ironic parallels are ones where the two strands are both sympathetic.
**Example:**
> They are singing for him at the table, all six of them, when the candles go out.
> Across the city he is signing the form that puts the house in someone else's name.

**Yields to:** `scene.crosscut_pair` — The contrast would be obvious and moral; and `scene.rich_to_poor_contrast` where the piece is openly arguing and needs a direct polemical comparison rather than restrained irony.  
`scene.ironic_parallel`

### Establish, Then Accelerate

**Also called:** the shortening crosscut, fragment length as tempo
**What it is:** A crosscut in which the fragments get progressively shorter as the sequence advances — a page each, then half a page, then three lines, then one — so that speed is produced by the cutting rate rather than by the content.
**Effect on the audience:** Physical acceleration. Fragment length is felt as heart rate, and an audience will experience a shortening crosscut as urgency even when nothing in the content has intensified.
**Used for and where it works best:** Climaxes, convergences, any sequence where two lines must meet. The rule that most drafts break: do not start fast. A crosscut that begins with one-line fragments has nowhere to go and flattens into a strobe within twenty seconds. Start long enough that the audience settles into each location, then take the room away. Pair with `rhythm.length_as_tempo`, which is the same principle at sentence scale.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Reels/Shorts, Game Narrative, Animation Feature, Vertical Micro-Drama | genres: Thriller, Crime, War, Action, Horror, Science Fiction, Sports
**Avoid when:** The sequence's meaning depends on a decision. Acceleration removes the space in which a character can weigh anything, so a shortening crosscut arriving at a choice produces a character who is swept rather than one who chooses.
**Example:**
> **Fragment 1:** a full half page in the stairwell.
> **Fragment 4:** "Third floor."
> **Fragment 7:** the door.
> **Fragment 8:** the door.

**Yields to:** `scene.reaction_dilemma_decision` — The sequence arrives at a choice.  
`scene.crosscut_acceleration`

## Silence and the scene without conflict

### The Silent Scene

**Also called:** the wordless scene, the dialogue-free scene
**What it is:** A complete scene — with a want, an obstacle and a turn — conducted with no dialogue at all. The turn is carried by action, object, sound and position.
**Effect on the audience:** Heightened attention and a shift in how they watch. Deprived of language, an audience reads bodies, and they read them far more carefully than they listen.
**Used for and where it works best:** Placed immediately after a heavily verbal scene, where the contrast does most of the work. It is also the strongest available answer to a scene whose dialogue you cannot make honest — grief, shame, reconciliation between people who do not have the words. The rule that makes a silent scene a scene and not a mood: it still needs a want you could write in five words, and the audience must be able to name it. Silence removes the dialogue, not the structure. Use `sub.silence_as_content` for silence inside a spoken scene, and `beat.silent_beat` for the one-beat version.
**Best in:** formats: Short Film, Feature Film, Animation Feature, Reels/Shorts, Comics, One-Hour Drama, Vertical Micro-Drama | genres: Drama, Family Drama, Heritage, Tragedy, War, Coming of Age, Horror
**Avoid when:** The scene carries plot the audience must understand precisely. Silent scenes are excellent at emotion and unreliable at information: audiences routinely misread who did what in a wordless scene, and a plot point delivered silently is a plot point half the audience will not have. Also avoid in audio formats, obviously, and in dialogue-driven comedy, where the removal of language removes the instrument.
**Example:**
> He wants the photograph back. She is washing up.
> He dries. He stacks. He stands in the doorway with the tea towel still in his hand. She turns the tap off, dries her hands, goes to the drawer, and hands him the whole envelope.

**Yields to:** `sub.silence_as_content` — The scene carries plot that must be precise.  
`scene.silent_scene`

### Channel Discipline in a Wordless Scene

**Also called:** the two-channel rule, selective visual storytelling
**What it is:** In a scene without dialogue, choose two information channels to carry the meaning — say, hands and sound, or position and one repeated object — and let everything else stay neutral. Do not use faces, weather, music, props, blocking and light all at once.
**Effect on the audience:** Clarity. A wordless scene running six expressive channels at once reads as ambiguity, because the audience has no way to know which one to follow, and ambiguity in silence is indistinguishable from emptiness.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any silent scene longer than about forty seconds, and all wordless animation, where the temptation to over-signal is strongest. The practical method: before writing, list the channels available — hands, eyes, distance between bodies, an object, sound, breath, entrances — pick two, and write the whole scene through those. The restriction is what makes the scene legible; it also makes it repeatable, so the second use of the same channel reads as a rhyme.
**Best in:** formats: Short Film, Animation Feature, Comics, Feature Film, Reels/Shorts, Vertical Micro-Drama | genres: Drama, Family Drama, Coming of Age, Heritage, Horror, Tragedy, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The scene is chaos by design — a crowd, a panic, a disaster. There the incoherence of channels is the content, and discipline would make the chaos look organised.
**Example:**
> **Channels: hands, and the sound of the ward.**
> Her hands smooth the sheet. Straighten it. Smooth it again. A trolley goes past. A phone somewhere. Her hands stop.
> *Her face is never described.*

`scene.wordless_channel_discipline`

### The Kishōtenketsu Scene

**Also called:** the four-part scene, ki-shō-ten-ketsu at scene scale, the conflict-free scene
**What it is:** A scene built in four movements rather than on opposition: **ki**, a situation is introduced; **shō**, it is developed without complication; **ten**, something enters that does not belong to the pattern — a new element, an image, a memory, a change of place; **ketsu**, the two halves are held together and the first half now means something different.
**Effect on the audience:** Recognition rather than resolution. The pleasure is not that a contest was settled but that a frame was moved, and the audience re-reads the previous two minutes themselves.
**Used for and where it works best:** Scenes in which nobody can want anything — a character alone, a landscape, a memory, a piece of heritage or documentary material, a children's story, a four-panel comic strip, a short reflective reel. It is the honest alternative to `scene.give_a_want` when a want would be false. The rule is that the **ten** must not be a conflict and must not be explained: it is placed, and the ketsu simply lets it stand beside what came before. If a character arrives to argue, you have written a Western scene with an unusual shape.
**Best in:** formats: Short Film, Animation Feature, Comics, Short Story, Documentary, Reels/Shorts, Novel | genres: Heritage, Family Drama, Coming of Age, Drama, Literary, Historical, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The story is running on an engine of escalating opposition, and this scene sits in the middle of it. A conflict-free scene inside a thriller's second act reads as a stall, however well built. Also avoid using it to excuse a scene that failed the dead scene test — Kishōtenketsu is a structure, not a permission slip.
**Example:**
> **Ki:** the old man opens the shop, as he has for forty years.
> **Shō:** he sets out the chairs, the glasses, the sugar, in the same order.
> **Ten:** a school bus stops outside and, for a moment, the window is full of children's faces.
> **Ketsu:** he sets out one more glass than he needs, and sits down.

**Yields to:** `scene.give_a_want` — The story runs on escalating opposition.  
`scene.kishotenketsu_scene`

### The Development Scene

**Also called:** the shō scene, the deepening scene, the scene that does not turn
**What it is:** A scene whose work is to deepen a situation the audience already has, without changing its charge — more of the same seen more closely, more precisely, more specifically. Nothing reverses; the resolution increases.
**Effect on the audience:** Immersion and trust. A story that occasionally stops advancing in order to be exact about something earns the right to be believed when it does advance.
**Used for and where it works best:** Heritage and documentary work, literary prose, slow-burn drama, and the second movement of any Kishōtenketsu structure. It is also the correct answer to a draft where everything turns: fifteen consecutive reversals produce the same monotony as fifteen consecutive flat scenes, and a development scene is the rest that makes the next turn audible. The rule that keeps it from becoming a dead scene: it must deliver something the audience did not have — a detail, a procedure, a rule of the world, a texture — and it must be shorter than the scenes around it.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Documentary, Short Story, Feature Film, Limited Series, Animation Feature, Podcast | genres: Heritage, Historical, Literary, Family Drama, Social Drama, Coming of Age, War
**Avoid when:** The draft is already slow, or the audience has no question live. A development scene works only when something is pending; with nothing pending it is exactly the dead scene that `scene.dead_scene_test` is built to catch, and the shape gives you no defence.
**Example:**
> Nothing changes between them. She shows him how her mother pinned the cloth — the order of the folds, the pin held in the mouth, the half-turn at the shoulder — and he watches, and gets it wrong, and watches again.

**Yields to:** `scene.value_turn` — Nothing is pending; the scene must turn.  
`scene.development_scene`

### The Meaningful Look

**Also called:** the silent scene with nothing in it, two people feeling at each other
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A scene written without dialogue in which nothing happens except faces: they look at each other, something passes between them, she looks away, he almost says something. The stage directions describe emotions rather than actions.
**Effect on the audience:** Nothing, delivered slowly. On the page a reader skims it; on screen it becomes whatever the actors invent, which means the scene was not written, it was commissioned.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere, and it is not the same thing as a silent scene. `scene.silent_scene` removes the dialogue and keeps the structure; this removes both and leaves a mood note for the actors to solve.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always, and it is worth learning to catch it in your own drafts, because it feels like restraint while you are writing it. The tell is in the verbs: if the action lines contain *realises*, *understands*, *something passes between them*, *she softens*, you have written interiority you have no access to. The repair is `scene.wordless_channel_discipline` plus one physical want — give somebody an object to get, a door to close, a task to finish — and let the feeling happen against the task, as in `emo.emotion_through_task`.
**Example:**
> **Written:** They look at each other for a long moment. Everything they have not said is in the room. She almost speaks. He nods, understanding.
> **Repaired:** She picks up his coat, and holds it out, and keeps holding it out after he has taken hold of the other sleeve.

**Not a choice:** this is a **named failure**, not an option. It is reached by the **Avoid when** line of the card you did choose, never by a selector, because a selector that listed it would be offering to fail. It sits on the `scene.` shelf and is not of it.  
**Yields to:** `scene.wordless_channel_discipline` and `emo.emotion_through_task` — always, because this card is a named failure and those two are its repair: two channels, and a physical want to play the feeling against.  
`scene.meaningful_look`

## Who is in the room

### The Two-Hander

**Also called:** the duet scene, the two-person scene
**What it is:** A scene with exactly two people and no interruption, sustained for longer than the surrounding scenes, in which the entire content is what passes between them.
**Effect on the audience:** Concentration. With two people there is nowhere for the audience's attention to go, so everything is magnified — every tactic, every pause, every failure to answer.
**Used for and where it works best:** The scenes a story exists to arrive at: the confrontation, the confession, the negotiation, the last conversation. Three things keep a long two-hander alive, and they are the three most drafts are missing. First, both characters want something and the wants are incompatible — not the same want at different intensities. Second, tactics change at least three times, per `scene.tactic_change`. Third, the room does something: a trap (`scene.trap_scene`), a clock (`scene.ticking_scene`) or a barrier (`scene.barrier_mid_scene`) so the scene is not two people who could leave. See also `rel.locked_pair`.
**Best in:** formats: Stage Play, Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Audio Drama, Short Film, Podcast | genres: Drama, Family Drama, Crime, Romance, Tragedy, Psychological, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The characters are not yet distinct enough to hold a page without attribution. A two-hander is the format that most brutally exposes a writer with one voice, and the diagnostic is to cover the character names and read: if you cannot tell who is speaking, fix the characters before writing the scene.
**Example:**
> Forty minutes, one table, no interruption. He wants her to sign. She wants to know what he told the police. Neither says either thing until minute thirty-one.

`scene.two_hander`

### The Ensemble Scene

**Also called:** the group scene, the table scene, the many-bodied scene
**What it is:** A scene with four or more characters present, organised so that the audience can follow it: a scene-level protagonist whose want drives it, a small number of speaking positions, and everyone else present and reacting rather than talking.
**Effect on the audience:** Social pressure and the pleasure of watching alliances form. A group scene done well is the only place where a story's whole world is visible at once.
**Used for and where it works best:** Family meals, meetings, funerals, weddings, briefings, classrooms — anywhere the story's community must be felt as a unit. The rule that makes the difference: a group scene is not a group conversation. Give one character the want, give one other the power to refuse, and give everyone else a position — who they will side with when it comes to it. A room of six with two negotiating and four choosing sides is legible; a room of six all making points is soup. Use `rel.ensemble_scene_legibility` for the character-level machinery and `rel.pair_off_the_room` when a large scene needs breaking up.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Stage Play, Novel, Half-Hour Comedy, Animation Feature | genres: Family Drama, Social Drama, Comedy, Heritage, Historical, Political, War
**Avoid when:** Vertical and short-form work, where a frame cannot hold four legible people and an audience scrolling has no time to sort them. Also avoid in early scenes of any work — a group scene before the audience knows names is a room of strangers arguing, which nobody can follow and nobody enjoys.
**Example:**
> Nine at the table. Only two of them are having the conversation — the uncle and the eldest son, about the land. The other seven are deciding, in silence and in the order they pass the bread, which of the two they are with.

**Yields to:** `scene.two_hander` — Vertical cannot hold four legible people.  
`scene.ensemble_scene`

### The Scene With a Child Present

**Also called:** the witness child, playing it over their head
**What it is:** An adult scene conducted with a child in the room — playing, half-listening, doing homework, asleep on someone's lap — and the adults adjusting everything they say because of it.
**Effect on the audience:** Constraint that reads as tenderness and threat at once. The audience watches two things: what the adults are doing to each other, and what it is costing the child to be present for it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Family drama, migration and heritage stories, divorce, poverty, war. It produces subtext automatically and for free, because the adults must encode; it is `scene.public_scene` with the most sympathetic possible audience inside the room. Two craft rules. The child must be doing something specific and ordinary, not watching with wide eyes. And the child's presence must change at least one line — if the scene would run identically without them, they are a prop. For a scene from inside the child's understanding, see `pov.child_pov`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Short Film, Limited Series, Animation Feature, Stage Play | genres: Family Drama, Social Drama, Heritage, War, Coming of Age, Drama, Tragedy
**Avoid when:** The child is used as an emotional multiplier on a scene that does not otherwise work. An audience recognises a child deployed for leverage, and the resentment is durable. Also avoid making the child the wisest person present, which is the genre's oldest and least convincing move.
**Example:**
> "We're not going to talk about this now."
> "We're talking about it now."
> From the floor, without looking up: "Can I have the blue one?"
> "Yes, habibi." Then, to him, in exactly the same voice: "Not in front of her. I mean it."

**Yields to:** `scene.public_scene` — The child would be leverage; use another witness.  
`scene.child_present`

### Played in Front of a Stranger

**Also called:** the indifferent witness, the third person who does not care
**What it is:** A private scene conducted in the presence of somebody who has no stake in it and no relationship to the participants — a taxi driver, a waiter, a nurse, a clerk, a barber — who continues their work throughout.
**Effect on the audience:** Exposure and shame. Being overheard by a stranger is a different register from being overheard by family: family judges, a stranger simply exists, and existing is worse, because it makes the participants ordinary.
**Used for and where it works best:** Scenes of humiliation, collapse, or a confession that cannot be taken back. The stranger's indifference is the technique's whole engine — they should never react, never comment, never become a character. The most effective version gives them a routine they complete on schedule regardless of what is happening: the barber finishes the haircut, the driver takes the correct route, the nurse changes the dressing. When their task ends, the scene ends. Distinct from `scene.third_party_arrival`, where the third party has a stake and changes what is possible.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Short Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Short Story, Vertical Micro-Drama, Stage Play | genres: Drama, Social Drama, Crime, Family Drama, Tragedy, Psychological, Comedy
**Avoid when:** You give the stranger a line of wisdom at the end. The single most common failure of this shape is the writer rewarding the stranger with a summarising remark, which converts an indifferent world into a commenting one and cancels the effect in a sentence.
**Example:**
> He tells her in the back of the taxi, because it is the only place they have been alone in a week. The driver turns the radio up two notches and does not look in the mirror once for eleven minutes.

**Yields to:** `scene.third_party_arrival` — The third person has a stake.  
`scene.before_a_stranger`

### The Unaware Participant

**Also called:** the character who does not know they are in the scene, the one-sided scene
**What it is:** A scene in which one character knows what is being decided and the other does not — a job interview the candidate thinks is a coffee, a goodbye the other person thinks is an ordinary Tuesday, an interrogation conducted as a friendly chat.
**Effect on the audience:** Sharp, sustained dread, produced entirely by asymmetry. The audience is aligned with whoever knows, and watches the other person do ordinary things that are now unbearable.
**Used for and where it works best:** Anywhere a betrayal, a decision or a diagnosis exists before it is spoken. It is one of the purest applications of `info.dramatic_irony`, and it is stronger than a confrontation of the same material, because the person in the dark is free to be charming, generous or trivial — and every such moment tightens the screw. The craft rule: the unaware character must be doing something good-natured. If they are unpleasant, the audience stops dreading and starts waiting for them to get it.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Limited Series, Short Film, Vertical Micro-Drama, Audio Drama | genres: Thriller, Crime, Drama, Tragedy, Family Drama, Psychological, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The audience does not yet know what the knowing character knows. Without that, it is just a pleasant conversation; the technique is powered by the audience's knowledge, not the character's. Withhold it and you have written a scene that only works on the second read.
**Example:**
> She has the letter in her bag. He spends the whole lunch planning the summer — the coast road, the house his cousin has, which week is cheaper — and asks her twice to check the dates.

**Yields to:** `scene.two_hander` — The audience is not ahead; play the confrontation.  
`scene.unaware_participant`

### Everyone Speaks

**Also called:** the round-robin scene, giving each character a line
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A group scene in which every present character gets dialogue, usually in rotation, usually because the writer feels each of them should be served. Nobody is silent, and nobody is listening.
**Effect on the audience:** They stop tracking. Four or more active speakers exceeds what an audience can follow without visual aids, so they retain the loudest line and lose the scene — and because everyone participated, no alliance is visible and no power is legible.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere. The legitimate version of the impulse is `scene.ensemble_scene`, which serves every character present by giving them a position rather than a line, and `rel.rotating_spotlight`, which serves them across scenes instead of within one.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always, and the pressure to do it comes from cast management rather than from the story: a writer protecting an actor, a series regular, or a character they like. The repair is to decide who the scene belongs to, cut every other speaking part to two lines or none, and give the silent characters positions instead of dialogue — where they are standing, who they look at, who they hand something to. A character who says nothing and moves across the room is more present than one who gets a line.
**Example:**
> **Written:** eight characters, twenty-two lines, everybody heard from, nothing decided.
> **Repaired:** the same eight. The aunt speaks nine times, the father twice, the rest not at all — and by the end, four of them have moved to the aunt's side of the room.

**Not a choice:** this is a **named failure**, not an option. It is reached by the **Avoid when** line of the card you did choose, never by a selector, because a selector that listed it would be offering to fail. It sits on the `scene.` shelf and is not of it.  
**Yields to:** `scene.ensemble_scene` — Give positions, not lines.  
`scene.everyone_speaks`

## Across distance and language

### The Phone Call Split Across Locations

**Also called:** the intercut call, the two-room scene
**What it is:** One scene conducted by two characters in two places, cut together so the call is a single dramatic unit — with a want, an obstacle and a turn — rather than two people relaying information.
**Effect on the audience:** Two rooms at once, and everything each character's room says about them that their voice does not. The gap between what someone says on the phone and what their room shows is the cheapest irony in the medium.
**Used for and where it works best:** Scenes between people who cannot be together — migration stories, separation, prison, hospital, war, distance of any kind. The rule that makes a phone scene a scene: something must be visible in one location that the other speaker does not know about. Without that, a call is a delivery mechanism, and dialogue over a cut is just dialogue. On the page, establish both locations properly before intercutting, and never write a call in which both parties are cooperative and honest.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Novel, Vertical Micro-Drama, Audio Drama, Reels/Shorts | genres: Family Drama, Migration, Crime, Thriller, Romance, War, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The two characters could plausibly be in the same room. Writers reach for phone calls to avoid staging, and a call between people who live in the same city reads as convenience. Also avoid long calls in short-form, where two locations and two faces exceed the frame's carrying capacity.
**Example:**
> Her side: "It's fine. Everything's fine." Behind her, the flat is empty except for the mattress.
> His side: "Say hello to your mother." Behind him, her mother is standing in the doorway, listening.

**Yields to:** `scene.one_sided_call` — Short-form, or the far end better unseen.  
`scene.phone_call_split`

### The One-Sided Call

**Also called:** the half-heard conversation, the single-ended call
**What it is:** A call in which the audience hears and sees only one participant. The other end is inferred entirely from the pauses, the answers and the speaker's face.
**Effect on the audience:** Active listening. An audience given half a conversation builds the other half, and what they build is more specific and more frightening than anything that could have been written for it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Bad news, threats, and any call whose content the audience must feel rather than know. It is much stronger than the intercut version when the other party is an institution, a stranger or an antagonist who is better left unseen. Two craft rules. Never write the pauses as *(listening)* — write what the speaker does in the gap, because the gap is the performance. And do not let the speaker repeat back what they were told; a character who says "he's *where*?" is transcribing for the audience.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Stage Play, Novel, Short Film, Vertical Micro-Drama, Reels/Shorts | genres: Drama, Thriller, Crime, Family Drama, Horror, Tragedy, Psychological
**Avoid when:** The information on the other end is precise and plot-critical — an address, a name, a number. Inference is unreliable for detail. Also avoid in audio drama, where a one-sided call and a monologue sound identical.
**Example:**
> "Yes."
> She sits down on the arm of the chair.
> "No, I can come now."
> She looks at the clock, then at the front door, and does not get up.
> "No. Don't call her. I'll call her."

**Yields to:** `scene.phone_call_split` — The far end carries precise plot.  
`scene.one_sided_call`

### The Scene in a Moving Vehicle

**Also called:** the car scene, the journey two-hander
**What it is:** A scene conducted in a moving car, bus, train or boat, where the participants are side by side, cannot leave, and one of them is occupied with driving.
**Effect on the audience:** Intimacy without confrontation. Two people who are not looking at each other say things they could not say face to face, and the audience registers the difference immediately.
**Used for and where it works best:** The scene where something long-avoided is finally said. It combines four pressures at once: nobody can leave (`scene.trap_scene`), the destination is a clock (`scene.ticking_scene`), eye contact is rationed, and one party has a task that licenses silence. This is why so many decisive scenes in film happen in cars, and it is unusually cheap to produce, which makes it a first-choice transplant for low-budget and AI-generated work — see `scene.transplant`. The craft rule: the driving must matter. A junction, a wrong turn, a car behind them, pulling over — the vehicle should intervene at least once, or the location is just a sofa with a windscreen.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Short Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Vertical Micro-Drama, Reels/Shorts | genres: Drama, Family Drama, Crime, Coming of Age, Thriller, Romance, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The scene needs bodies — somebody standing up, walking out, touching someone, being blocked at a door. A car forbids all of it. Also avoid stacking two car scenes near each other; the shape is distinctive enough that the second one reads as a house style.
**Example:**
> She asks him at the lights, because at the lights he has to look forward. He answers at the next set. Between them he indicates, changes lane, and says nothing for a mile and a half.

**Yields to:** `scene.threshold_scene` — The scene needs bodies, doors, blocking.  
`scene.moving_vehicle`

### The Scene Conducted in a Second Language

**Also called:** the foreign-language scene, the subtitled scene, the scene in the other tongue
**What it is:** A scene in which the characters speak a language that is not the work's primary language — or in which one character is operating in their second language and the other is not — and the difficulty of speaking is part of the scene's content.
**Effect on the audience:** Altered status, instantly legible. Whoever is in their own language is larger, and whoever is borrowing one is smaller, whatever the dialogue says.
**Used for and where it works best:** Migration, heritage, occupation, exile, mixed families, any story where power and language travel together. The craft rule that separates real writing from decoration: the second-language speaker should not be written with broken grammar but with a smaller vocabulary and a slower route to the point — competent people in a second language sound simple, not stupid. The reverse case is equally useful: a character who becomes funny, cruel or fluent the moment they switch back into their own language tells the audience what has been lost. Use `voice.second_language_voice` for the line-level craft, `voice.code_switching` for the switch itself, and `sub.across_languages` for subtext that must survive translation.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Novel, Short Film, One-Hour Drama, Documentary, Stage Play | genres: Migration, Heritage, Family Drama, Social Drama, War, Historical, Drama
**Avoid when:** The scene's plot content is heavy and the audience is reading subtitles. Reading costs attention, so subtitled scenes should be simpler than the scenes around them — one idea, fewer speakers, shorter lines. Also avoid if the only reason for the language is texture; audiences can tell decoration from necessity, and in heritage work they are unusually unforgiving about it.
**Example:**
> In English, to the housing officer: "The letter came. I am asking about the letter. It says the date." Careful, slow, twice as polite as necessary.
> In the corridor afterwards, to her son, in Arabic: a single sentence, fast, with a joke in it, and she is a different person for the length of it.

**Yields to:** `voice.second_language_voice` — Plot-heavy scene under subtitles.  
`scene.second_language_scene`

### The Untranslated Line

**Also called:** the unsubtitled beat, deliberate withholding of translation
**What it is:** One or two lines in a scene are deliberately left untranslated — no subtitle, no gloss, no paraphrase — so that part of the audience understands and part does not.
**Effect on the audience:** Split. Speakers of the language receive the line; everyone else receives the experience of being outside it, which is the scene's subject delivered directly rather than depicted.
**Used for and where it works best:** Exactly one kind of moment: when exclusion is the point. A prayer, a curse, a joke at the outsider's expense, a private endearment, a phrase with no equivalent. It works because the untranslated portion is short and its *function* is clear from context even when its content is not — the audience must know that a blessing was given, even if they do not know its words. Get this wrong and you have simply withheld information.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Short Film, Limited Series, Novel, Documentary, One-Hour Drama | genres: Heritage, Migration, War, Social Drama, Historical, Family Drama, Drama
**Avoid when:** The line carries plot, or when there is more than one of them in a scene. Also avoid on platforms where captions are auto-generated and burned in — the decision will be overridden by the platform, and in vertical and social distribution it almost always is.
**Example:**
> The officer asks her to say it in English. She says it in Syriac instead, once, looking at her son.
> No subtitle. Her son's face does the translating, and does not do it well.

**Yields to:** `scene.second_language_scene` — The line carries plot, or captions are forced.  
`scene.untranslated_line`

## Length and the single action

### Length Follows Value Size

**Also called:** scene duration proportional to the turn, the length rule
**What it is:** A scene's length is set by how large a value it moves, not by how much material the writer has. A small turn gets a short scene; the largest turn in the story gets the longest scene in the story.
**Effect on the audience:** Correct weighting without a word of emphasis. Duration is how an audience learns what matters, and a story where a minor scene runs longest has told them, accurately, that the writer's judgement cannot be trusted.
**Used for and where it works best:** The rewrite, as a whole-draft audit. List every scene with its page count and the size of its turn on a scale of one to three. Anywhere a one-point turn occupies three pages, cut; anywhere a three-point turn occupies half a page, expand or you have crushed the payoff. Contemporary screen scenes average somewhere between one and two and a half pages and have been getting shorter for thirty years; treat anything past three pages as requiring a reason, not as forbidden. See `time.scene_duration` for duration as a time device.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Limited Series, Short Film, Vertical Micro-Drama | genres: all narrative
**Avoid when:** As a rule applied mechanically to comedy, farce or stage work, where a scene's length is governed by the escalation of a joke or by the physical logic of a set. Also note that prose obeys it differently: in a novel, weight is carried by detail density as much as by word count, so a short scene written closely can outweigh a long one written at distance.
**Example:**
> **Audit line:** Scene 34 — 3.5 pages — turn: he decides to take the later train. *Cut to half a page.*
> **Audit line:** Scene 51 — 0.75 pages — turn: she tells him the child is not his. *This is the scene the film is for. Write it properly.*

`scene.length_follows_value`

### The One-Line Scene

**Also called:** the fragment scene, the single-image scene, the flash
**What it is:** A complete scene of one image, one line or one action — six seconds, two sentences — placed between longer scenes.
**Effect on the audience:** Rhythm, and a jolt. After two long scenes, a scene that lasts one beat resets the audience's sense of time and makes the next long scene feel shorter than it is.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any draft with `rhythm.monotone_length` at scene scale — every scene two pages, every scene a conversation. A one-line scene can carry a fact, a passage of time, a joke or a threat, and it is the most efficient available answer to a pacing note. In prose it is the one-line paragraph or the one-line chapter; in vertical drama it is the whole grammar of the form. The rule: it must be a scene, not a shot. Something must be different afterwards, even if what is different is only the audience's information.
**Best in:** formats: Vertical Micro-Drama, Reels/Shorts, Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Comics, One-Hour Drama | genres: Thriller, Crime, Comedy, Drama, Psychological, Horror, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** Several appear together. Three one-line scenes in a row is not rhythm, it is a montage without the honesty of being labelled one, and it usually means a sequence has been cut down rather than written short.
**Example:**
> Scene 40, complete:
> The shop. The shutter half down. A hand-written sign taped to the glass: *CLOSED — ASK NEXT DOOR.*

**Yields to:** `scene.montage_value_turn` — Three in a row: write it as a montage.  
`scene.one_line_scene`

### The Scene That Is One Long Action

**Also called:** the single-task scene, the procedure scene, real time on one job
**What it is:** A scene consisting of one continuous physical action performed from beginning to end — washing a body, building a wall, butchering a lamb, making bread, digging, packing a house — with the emotional content carried entirely by how the task is done and by what interrupts it.
**Effect on the audience:** Absorption, then trust. Watching a task performed correctly by someone who knows how is one of the few pleasures that does not depend on plot, and it makes an audience believe in the world around it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Heritage, historical and documentary work, grief, labour, craft, and the scene after a catastrophe when nobody can speak. The rule is completeness: the task must be shown from start to finish, in the right order, with the real steps, because a task performed wrongly destroys more credibility than it builds. It is also one of the only structures that can hold real time (`time.real_time`) without becoming slow, and it gives grief somewhere to live — see `emo.emotion_through_task` and `sub.hands_business`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Documentary, Short Film, Novel, Animation Feature, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series | genres: Heritage, Historical, Drama, War, Family Drama, Social Drama, Tragedy
**Avoid when:** You have not researched the task. The audience that knows the work will be the audience that cares most, and they will be gone by the third wrong step. Also avoid when the task is generic — cooking an unspecified meal, fixing an unspecified engine. The specificity is the entire technique.
**Example:**
> They wash him in the order the washing is done, and the only dialogue in four minutes is the eldest saying "the other arm" and, later, "the water's gone cold" — and somebody leaving to boil more.

`scene.single_action_scene`

### The Waiting Room Scene

**Also called:** the anteroom, the corridor scene, the scene before the scene
**What it is:** A scene in which nothing can be done because the characters are waiting for something to be decided elsewhere — outside a theatre, a courtroom, an office, a ward, a border post — and the wait itself is the whole content.
**Effect on the audience:** Their own impatience, converted into the scene's tension. Waiting is the one emotion an audience can be made to share exactly, in real time, with the characters.
**Used for and where it works best:** Immediately before a verdict, a birth, a surgery, a decision, a raid — never after. The craft rule that makes it a scene rather than a pause: waiting must not be the characters' only activity. Give them a petty conflict to conduct while they wait — a seat, a phone charger, whose fault it is that they are here, whether to eat — and let the enormous thing appear only once, obliquely, near the end. Small friction in a large wait is how real people behave and it is the only version that does not read as stalling. See `tens.waiting_as_weapon`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Stage Play, Novel, Short Film, Limited Series, Audio Drama | genres: Family Drama, Medical, Drama, Social Drama, Crime, War, Migration
**Avoid when:** The outcome being waited for is not one the audience cares about, or when the wait comes after the audience already knows the answer. A wait whose result the audience holds is not suspense; it is a delay, and they feel it as one.
**Example:**
> Three hours in the corridor. They argue about whether the vending machine took his coin, twice. His brother goes out to smoke four times. Nobody says the word *operation* until the doors open, and then nobody says anything.

**Yields to:** `scene.opening_on_reaction` — The audience already holds the outcome.  
`scene.waiting_room`

## Arrivals, departures and the joins

### The Arrival Scene

**Also called:** the entrance scene, coming back, the homecoming
**What it is:** A scene whose entire content is somebody arriving somewhere — a returning relative, a new colleague, a guest, a stranger — and the place adjusting around them.
**Effect on the audience:** A full map of a social world in one scene. How a room reorganises for an arrival tells the audience who has status, who is afraid, and what the newcomer is to each person, without a line of exposition.
**Used for and where it works best:** First acts, first episodes, and any point where the story's equilibrium must be disturbed. It is the correct exception to `scene.late_entry`: when the arrival *is* the scene, you play the arrival in full. The craft rule is to write the room's behaviour rather than the arriver's feelings — who stands, who does not, who offers what, who was not told they were coming, what gets moved or hidden in the thirty seconds before the door opens. The arriver should be given almost nothing to say.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Novel, Stage Play, Animation Feature, Short Film | genres: Family Drama, Heritage, Drama, Migration, Social Drama, Comedy, Historical
**Avoid when:** The arrival has been anticipated for so long that the scene is a payoff the audience has already written. There, either skip to the aftermath — see `scene.opening_on_reaction` — or make the arrival wrong in a specific way: the wrong time, the wrong person with them, the wrong door.
**Example:**
> Eleven years and they have had two days' notice. His mother has cooked for fifteen. His brother is not there. Nobody mentions that his brother is not there until the plates are down, and then only his aunt, and only once.

**Yields to:** `scene.opening_on_reaction` — where the arrival has been anticipated so long that the scene is a payoff the audience has already written.  
`scene.arrival_scene`

### The Departure Scene

**Also called:** the leaving scene, the goodbye, the last look at the room
**What it is:** A scene whose content is somebody leaving — and, specifically, the practical business of leaving: what is taken, what is left, what is given back, who carries the bag, who stays inside.
**Effect on the audience:** Finality they can measure. Objects leaving a room is the most concrete statement of change available, and it lands harder than any line about it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Act ends, season ends, migration stories, and the second half of any relationship's arc. The rule that distinguishes a good departure from a sentimental one: the emotion goes into the logistics. Who takes the charger. Whether the key is left on the table or posted later. Which photographs are taken out of which frames. The characters should be talking about something trivially practical while the leaving happens — see `dial.trivial_surface` in spirit, and `scene.transaction_scene` for the object mechanics.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Short Film, Limited Series, Stage Play, Animation Feature | genres: Family Drama, Migration, Drama, Romance, Tragedy, Heritage, War
**Avoid when:** Somebody delivers a summarising speech at the door. The door speech is the most reliably fatal move in the form, because it converts everything the story built into a paraphrase of itself. If a character must speak at the door, give them one sentence about something small.
**Example:**
> She takes the pan she brought when she moved in, and the good scissors, and leaves the rest. At the door: "There's chicken in the freezer. Take it out tonight or it won't defrost."

`scene.departure_scene`

### Cause Out, Question In

**Also called:** the therefore join, the scene-to-scene chain test
**What it is:** A test and a repair for the joins between scenes. Every scene must exit on a cause — something that happened — and the next must enter on the consequence or the question that cause produced. If two consecutive scenes can be swapped without damage, there is no join between them, only sequence.
**Effect on the work:** The difference between a story and a list. Causality is felt as inevitability, and inevitability is what audiences call "well structured" without being able to point to anything.
**Used for and where it works best:** The structural pass, and especially on second acts, which are where scenes most often become adjacent rather than consequent. Method: write each scene's exit cause and each next scene's entry question in two columns and read down. Every "and then" is a fault; every "therefore" or "but" is a join. It is the scene-level companion to `scene.scene_question` and sits under `seq.scene_welding` at the sequence level.
**Best in:** formats: all narrative | genres: all narrative
**Avoid when:** Deliberately non-causal structures — mosaic, anthology, portrait, Kishōtenketsu-derived work, documentary built by theme. There, the joins are made by rhyme, subject or contrast, and the causal test will report faults that are features.
**Example:**
> **And then:** Scene 18, she learns her father is ill. Scene 19, she is at work, dealing with an unrelated customer.
> **Therefore:** Scene 18, she learns her father is ill. Scene 19, she is at work asking her manager for three weeks she has not earned.

**Yields to:** `scene.match_join` — Non-causal structures join by rhyme.  
`scene.cause_out_question_in`

### The Match Join

**Also called:** the match cut on the page, the rhymed join, the hinge image
**What it is:** Two consecutive scenes joined by a shared element — the same object, the same gesture, the same line of dialogue, the same shape, the same sound — so the cut carries an argument about the two scenes' relationship.
**Effect on the audience:** An idea delivered in the join rather than in the content. Match joins are how a story says *this is that* without anyone saying it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Across time jumps, between parallel characters, and anywhere a theme needs asserting without a speech. Written into prose it becomes the shared image across a chapter break; in comics it is the shape repeated across the gutter. The craft rule is that the match must make a claim the story would defend. A clever rhyme with no argument behind it is decoration and the audience feels the writer's hand. The strongest version matches an ordinary object across a large gap: the same cup, forty years apart.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Animation Feature, Comics, Novel, Limited Series, Short Film, Documentary | genres: Drama, Heritage, Historical, Tragedy, Coming of Age, War, Psychological
**Avoid when:** More than two or three times in a work. Match joins announce authorship, and a film built on them becomes an essay about its own construction. Also avoid when the two scenes are already obviously related; the device is for connecting things the audience would not otherwise connect.
**Example:**
> Scene ends: the boy's hand on the door handle of the shop, 1961.
> Scene begins: the same handle, the same hand, the same shop, forty years of paint later.

**Yields to:** `scene.cause_out_question_in` — The two scenes are already obviously related.  
`scene.match_join`

### The Pre-lap Join

**Also called:** the overlapping join, sound-first entry, dialogue pre-lap
**What it is:** The next scene's sound — a line of dialogue, a voice, a noise — begins over the end of the current scene, so the audience is already in the new scene before the picture changes.
**Effect on the audience:** Seamlessness and speed. A pre-lapped join removes the small gap in which attention resets, which is why a sequence built on them feels like one continuous piece rather than a series of scenes.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any run of short scenes that must move — investigations, preparations, escalating sequences, the compressed middle of a feature. It is also the correct answer to a sequence where every cut feels like a full stop. The written form is simple: put the incoming line in the outgoing scene, marked as pre-lap or as offscreen sound. The craft rule: pre-lap the line that answers, contradicts or mocks what was just said, because a pre-lap is a join and a join should argue. See `seq.sequence_handoff` for the unit above.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Reels/Shorts, Documentary, Animation Feature | genres: Crime, Thriller, Drama, Comedy, Political, Social Drama, War
**Avoid when:** The outgoing scene needs its silence — after a death, a departure, a held ending. A pre-lap over a held exit cancels the hold, which is the most common way a good ending gets thrown away in the edit. Also avoid as a default rhythm, where it flattens every scene boundary and leaves the audience with no punctuation at all.
**Example:**
> She says nothing, and puts the phone in her coat.
> **Over the last two seconds, her brother's voice:** "—she's not going to say yes, you know that."
> *Cut to: the brother, mid-sentence, in a different city.*

**Yields to:** `scene.held_exit` — where the outgoing scene needs its silence. A pre-lap over a held exit cancels the hold, which is the commonest way a good ending is thrown away in the edit.  
`scene.prelap_join`

## Attacking a scene you have already fixed

### The Third Attack

**Also called:** changing the question, the structural rewrite of a scene
**What it is:** A method for a scene you have rewritten twice and that still does not work. Instead of rewriting the dialogue again, change one structural fact and rewrite from that: who is present, where it happens, who wants it, what is physically at stake, or what has already happened before the scene begins.
**Effect on the work:** It breaks the writer out of the loop that line-editing creates. A scene rewritten three times at the dialogue level is usually the same scene in three costumes, because the fault is never in the lines — the lines are downstream of a structural fact the writer has stopped being able to see.
**Used for and where it works best:** The specific moment when a scene has resisted two passes. The discipline is to change exactly one fact and write the whole scene again from the top, without consulting the previous draft — consulting it re-imports the problem. Run the changes in this order, because it is roughly the order of increasing power: location (`scene.transplant`), agenda (`scene.swap_who_wants`), participants, prior event, and last of all whether the scene should exist at all (`scene.merge_two_scenes`, `scene.absorb_and_delete`).
**Best in:** formats: all narrative | genres: all narrative
**Avoid when:** The scene works and you are attacking it out of anxiety. There is a real and common failure where a writer keeps rewriting a functioning scene until it stops functioning; the test is whether a reader has ever complained about it, or only you.
**Example:**
> **Passes one and two:** the argument in the kitchen, better lines each time, still dead.
> **Third attack, one fact changed:** it is not an argument. He came to return the key and she will not take it. Same information, no kitchen, fourteen lines.

`scene.third_attack`

### Change the Shape, Keep the Content

**Also called:** re-shaping, refitting the scene, shape substitution
**What it is:** Take a failing scene's content — its information, its turn, its relationships — and rebuild it inside a different scene shape: as a transaction, a threshold scene, a public scene, a silent scene, a phone call, a waiting room, a single long action.
**Effect on the work:** A scene that felt obligatory becomes one the audience has not seen, without losing anything the plot required. Most "boring" scenes are not boring in content; they are in the default shape — two people, one room, sitting, talking.
**Used for and where it works best:** Drafts in which several scenes have the same silhouette. The method is fast enough to do in an hour: list every scene's shape in one word, and wherever the same word appears three times in a row, refit the middle one. The rule is that only the shape changes — same turn, same information, same outcome — otherwise you are writing a new scene and will lose the structure you already proved. This is the technique that most reliably makes a low-budget or AI-generated production look expensive, because shape variety reads as coverage variety.
**Best in:** formats: all narrative | genres: all narrative
**Avoid when:** The content is already carried by the shape — a threshold scene about exclusion, a vehicle scene about not looking at each other. Refitting those breaks the marriage of form and content that was doing the work.
**Example:**
> **Shape audit:** scene 21 — two in a room. Scene 22 — two in a room. Scene 23 — two in a room.
> **Refit 22 as a transaction:** the same conversation, conducted while he hands over the boxes from the flat, one at a time, and she checks each one.

**Yields to:** `scene.third_attack` — Form and content already married.  
`scene.change_the_shape`

### The Overworked Scene

**Also called:** the polished corpse, the scene rewritten to death
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A scene that has been rewritten so many times that every line is good and the scene has no life. The dialogue is quotable, the jokes land, the structure is correct, and nothing in it feels like it is happening for the first time.
**Effect on the audience:** Admiration without belief. They can see the writing, which means they are watching a writer rather than people — and the tell is usually that every character is equally articulate and nobody is ever imprecise.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere, and it is the specific risk of doing the rest of this category properly. The productive version of the same energy is `scene.third_attack`, which changes a structural fact instead of improving the lines — a scene rewritten at the structural level does not die of polish.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always, and it is a late-draft disease, so it mostly afflicts writers who are working hard. Two diagnostics. Read the scene aloud and count how many lines nobody would actually say out loud; count how many times a character searches for a word, repeats themselves, or answers the wrong question. Polish removes exactly those, and those are where the life was. The repair is to write the scene once more, fast, in twenty minutes, without looking at the polished version, and then to take back only the two or three lines the fast version proves you needed.
**Example:**
> **Overworked:** "You were always going to leave. I just didn't know you'd take the good years with you."
> **Alive:** "You're taking the car?" / "I need the car." / "I know. I know you do. I'm asking if you're taking it."

**Not a choice:** this is a **named failure**, not an option. It is reached by the **Avoid when** line of the card you did choose, never by a selector, because a selector that listed it would be offering to fail. It sits on the `scene.` shelf and is not of it.  
**Yields to:** `scene.third_attack` — Change a structural fact, not the lines.  
`scene.overworked_scene`
