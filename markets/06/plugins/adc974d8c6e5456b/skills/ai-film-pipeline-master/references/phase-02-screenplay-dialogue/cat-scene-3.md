## Consent, Access and the Contributor

### Going Back for Consent

**Also called:** re-consent, the second conversation, rolling consent as a scene, the visit before lock, asking again
**What it is:** A scene in which the maker returns to a contributor because the piece has become something other than the thing they agreed to, names the change out loud, and asks again — knowing the answer can be no.
**Effect on the audience:** When it appears on screen, it is the only moment in a documentary where the audience watches the film's own power being handed back, and it buys more trust in ninety seconds than any narration of good intentions. When it stays off screen, which is most of the time, its outcome is still visible in the cut: a film that has had this conversation is shaped differently from one that has not.
**Used for and where it works best:** Every documentary whose subject changed during the edit, and every commissioned piece built on testimony. It is a scene with a real value turn, so write it as one. **Say what changed before you say what you want** — one sentence, in their words not yours: *when we filmed you this was a film about the school; it is now a film about your brother.* **Offer the specific material, not the cut.** Nobody can consent to a ninety-minute film they watch once; anyone can respond to the four minutes they are in, watched twice. **Name what you cannot undo** — what has screened, what is archived, what a broadcaster now owns — because a promise you cannot keep is the thing that ends the relationship, not the film. **Ask a question that can be answered no.** "Are you still happy with this?" cannot be; "Do you want this out of the film?" can. And write the answer down in their words while you are still in the room. The scene's turn is not permission — it is standing. Someone who agrees reluctantly has told you something the film now has to carry. For the version where there is no finished piece to show, `inter.consent_without_a_cut`; for what may and may not be merged, `char.real_person_composite`.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary, Heritage Documentary, Documentary Interview, Essay Film, Reportage, Memoir | genres: Testimony, Heritage, Journalism, Migration, Social Drama, Faith
**Avoid when:** The cut is locked and the question is cosmetic. Asking a person to bless a decision you will not reverse is worse than not asking, because it converts them from a contributor into an accomplice and they will know. The other failure mode is asking so early and so often that the contributor starts editing themselves — re-consent on a *structural* change, not on every edit.
**Example:**
> "I need to tell you what it turned into before I ask you anything. When we filmed you, this was about the flood. It's about your brother now. He's in six scenes and he's in the last one."
> "You said the flood."
> "I did. That's why I'm here. I've brought the four minutes you're in — not the film, the four minutes. Watch it twice. If you want it out, it comes out, and I'm not going to argue with you about it. What I can't do is take it out of the version that played in Erbil in March."
> She watches it once. Then she says: "Play it again without the music."

**Yields to:** `inter.consent_without_a_cut` — There is no finished cut to show.  
`scene.reconsent_scene`

### The Approval Conversation

**Also called:** the veto meeting, showing them the cut, the client review, the subject screening, the sign-off
**What it is:** A scene in which someone holding a contractual or practical power to stop the work sees it and responds — so the scene you are actually in is not about the work's quality but about what they can take away.
**Effect on the audience:** Dramatised, it is one of the purest power scenes available, because both parties are pretending to discuss craft while conducting a negotiation about control. Lived, it decides what the finished piece is.
**Used for and where it works best:** Commissioned work of every kind, and any drama about people who make things. The craft rules are negotiation rules. **Write both walk-aways before you enter** — what happens to you if they refuse, what happens to them if they refuse — because the party with the worse alternative concedes, regardless of who is right (`seq.negotiation_sequence`). **Decide in advance which two notes you will take and which one you will not,** and take the two early: a reviewer who has won twice is measurably harder to refuse the third time, and you get to choose which three the fight is about. **Never screen to a group.** Approval in a room becomes performance for the others present, and someone will object in order to be seen objecting. **Separate the note from its proposed fix** — "cut the whole section" is usually a report that one line in it is wrong. For dramatising it, the scene turns when the reviewer stops talking about the work and says what they are actually protecting.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary, Heritage Documentary, Corporate, Brand Film, Documentary Interview, Reportage | genres: Journalism, Workplace, Heritage, Testimony, Political, Social Drama
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2028-09. Approval and access clauses are moving fast: duty-of-care pledges from producers' bodies, AI-reuse terms arriving in commissioning contracts, and data-protection law reaching participatory work. The craft position holds; the paperwork around it will not.
**Avoid when:** You granted approval in order to get access and the piece's whole subject is the person granting it. That is subject capture, and it has a shape you can recognise from the outside: the difficult question stops being asked somewhere in the second act, and the film becomes authorised without anyone deciding that it should. If you cannot make the piece without a veto, say in the piece that there was one — a disclosed veto is a fact the audience can weigh, and an undisclosed one is the thing that discredits the work later.
**Example:**
> "It's a strong film. I just think the section about my father is unbalanced."
> "Which part?"
> "All of it, really. It's eleven minutes."
> "Tell me the line that's wrong."
> *A pause of the kind that is itself an answer.*
> "There isn't a line that's wrong."

**Yields to:** AUTHORITY editorial independence, a disclosed veto — Subject capture; disclose it or refuse.  
`scene.approval_conversation`

### The Interview That Goes Wrong

**Also called:** the subject who stops, the retraction, the turned question, the walk-out, the interview that becomes the story
**What it is:** A scene in which an interview fails in one of three specific ways — the subject stops answering, retracts something already said, or turns the question back on the interviewer — and the failure, rather than the information, becomes the scene's content.
**Effect on the audience:** Total attention. An audience can tell the difference between a rehearsed answer and a person deciding whether to keep talking, and the moment of deciding is the most watchable thing an interview produces.
**Used for and where it works best:** Documentary interview, journalism drama, interrogation scenes, and any two-hander where one party has come to extract something. **Pick which of the three it is before you write a line, because each has a different value turn.** The *stop* turns on cost — the subject has calculated what the answer will do to someone else, and the scene's job is to make that calculation visible without narrating it. The *retraction* turns on the record: something already true is being unmade, and the scene is about whether the interviewer lets it go. The *turned question* is a status flip (`char.status_flip`) — the subject takes the interviewer's instrument and uses it, and the interviewer's answer, or refusal, is the scene. Two rules from practice. **The evasion is the content**: a subject who refuses the same question three times has told the audience something an answer never could, so do not cut to the next question — stay. And **do not follow them**: an interviewer who accepts the subject's preferred subject has conceded the scene, and the audience registers the concession immediately.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary Interview, Documentary, Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Reportage, Stage Play | genres: Journalism, Testimony, Crime, Political, Drama, Psychological
**Avoid when:** The subject's refusal is reasonable and the piece treats it as guilt. An audience sides with a person protecting someone else faster than it sides with a reporter, and a scene built to make a decent refusal look evasive is a scene that loses its own case. Also avoid stacking two of these in one piece; the second reads as the interviewer's technique failing rather than as the subject's choice.
**Example:**
> "Who authorised the transfer?"
> "I've told you what I know about the transfer."
> "You've told me when it happened. I'm asking who authorised it."
> *She looks at the recorder, not at him.*
> "Why does it matter to you? You didn't live here."
> "It doesn't matter to me."
> "Then turn it off."
> *He doesn't. She waits him out. He turns it off. The scene continues for another ninety seconds, and the film does not have a word of it.*

**Yields to:** `scene.right_of_reply` — A reasonable refusal must be printed, not framed.  
`scene.interview_breaks`

### The Life Performed for the Camera

**Also called:** the knowing contributor, self-presentation, the subject who has seen documentaries, the arranged everyday, the second take of a real life
**What it is:** A scene in which a real person conducts their own life while aware of being filmed and competent at being filmed — so what is captured is not behaviour but a person's chosen version of their behaviour.
**Effect on the audience:** Double reading, and it is a richer one than unselfconsciousness would give. Audiences now recognise performance-of-self on sight; a film that pretends it is not happening loses them, and a film that makes it the subject gains a whole layer.
**Used for and where it works best:** Observational and participatory documentary, heritage films where a community is showing itself, vlogs, and any scene with a contributor who has media training or simply pride. The craft rule is to **cut to the seam, not around it**: the usable moment is where the performance slips, where the person checks whether it landed, or where two performers in the same room contradict each other's version. That seam is the scene's turn. Second rule: **arrangement must be declared.** Reshooting a routine action — someone walking through a door twice — is legitimate and universal. Staging an event that would not otherwise have happened is a different thing, and if it stays in the piece the audience must be told, on screen. Third: write the *reason* the person is performing, because there always is one, and it is usually the most interesting fact in the scene — they are performing for a dead parent, for the neighbours, for a version of themselves from 1991. Related: `voice.confessional_to_camera` for the piece-to-camera address, `sub.behaviour_contradicts` for the gap between a line and a body.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary, Heritage Documentary, Documentary Series, Essay Film, Reels/Shorts, Vlog | genres: Journalism, Testimony, Social Drama, Lifestyle, Heritage, Satire
**Avoid when:** The performance is all there is and you have no second source. A film built entirely from people's preferred versions of themselves is a brochure, and no amount of handheld camera changes that. The other failure is condescension — cutting a contributor's self-presentation for laughs, which the audience reads as the film picking on someone who trusted it.
**Example:**
> He has set out the tools on the bench in a row, which is not how he keeps them.
> "Normally I'd have the plane here." He moves it. Moves it back. "No. It lives there."
> Then, to the camera, and this is the shot: "My father had it off a man in Mosul. That's what you want, isn't it."

**Yields to:** `scene.transcript_built` — No second source; use contradicting accounts.  
`scene.performed_for_camera`

### The Reconstruction Shot Like Archive

**Also called:** the unlabelled restaging, grain as evidence, sepia fraud, the dramatised insert passed off, unmarked recreation
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A reconstructed scene shot in the visual grammar of archive — shallow focus, grain, gate weave, desaturation, hands and backs of heads — and cut into the piece without a label, so the audience cannot tell what is evidence and what is invention.
**Effect on the audience:** It works, once, and then it destroys the piece. The moment one viewer identifies a reconstruction that was not marked, every other image in the film becomes a question, including the real ones. This is the fastest way for a rigorous film to lose its standing, and it is irreversible: you cannot re-earn evidentiary trust in the same film.
**Used for and where it works best:** As a diagnosis. Run the piece and ask of every image: could a viewer tell, from this shot alone, whether it happened? Where the answer is no, either label it or change how it is shot. Then use the positive version, which is genuinely stronger: **a signalled reconstruction is dramatically better than a disguised one,** because an audience that has been told what it is watching can attend to what the scene *shows* instead of spending the scene wondering whether it is real. Signal it in the grammar, not only in a corner caption that half the audience misses — clean modern frame, no grain, a deliberate refusal to resolve the faces, a caption inside the first five seconds. The same applies to a still treated to imply motion it never had, which is the same offence in a subtler form.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (most damaging in Heritage Documentary, Documentary Series and True Crime, where the audience's default assumption is that everything on screen is evidence)
**Avoid when:** — the only legitimate neighbour is declared docudrama, where the whole piece announces itself as dramatised at the top and the audience has consented in advance. Even there, mixing genuine archive into dramatised material without distinguishing them reproduces the failure inside a form that was supposed to be exempt from it.
**Example:**
> **The failure:** 16mm grain, a hand on a rifle bolt, dust, no caption. Two shots later, a genuine newsreel frame of the same regiment, graded to match. Nothing on screen tells the viewer which is which, and the grade was chosen so that nothing would.
> **The repair:** clean digital frame, full colour, the actor's face never resolved, and across the bottom for the first four seconds: **Reconstruction. No footage of the crossing survives.** The scene is thirty seconds long and it is now the most trusted thing in the film.

**Not a choice:** this is a **named failure**, not an option. It is reached by the **Avoid when** line of the card you did choose, never by a selector, because a selector that listed it would be offering to fail. It sits on the `scene.` shelf and is not of it.  
`scene.reconstruction_unmarked`

## Rooms Where Something Is Decided

### Putting It to Them

**Also called:** the right of reply, the no-surprises approach, the doorstep, the letter before publication, the response request
**What it is:** The scene in which the specific allegation is put to the person it is about, with a real deadline, before publication — and the shape of their answer, including a refusal, becomes part of the piece.
**Effect on the audience:** It is the difference between a case and an accusation. A reader who sees the subject was asked, and sees what they said, is reading a report; a reader who never sees the approach is reading an argument and will discount it accordingly, whether or not it is true.
**Used for and where it works best:** All reportage and investigative documentary, and any drama about journalism. The craft rules are procedural and they change the prose. **Put the allegation, not the subject.** A letter saying "we are making a film about your company" produces a press officer; a letter saying "we have three invoices dated 4 March showing payments to your brother-in-law's firm, and we intend to publish them Thursday" produces an answer. **One specific claim per numbered paragraph, with the evidence named,** because a subject who cannot tell which claim to deny will deny everything generally, and a general denial is worth nothing to the reader. **Give a real deadline and honour it.** **Print the refusal verbatim** — a refusal has a shape, and "declined to comment" throws away the difference between silence, a lawyer's letter and *no one by that name has ever worked here*. And the reply changes the piece even when it is nothing, because the paragraph it sits in has to be written to survive it. Adjacent: `info.embargoed_fact` for the fact you hold and cannot state; `info.access_as_structure` for what access itself does to a piece's shape.
**Best in:** formats: Reportage, Long-Form Non-Fiction, Documentary, Documentary Series, Podcast, One-Hour Drama | genres: Journalism, Political, Crime, Social Drama, Advocacy, Workplace
**Avoid when:** The approach would put a source in danger, which is the one real exception and has to be reasoned on the page rather than assumed. The failure mode in the other direction is the ambush dressed as a right of reply — a camera at a car door with a question the person has had four seconds to hear. That is a scene about the reporter, and audiences have stopped reading it as courage.
**Example:**
> **Weak:** "We're making a programme about the redevelopment and would welcome your participation."
> **Working:** "We intend to publish on Thursday 14th. Three points, and I would like your response to each. 1. Between March and July 2019 the trust paid £412,000 to Kerrin Ltd. 2. Kerrin Ltd was incorporated by your son-in-law on 2 March 2019. 3. No tender was advertised. If any of these is wrong, tell me which one and I will correct it."
> **What ran:** *Asked about the three payments, Mr Doulis said: "I'm not going to be interviewed by you." Kerrin Ltd did not respond. The trust said the tender rules "did not apply to works of this nature" and declined to say which rule it meant.*

**Yields to:** `info.embargoed_fact` — Putting it would endanger a source.  
`scene.right_of_reply`

### The Negotiation With No Leverage

**Also called:** the extortion scene, bargaining from nothing, the petition, asking without a walk-away, the supplicant scene
**What it is:** A scene between two parties where one of them has no alternative at all — no walk-away, nothing to withhold, nowhere else to go — so the scene cannot run on concessions and must run on something else.
**Effect on the audience:** Dread with an odd intimacy in it. The audience knows the arithmetic is settled, so they stop watching for the outcome and start watching for what the weaker party is willing to spend, which is a more uncomfortable thing to watch than suspense.
**Used for and where it works best:** Migration, eviction, debt, sentencing, sickness, asylum, occupation — every situation where a person must ask something of a system or a person who can simply refuse. `seq.negotiation_sequence` names the shape and explicitly hands this case away: with no alternative there is no negotiation. So write the three instruments the powerless actually have. **Time** — delay, presence, refusing to leave, coming back on Tuesday. **Witnesses** — the scene changes when a third person can see it (`scene.public_scene`, `scene.before_a_stranger`). **And the strong party's self-image**, which is almost always the real lever: the need to be thanked, to be considered fair, to not be the villain in the story of their own afternoon. The scene turns on which instrument the weaker party reaches for, and on what reaching for it costs them. The absolute requirement: **the strong party must want something non-material in the scene.** If they want nothing at all, there is no scene, only a refusal. And write the cost in a concrete unit — not dignity, but the words she has to say about her own son to get the form stamped.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Stage Play, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Limited Series, Short Film | genres: Social Drama, Migration, Political, Drama, Tragedy, Workplace
**Avoid when:** The audience has already watched two of them. The shape is static by design, and a third instance reads as the writer's thesis rather than as the character's week — vary it with `scene.trap_scene` or hand the same situation to a different character. Also avoid ending on the concession: the scene is over one beat after the powerless party spends the thing, not at the moment the powerful party says yes.
**Example:**
> "The window was Friday."
> "I was in the hospital on Friday."
> "Then the appeal window opens in April."
> *She does not leave. She sits down, which is not permitted, and which he could end by standing up.*
> "My daughter is eleven. In April she will have been out of school for seven months and she has begun to say she doesn't want to go back. If I write that down, is it the kind of thing that helps?"
> *He does not answer that. He says: "Write it down." Which is a yes, and costs her exactly what she thought it would.*

**Yields to:** `scene.trap_scene` — from the third instance onward, where the static shape starts reading as the writer's thesis rather than the character's week.  
`scene.no_leverage`

### The Apology That Lands

**Also called:** the working apology, the repair offer, the accepted amends, the six-element apology
**What it is:** An apology scene built so that the apology is actually received — which depends on a specific order of content, not on sincerity, and which the database has previously catalogued only in its failing forms.
**Effect on the audience:** Release, and a durable kind. Audiences are unusually expert at apologies because they have given and received bad ones, and they can feel the difference between a person taking responsibility and a person managing their own discomfort.
**Used for and where it works best:** Family drama, late-season repair, redemption arcs, workplace and institutional stories, and any comedy that has to land a real beat. The order is what makes it work, and it comes out of the research on which apologies are actually accepted. **Responsibility first and unhedged** — *I did it, it was my fault* — with no explanation attached, because an explanation arriving in the same breath is heard as a defence. **An offer of repair second,** and this is the element writers most often omit: a concrete thing the apologiser will do, which gives the wronged party something to accept or refuse and therefore gives the scene a second half. Regret, explanation and a statement of what will change come after, and they matter less than either of the first two. **Cut the request for forgiveness.** It is the weakest element, it hands the scene back to the apologiser, and its absence is what makes an apology feel like it was not a transaction. The scene's value turn is not forgiveness — it is the moment the apologiser stops explaining. Write everything up to that moment as explanation, and the turn as the silence after it stops. Then let the wronged party set the terms: `rel.reconciliation_on_new_terms`. The failure twin is `rel.instant_reconciliation`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Stage Play, Novel, Limited Series, Half-Hour Comedy | genres: Family Drama, Drama, Redemption, Romance, Social Drama, Workplace
**Avoid when:** The story's argument is that this person cannot change, in which case a working apology contradicts the whole design and you want the failing version. Also avoid giving it to a character who has not lost anything yet — an apology that arrives before the cost has landed is a bid to skip the cost, and the audience scores it that way.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "I'm sorry you felt shut out. It was a difficult year and I was doing what I thought was right for everyone, and if I could go back — look, can we please just put this behind us?"
> **Working:**
> "I took the money out of the account in September and I didn't tell you. That's the thing. I did that."
> "Why—"
> "No. I'll tell you why in a minute if you want it, but that's not what I came to say."
> *A silence. This is the turn.*
> "I've transferred four hundred back and I'll do four hundred on the first of every month until it's whole. You can look at the statements. You don't have to tell me anything today."

**Yields to:** `rel.instant_reconciliation` — The story argues this person cannot change.  
`scene.apology_that_lands`

### The Handover of Responsibility

**Also called:** the passing of duty, taking it on, the keys scene, the charge, the inheritance of an obligation
**What it is:** A scene in which a duty rather than an object moves from one person to another — the care of someone, the keeping of a record, the running of a place, the silence about something — and the drama is in whether it is genuinely accepted.
**Effect on the audience:** A specific sensation with no other source: the feeling that the story's weight has shifted to a different shoulder, and that the first person is now free and diminished at the same time.
**Used for and where it works best:** Heritage and family stories, war, faith, migration, and the end of any long series. It is not `scene.transaction_scene` — no object has to move, and an object often gets in the way — and it is not `rel.succession_fight`, because nobody here is competing. Build it in three parts. **The naming of what the duty actually is,** in the smallest possible terms: not "the family" but *Tuesdays, and the phone bill, and you never tell her about Nabil.* **The transfer**, which can be a single word. **And the first act performed under it**, which is the only part that proves acceptance. If you must cut one, cut the middle; **never cut the third**, because a handover without a first act is a promise and the audience does not believe promises. Two further rules: the giver must lose something by giving it, or the scene is a relief rather than a turn; and the receiver should get one detail wrong immediately, because competence in the first minute makes the duty look light.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Limited Series, Novel, Stage Play, One-Hour Drama, Heritage Documentary | genres: Heritage, Family Drama, War, Historical, Faith, Migration
**Avoid when:** The receiver has already been doing the job for an act and everyone knows it. Then the handover is ceremonial, and a ceremonial handover belongs in a montage or a single line, not in a scene. The failure mode is the blessing speech — the older character explaining what the duty means, which is theme delivered as dialogue and removes the only thing the scene had, which was doubt.
**Example:**
> "It's Tuesdays. She's ready at nine and she won't say if she isn't. The chemist knows her by the other name, the old one — don't correct them. And you never mention Nabil, not even to say he rang."
> "He rings?"
> "That's what I mean."
> *He takes the list. Later that week, on the phone to the chemist, he gives her married name, and has to start the call again.*

**Yields to:** `scene.one_line_scene` — Ceremonial handover: a line, not a scene.  
`scene.handover`

## Real Speech and the Writer in the Room

### The Scene the Writer Is In

**Also called:** first-person reportage, the reporter as a character, immersion on the page, the earned "I", the witness who is writing
**What it is:** A non-fiction scene in which the writer appears as a participant rather than as an invisible recorder, because their presence changed what happened or is the only reason the reader can be there at all.
**Effect on the audience:** Calibration. A reader who can see where the writer stood can weigh everything else in the piece; a reader who cannot is quietly asked to accept omniscience from a person who was demonstrably in a room.
**Used for and where it works best:** Reportage, immersion journalism, the essay film, memoir-adjacent non-fiction, and heritage work where the writer is from the community being written about. The stance is a decision made before the first sentence — present and named, present and invisible, or absent — and the commonest failure is not the choice but the **drift**: a piece that begins as observation and becomes advocacy in the middle third, at the point where the writer got angry. So fix the stance and hold it. **Three legitimate reasons to appear, and no others:** you caused what happened, you are the only witness to it, or the reader needs to know how you know. **Use "I" for the transaction, not for the feeling** — *I asked him three times* earns its place; *I felt a chill* does not, because it spends the reader's trust on something they cannot check. And the hard prohibition that separates this from fiction: **you may not furnish the scene beyond the evidence.** The weather nobody recorded and the expression nobody described read as vividness and function as invention. Related: `pov.direct_address` for the fiction-side move, `info.access_as_structure` for what your access does to the shape.
**Best in:** formats: Reportage, Long-Form Non-Fiction, Essay, Memoir, Essay Film, Documentary | genres: Journalism, Literary, Social Drama, Migration, Nature, Advocacy
**Avoid when:** Your presence is the least interesting thing in the room. The first person is a claim on the reader's attention and it has to be earned by consequence, not by effort — a paragraph explaining why you took the assignment is the standard tell. If you are appearing in order to prove you were there, put the proof in the reporting instead.
**Example:**
> **Unearned:** *I had been thinking about the river for years before I finally drove out to see it, and as I stood on the bank in the thin October light I felt the weight of all those years settle on me.*
> **Earned:** *I asked him three times whether the wells had been tested, and three times he answered a question about the pipeline. The fourth time I put my notebook down, which I should not have done, and he said: "You're going to write that I wouldn't tell you." I said probably. He said: "Write it. It's the only true thing you've got."*

### The Scene Built From a Transcript

**Also called:** verbatim construction, the tribunal scene, documentary theatre, transcript assembly, the paper edit as a scene
**What it is:** A scene composed entirely from things real people actually said, shaped by cutting and ordering rather than by writing — where the writer's whole craft is subtraction and juxtaposition.
**Effect on the audience:** An authority no invented dialogue can reach, and a strange one: real speech is less well-formed than written speech, and the roughness itself reads as evidence. Audiences relax their guard against rhetoric and raise it against tidiness.
**Used for and where it works best:** Verbatim theatre, tribunal plays, documentary interview films, testimony podcasts, and the paper edit that precedes every interview-led documentary. The rules are strict because the form's only asset is that it is not making things up. **Cut only; never add.** The moment you write a connecting clause into someone's mouth, the form's claim collapses and you have a drama with real names in it. **Use a bridge, not a sentence they did not say** — a narrator, a caption, a stage direction, an acknowledged edit — to cover the join. **Keep the hesitations that mean something and cut the ones that do not**, which is a judgement, and the honest way to make it is to ask whether the pause is the answer; the pause before a difficult reply is content and cutting it is the form's commonest act of vandalism. **Get the question inside the answer** in the room, because the question will be removed later. **Cast for disagreement, not coverage**: the material is the contradiction between two accounts of the same night, and four people who agree are one long interview. For the prose-line question of how much to clean, `rhythm.transcript_clean`; for transcript as a voice, `voice.transcript_voice`.
**Best in:** formats: Stage Play, Documentary, Documentary Interview, Audio Drama, Podcast, Monologue | genres: Testimony, Journalism, Social Drama, Political, Heritage, Courtroom
**Avoid when:** The transcript has no conflict in it. Verbatim is a shaping technique, not a story generator, and thirty pages of one considerate person being fair to everybody cannot be cut into a scene. Also avoid it where anonymity is required and the speech itself identifies the speaker — a distinctive idiom is a fingerprint, and paraphrasing to protect someone takes you out of the form.
**Example:**
> **Raw, two interviews, forty minutes apart in the same building:**
> A: "...and then, I mean, I don't know, we were told, they said the gates would be, would stay open until eight, that was the, we understood that."
> B: "The gates were shut at seven. They're shut at seven every day. It's on the sign."
> **Cut, with a bridge and nothing added:**
> A: "We were told the gates would stay open until eight. We understood that."
> *(caption: the sign at the north entrance)*
> B: "They're shut at seven every day. It's on the sign."
> A: "We understood that."

**Yields to:** `char.real_person_composite` — Anonymity required; the idiom identifies.  
`scene.transcript_built`

### The Scene Made of Two Moments That Were Not Adjacent

**Also called:** the frankenbite, the stitched answer, the manufactured reaction, the composite exchange, the reverse that never happened
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A scene assembled in the edit from material that never occurred together — an answer built from two answers, a reaction cut in from a different conversation, a reply the speaker made to a different question — presented as a single continuous exchange.
**Effect on the audience:** Nothing, until it is found out. Then it retroactively falsifies every other scene in the piece, and the contributor's own account of what happened becomes the story instead of the film's.
**Used for and where it works best:** As a diagnostic pass, and it has a mechanical test: take every cut inside an answer and every reaction shot, and write on the edit list what the person was actually responding to at the moment that frame was recorded. Where you cannot answer, you have either a problem or a piece of work to do. The honest neighbours are legitimate and should be used instead — **shorten an answer without changing what it claims**, cover the join with archive or B-roll rather than with a jump the audience will read as concealment, and where a contributor said the same thing better in a second sitting, use the second sitting whole. The line is not tidiness; it is whether the finished scene asserts something the material does not support. Cutting *"they said the gates would stay open, I mean, they, we understood that"* down to *"they said the gates would stay open"* is editing. Joining that to a sentence from twenty minutes later so it answers a question that was never put to them is a fabrication with a friendly name.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (most damaging in Documentary Interview, True Crime and Heritage Documentary, where a contributor's exact words may be the only surviving record)
**Avoid when:** — there is no legitimate version in non-fiction. In scripted work the move is simply a cut and carries no claim at all. The tempting middle case — a contributor who "would have said it anyway", or who approves the stitched version afterwards — is not an exception: their approval covers the sentence, not the claim that they said it then, to that question, in that room.
**Example:**
> **What was recorded, 11:04:** Q: "Did you enjoy working there?" A: "For the first two years, yes. It was a good place."
> **What was recorded, 11:26:** Q: "What do you make of the allegations?" A: "I'm not going to get into that. You'd have to ask them."
> **The frankenbite, as cut:** Q: "What do you make of the allegations?" A: "It was a good place. You'd have to ask them."
> Nothing in that exchange is invented. Every word is hers. She never said it.

**Not a choice:** this is a **named failure**, not an option. It is reached by the **Avoid when** line of the card you did choose, never by a selector, because a selector that listed it would be offering to fail. It sits on the `scene.` shelf and is not of it.  
**Yields to:** `scene.transcript_built` — Cut only, never join non-adjacent material.  
`scene.false_adjacency`

## Who the Scene Is Really Aimed At

### Told What They Already Suspect

**Also called:** the confirmation scene, the thing said out loud, the suspicion made official, saying it in the room
**What it is:** A scene whose information is already known to the person receiving it, so the scene cannot run on the news — it runs on who says it, what saying it costs them, and the fact that it can no longer be unsaid.
**Effect on the audience:** The particular quiet of a thing becoming official. Because the audience is also ahead, they are not waiting for a reveal; they are watching two people decide to stop pretending, which is a more adult tension than surprise.
**Used for and where it works best:** Family drama, illness, infidelity, institutional decline, the late midpoint of almost any slow story, and every heritage story in which a family has agreed not to discuss something. It is the twin of `emo.bad_news_delivery` and its rules are inverted: there, the news is new and the craft is the warning shot and the pause; here the news is old and the craft is elsewhere. **Write the recipient's line before the speaker's** — they should be ahead, and the scene is far better when the recipient supplies a detail the speaker did not have. **Give the speaker a cost**: they are not delivering information, they are ending an arrangement that was protecting them too. **And end on an action that was impossible five minutes earlier** — a phone call made, a door left open, a name used — because the turn is not knowledge, it is that the pretence is now unavailable. A scene of this kind that ends on a reaction has not turned.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Limited Series, Stage Play, Short Film | genres: Family Drama, Drama, Crime, Medical, Tragedy, Psychological
**Avoid when:** The audience is behind the characters rather than ahead of them. If the information is genuinely new to the viewer, this shape throws away a reveal you had; use the bad-news shape or restructure so we learn it earlier. The failure mode is the recipient saying "I know" and the scene stopping — "I know" is the first line, not the last.
**Example:**
> "I need to tell you something about the shop."
> "It's gone."
> "...Yes."
> "It went in March. Hanan's husband does the deliveries, he stopped coming in April." *She has been ahead of him for five months.* "Say it anyway."
> "The shop's gone."
> *She stands, goes to the drawer, and takes out the envelope she has had ready since April. That envelope could not have come out of the drawer six lines ago.*

**Yields to:** `emo.bad_news_delivery` — The audience is behind the characters.  
`scene.confirmation_scene`

### The Speech Aimed Through the Room

**Also called:** the second addressee, the toast with a target, the briefing aimed at one person, speaking past the audience, the aimed eulogy
**What it is:** A formal address — a toast, a eulogy, a briefing, a sermon, a prize acceptance — whose real addressee is one person present, so the room is not the audience but the instrument used to reach them.
**Effect on the audience:** Triple vision, and it is the most efficient subtext machine in the form: the audience hears what the room hears, sees what the target hears, and watches the target be unable to answer. The inability to answer is the whole pleasure.
**Used for and where it works best:** Weddings, funerals, board meetings, prize-givings, homecomings, church — any occasion where speaking is permitted and answering is not. It is not `scene.public_scene`, where a private conflict is conducted under a social surface by both parties; here only one party is speaking, and the surface is not a constraint but a weapon. The speech forms themselves are held elsewhere — `dial.the_toast`, `dial.the_eulogy`, `dial.earned_speech` — and this card is what to do with the room. **Write the room's version of the speech first, complete and unobjectionable.** **Then find the one sentence that only the target can hear correctly.** One, not three: a second aimed line makes the room start looking around, and once the room has noticed, the weapon is gone and you are in a different scene. **Give the target no line.** Their silence in public is the pressure, and any reply collapses the structure. And decide who else in the room knows, because a single third person who understands — and who looks at the target instead of the speaker — is worth more than a whole audience of the oblivious.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Stage Play, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Novel, Short Film | genres: Family Drama, Political, Heritage, Satire, Workplace, Tragedy
**Avoid when:** The room would not sit still for it. The form depends on an occasion whose etiquette forbids interruption; in a setting where anyone can answer back, the aimed line gets answered and the scene becomes an argument — which may be better, but is a different scene. Also avoid when the audience does not yet know what the aimed line refers to: this shape spends information, it does not deliver it.
**Example:**
> "...and my father, God rest him, taught all four of us the same thing. He said: *a man is what he does when the account is short.*"
> *Forty people laugh, because it is a good line and they knew the old man.*
> "Anyway. To Nahrein and Bassam."
> *Forty glasses go up. His brother's does not, and one cousin at the back is not looking at the speaker at all.*

**Yields to:** `scene.public_scene` — The room can answer back.  
`scene.aimed_through_the_room`
