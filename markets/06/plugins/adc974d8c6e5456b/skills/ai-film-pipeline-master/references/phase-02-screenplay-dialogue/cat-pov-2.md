## Tense, Frame and the Layers of Telling

### Tense as a Distance Instrument

**Also called:** narrative tense, present-tense fiction, the immediacy choice, the historical present
**What it is:** The tense the narration sits in — simple past, present, or the historical present laid over past events — sets a fixed floor and ceiling on how far the reader can stand from the telling, independently of person and independently of diction.
**Effect on the audience:** Past tense implies a survivor and a settled account, and gives the reader a reflective seat. Present tense removes the survivor and removes the seat: the reader is not being told, she is watching, and she cannot be sure anyone lived to report it. The historical present — past events narrated in the present, "so I walk in and he's already got the money out" — sounds like speech, and reads as a person performing a story to your face.
**Used for and where it works best:** Choose past when the narrator's hindsight is an asset — irony, elegy, structural freedom to jump and summarise, a narrating intelligence that can rank events. Choose present when *will she survive* is the live question, when the character's judgement must stay as unformed as the reader's, or when the whole book is one continuous pressure. Two craft rules do most of the work. **First: immediacy is not intimacy.** A distant third in present tense is further from a character's inner life than a close third in past tense; if you chose present for closeness and the pages still read cold, the problem is your rung on `pov.psychic_distance_ladder`, not your verbs. **Second: present tense costs you the summary gear.** Past tense can cover nineteen years in a clause; present tense makes every compression audible, which is why present-tense novels so often feel long in the middle. Reserve the historical present for narrators who are performing — the bar story, the deposition, the documentary voice, the heritage teller of `pov.oral_narration` — and hold it absolutely consistently, because a slip into past inside a historical-present passage reads as an error, never as a gear change.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Memoir, Feature Film, Audio Drama, Reportage | genres: Literary, Thriller, Horror, Coming of Age, Survival, Testimony
**Avoid when:** The book needs a wide time span, frequent flashback, or a narrator who knows more than the character — all three are cheap in past and expensive in present. Avoid present tense in commercial historical fiction specifically: it strips the sense of a record being kept, which is a large part of what that reader came for. And avoid changing tense to mark a timeline — that is chronology signposting and belongs with `time.dual_timeline`, not here.
**Example:**
> Past: *He waited outside the room for twenty minutes before anyone came. Later he would be told it had been four.*
> Present: *He waits outside the room. The corridor has one chair in it and he does not sit down.*
> Historical present: *So I'm standing in this corridor, right, one chair, and I don't sit down — I don't know why I don't sit down, I've thought about it since.*

**Yields to:** `time.dual_timeline` — Tense used to mark a timeline, not distance.  
`pov.tense_as_distance`

### Nested Tellers

**Also called:** frame narrative, the story within a story, embedded narration, Chinese-box narration, the Marlow structure
**What it is:** A narrator introduces a second narrator, who tells the real story — sometimes to a third depth. Each layer is a separate viewpoint with its own distance, its own audience and its own reasons to shade the account.
**Effect on the audience:** Distance stacks. Every layer of telling puts another pane of glass between the reader and the events, which cools the material and simultaneously makes it feel preserved, dangerous, or worth the effort of retrieval.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when the *act of transmission* is part of the subject: a confession that needed a listener, a story that survived a war, testimony given to someone with a reason to want it. The mechanics of the frame itself are `time.frame_narrative`; what belongs here is the POV consequence. Three craft rules. (1) **Every layer costs a rung of closeness**, so build in the reverse order to your instinct — decide the innermost distance you need at the climax, then check that two layers of frame have not put you at rung 2 when you needed rung 5. (2) **Close the outer frame, and close it on a change.** A frame that opens and never returns is a discarded POV and reads as an unpaid setup; a frame that returns and merely says "and that was the story" has spent twenty pages to say nothing. The outer narrator must be different at the end for having heard it. (3) **Plant one object or fact in the inner story that the outer frame can pick up** — that single hinge is what makes the structure feel authored rather than decorative. The frame is also the cheapest honest licence for an unreliable inner account, per `info.unreliable_narrator`: the listener heard it this way, and the listener is on the page.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Novella, Short Story, Heritage Documentary, Audio Drama, Stage Play | genres: Literary, Horror, Historical, Heritage, Myth, Mystery
**Avoid when:** The inner story is already gripping and the frame is only an excuse to start elsewhere. Then the frame is an obstacle the reader must climb twice. Also avoid three layers unless the third layer is the point; readers track two tellers comfortably and lose count at three.
**Example:**
> Flat: *In 1943 my grandmother crossed the mountains on foot with two children.*
> Framed, and the frame earning its keep: *She told it the same way every Easter, and every Easter she left out the second child. I was thirty-one before I asked, and she said, "There were two," and went on peeling the apple, and that is the whole of what I know about my uncle.*

**Yields to:** `pov.oral_narration` — Want transmission without a frame read twice.  
`pov.nested_tellers`

### The Implied Author

**Also called:** Booth's implied author, the second self, the authorial norms, the intelligence behind the narrator
**What it is:** The version of an author a reader assembles from the whole text — the set of values and judgements that decided *which* narrator to use, which scenes to include and where the sympathy lands. It is not the narrator, and it is not the living writer.
**Effect on the audience:** It is the reader's reference point for irony. Every time a reader thinks *the narrator believes that, but the book does not*, she is measuring the gap between narrator and implied author. Without a stable implied author, no narrator can be unreliable, because there is nothing to be unreliable against.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it as a working tool exactly when a narrator holds views you do not: the racist grandfather, the true believer, the abuser who thinks he is a romantic. The craft rule is that **you never correct the narrator in the narration — you correct him in the arrangement.** Choose the scene that follows his speech, the detail he dismisses in half a line, the person given the last word, the thing the reader can see that he cannot. That arrangement *is* the implied author and it is legible to readers without a single line of authorial comment. Two practical uses: it is the diagnosis for a book that reads as endorsing something you meant to indict, and it is the reason `char.mouthpiece` fails — a character who states your view collapses the gap and leaves the reader nothing to do. Related, not identical, to the persona question in `voice.narrating_persona`: that is how the telling sounds, this is what the book believes.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Memoir, Feature Film, Limited Series, Essay | genres: Literary, Satire, Historical, Psychological, Social Drama, Crime
**Avoid when:** Nothing to avoid — every finished text has one whether or not the writer designed it. The only failure is leaving it to chance and then being surprised at what reviewers say the book thinks.
**Example:**
> Narrator: *She was lucky I took her back after all that, and I told her so, and she cried, which I took as agreement.*
> Implied author, working purely through arrangement — the next line: *She was gone by March. The note she left was on the back of a gas bill, which was the only paper in the house, because he did not like the mess of paper.*

`pov.implied_author`

### Focalisation: Who Sees Versus Who Speaks

**Also called:** Genette's focalisation, the focaliser, perspective versus voice, internal and external focalisation
**What it is:** Genette's separation of two questions English craft talk jams together as "point of view": **who speaks** (the narrator, the source of the words) and **who sees** (the focaliser, the consciousness whose perception and knowledge filter the scene). They can be different people, and in most good prose they routinely are.
**Effect on the audience:** Invisible as theory, decisive in practice. It is what lets a book sound adult and perceive as a child, or sound cold and perceive as a man in a panic.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it the moment your POV problem will not solve as a POV problem — when you want a character's *access* without a character's *vocabulary*, or a narrator's *authority* over a character's *ignorance*. The working version of the theory is three settings, and you should be able to name yours for every scene. **Zero focalisation**: the teller sees more than any character — `pov.omniscient`. **Internal focalisation**: the teller sees exactly what one character sees — `pov.close_third`, `pov.first_person_central`. **External focalisation**: the teller sees less than the characters, reporting surface only — `pov.objective_camera`. The craft rule that pays for the terminology: **you may hold voice steady and move focalisation, or hold focalisation steady and move voice, but if both slide in one paragraph the reader registers `pov.head_hopping`.** The most useful single application is the adult narrator with a child focaliser — it is how `pov.child_pov` gets written without baby talk — and the second most useful is the retrospective narrator of `pov.retrospective_distance`, where the speaker is the older self and the seer is the younger.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Feature Film, Limited Series, Comics, Audiobook | genres: Literary, Historical, Coming of Age, Psychological, Crime, War
**Avoid when:** You are drafting. This is an analysis and revision instrument; running it at speed in a first draft produces self-conscious, over-managed prose. Diagnose with it on the second pass.
**Example:**
> Speaker and seer the same (child voice, child eye): *There was a lot of red on the floor and Mama said don't come in, so I didn't come in, and then a man came with a bag.*
> Speaker adult, seer still the child: *There was more of it than a six-year-old had a scale for. What I understood was the instruction — do not come in — and I obeyed it perfectly, for eleven minutes, until a man arrived with a bag and everyone stopped saying my name.*

`pov.focalisation`

## Who the Narrator Is Talking To

### The Narratee

**Also called:** the addressee, the implied listener, who the narrator thinks is listening, the internal audience
**What it is:** The figure the narration is aimed at — a named person, a court, a child not yet born, a stranger on a train, a general reader, or nobody in particular. It exists in every narrated text; most writers never choose it.
**Effect on the audience:** It sets what the narrator can leave out, what he must explain, what he will exaggerate, and where he lies. Readers feel all of this as *character* without being able to name the cause.
**Used for and where it works best:** Decide it before page one, and write it at the top of the file: *who is she telling this to, and why now.* It is the highest-leverage undiscussed decision in first-person fiction, and the fastest repair for a first-person voice that feels generic — a narrator with no addressee has no reason to select, so she reports everything evenly. Specific consequences you get for free: a narrator addressing someone who was *there* cannot explain the basics, which kills exposition problems outright; a narrator addressing an enemy becomes defensive and therefore unreliable without you engineering a thing; a narrator addressing the dead becomes elegiac and stops performing. The craft rule: **the narratee should want something the narrator is reluctant to give.** Related to `voice.implied_listener`, which is the same decision felt as tone; here it is felt as information control, and it sets the real ceiling on `info.disclosure_budget`.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Memoir, Monologue, Audio Drama, Podcast | genres: Literary, Crime, Testimony, Romance, Psychological, Historical
**Avoid when:** The narratee would have to be named and the naming would give away the ending. Some books need the addressee held back — but *you* still need to know, and holding back is not the same as never deciding. Also avoid over-specifying in third person, where an insistent internal audience starts to read as a frame you have not built.
**Example:**
> No narratee — the voice explains to nobody in particular: *My father worked at the tannery on the east side of town, which had been there since the 1920s and employed most of our neighbours.*
> Narratee = his daughter, who has never been: *Your grandfather worked at the tannery. You would not have liked the smell and neither did I, and I want you to understand that everyone on our street smelled of it, so nobody smelled of it.*

**Yields to:** `pov.nested_tellers` — Insistent internal audience in third; build the frame.  
`pov.narratee`

### Direct Address and the Broken Fourth Wall

**Also called:** breaking the fourth wall, dear reader, metalepsis, aside to camera, authorial intrusion
**What it is:** The narrator or a character steps across the boundary of the fiction and speaks to the audience as an audience — acknowledging the book as a book, the camera as a camera, the telling as a telling.
**Effect on the audience:** Complicity and a jolt of wakefulness. The audience is flattered into the position of co-conspirator, and simultaneously reminded that what they are watching is made, which cuts immersion and raises attention.
**Used for and where it works best:** Three jobs it does better than anything else: establishing a narrator's personality in one line on page one; getting a laugh out of the gap between what a character says publicly and what he tells us privately; and landing a final sting by handing the audience the meaning directly. The craft rules are strict because the device is cheap. **(1) Establish it in the first scene or do not use it** — a fourth-wall break in chapter nineteen of a sealed book reads as panic, and is a broken contract under `pov.contract` rather than a `pov.licensed_break`, because it is not about anything. **(2) The break must cost the character something**, or it is a comedian doing tight five. **(3) Never use it to hand over information you failed to dramatise** — that is `expo.exposition_fairy` wearing a wink. The strongest late-break variant, and the only one that survives without a first-page setup, is the one where the fiction's fabric is the subject: a narrator who admits he has been arranging the scenes you have read. Tonally distinct from the second-person conscription of `pov.second_person`: that puts the reader *inside* the story, this pulls her outside it.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Half-Hour Comedy, Stage Play, Monologue, Web Series, Picture Book | genres: Comedy, Satire, Literary, Dark Comedy, Farce, Kids
**Avoid when:** The story is asking for grief, dread or suspense at full immersion. Every break is a small exit, and a reader who has been let out three times will not be all the way in for the fourth act. Also avoid in earnest realist drama, where it plays as the writer apologising for the material.
**Example:**
> Inside the wall: *He told them he had never met her. His hands were flat on the table, very still.*
> Through it, and paying for itself: *He told them he had never met her. Reader, I want you on my side here, so I am going to admit it now rather than let you find out in chapter nine: he had met her, and I was the one who introduced them.*

**Yields to:** `pov.second_person` — Implicate the audience without leaving the story.  
`pov.direct_address`

### The Reader-Surrogate Viewpoint

**Also called:** the audience surrogate, the newcomer POV, the everyman viewpoint, the fish out of water narrator
**What it is:** Assigning the viewpoint to the character who knows least — the new recruit, the visiting journalist, the bride entering the family, the apprentice — so the world can be explained to them and therefore to the reader.
**Effect on the audience:** Frictionless entry into a dense world. The reader is never ahead of or behind the narrator, and questions get asked out loud at the moment the reader forms them.
**Used for and where it works best:** Legitimate and very strong in exactly one condition: **the newcomer must want something from the world, not merely want to understand it.** The version that works is the recruit who needs to pass, the journalist who needs the story, the in-law who needs to be accepted — their ignorance is a *liability with stakes*, so every explanation is also a test they can fail. That is what separates it from the furniture problem of `pov.inert_viewpoint`, and it is also why the surrogate should start losing his outsider status by the end of act one; if he is still asking what things mean at the midpoint, he is a tour bus. Use it above all in worlds with heavy rules — institutions, subcultures, fantasy systems, trades — where `expo.newcomer_eye` is doing the exposition work anyway. Two upgrades worth the effort: give him a piece of expertise the insiders lack, so the flow of information runs both ways; and let one of his naive questions turn out to be the right question nobody asked. The failure side is catalogued at `char.audience_surrogate_trap` — read it before you commit.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Series, Feature Film, Game Narrative, Procedural, Middle Grade | genres: Fantasy, Science Fiction, Crime, Workplace, Medical, Coming of Age
**Avoid when:** The world is not strange. A surrogate in a familiar setting has nothing to be introduced to and becomes a passenger. Also avoid when you have a character with a real stake standing in the same room — give them the scene and let the reader work, per `pov.scene_ownership`.
**Example:**
> Passenger: *"So the night shift signs the book and then hands over to us?" Dana asked. Yusuf explained the system to her.*
> Staked: *Dana had signed the book at 06:02. She had watched Yusuf do it at 06:00 and copied him exactly, and she did not learn until Thursday that the two minutes were the entire point of the book.*

**Yields to:** `pov.scene_ownership` — Someone with a real stake is in the room.  
`pov.reader_surrogate`

## Difficult Owners

### The Posthumous Narrator

**Also called:** the dead narrator, first person posthumous, narration from beyond the grave, the ghost teller
**What it is:** The narrator is dead — either dead from the first page and telling it back, or dying partway through and continuing. No ghost need appear; the deadness is a property of the telling, not of the plot.
**Effect on the audience:** Grief with the suspense removed, and an unusual permission: a narrator with nothing left to lose can be honest in a way living narrators cannot. Readers also relax, in a strange way — the worst has already happened, so they can look directly at it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when the subject is aftermath rather than outcome: what a death did to the people around it, what the dead can now see about their own life, an unsolved case seen from the only viewpoint that knows the answer. The craft rules. **(1) Declare it early or make the discovery a real turn.** The two positions are "I was fourteen when I was murdered" on page one, and the late reveal that the narrator has been dead throughout — and the late version only works if everything before it re-reads correctly, so audit the whole manuscript for lines only a living narrator would write. **(2) Define the rules of the dead narrator's access and never exceed them.** Can she see all rooms, or only the ones she loved? Does she know the future? An unbounded dead narrator is just `pov.omniscient` with a sad backstory, and loses the specific pathos of a viewpoint that can see everything and touch nothing. **(3) Keep the want.** Death does not remove desire, and a dead narrator with no want is a camera in a cloud; the good ones desperately want something they cannot have — to be found, to be believed, to have their mother stop.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Feature Film, Limited Series, Audio Drama, Podcast | genres: Literary, Crime, Mystery, Family Drama, Horror, Faith
**Avoid when:** The book's engine is whether the protagonist survives, obviously — but the real trap is subtler: posthumous narration flattens all mid-book jeopardy for *that character*, so if she is your only viewpoint the middle three hundred pages have no floor. Also avoid the consolation default; a dead narrator who has achieved peace and understanding by chapter two has nowhere to go.
**Example:**
> Living retrospect: *I nearly drowned that summer and I have never been able to look at that stretch of river since.*
> Posthumous: *The river was two degrees colder than the week before, which nobody measured, and I am the only one who knows how long it took. My sister has told people three minutes for twenty years. It was closer to nine, and I would rather she kept saying three.*

**Yields to:** `pov.retrospective_distance` — Mid-book jeopardy must survive; living retrospect.  
`pov.posthumous_narrator`

### The Split Narrator

**Also called:** the divided self, multiple-personality narration, the dissociated narrator, alter narration, the second voice
**What it is:** One body, more than one narrating self — dissociative identity, an internal second speaker, a self who addresses himself in the second person, or an account in which the narrator plainly does not know what he did.
**Effect on the audience:** Genuine dread, and a distinctive reading experience: the reader begins to trust the *text* while distrusting the *teller*, and starts assembling a third account neither self can give.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when the subject is authorship of one's own actions — addiction, trauma, guilt, illness, radicalisation. Four craft rules keep it from becoming a gimmick. **(1) Give each self a different perceptual signature, not just a different opinion** — one notices exits and hands, the other notices what people meant; that is `char.what_they_notice` applied twice inside one skull. **(2) Mark the switch with something the reader can feel before she can name it** — a tense change, a lost hour, a shift in whether the narrator uses her own name. **(3) The gaps are the plot.** Write what falls into the seam between the selves and let the reader build the missing scene; this is the strongest legitimate use of `info.restricted_narration` inside a single character. **(4) Land the selves in a relationship by the end — merged, at war, one silenced — because a split that simply continues is an unfinished structure, not an ambiguous one.** The dissociative second person is a close cousin of `pov.second_person` and is often the honest reason a writer reached for "you" in the first place.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Feature Film, Limited Series, Audio Drama, Stage Play | genres: Psychological, Horror, Thriller, Literary, Addiction, Crime
**Avoid when:** The split is a plot twist and nothing else. "It was him all along" has been spent, and a reveal-only split retroactively converts every earlier page into a trick, which readers resent. Also avoid when you are using a real clinical condition as a horror engine without having read anything about it — see `pov.positionality`.
**Example:**
> One self: *I do not remember driving home. I must have driven home, because the car was in the drive and the keys were in the bowl.*
> The other, four pages later: *You drove home on the coast road because you wanted the long way and you have always wanted the long way. You put the keys in the bowl like a good boy. You washed your hands twice and you did not ask yourself why twice.*

**Yields to:** `pov.positionality` — A real clinical condition used as horror engine.  
`pov.split_narrator`

### Narrating a Radically Different Cognition

**Also called:** neurodivergent POV, the altered mind narrator, cognitive estrangement from inside, writing dementia, machine interiority
**What it is:** Building a viewpoint whose perception, memory, attention, time sense or causal model differ structurally from the assumed reader's — autism, psychosis, dementia, aphasia, an artificial mind, severe pain, extreme sleep deprivation.
**Effect on the audience:** The reader receives the world through a different filter and must do real work to assemble it, which produces both intimacy and the specific vertigo of not being able to check your own footing.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use it when the *difference itself* is the story's territory, not as a decoration on a thriller. The craft rules that separate the honest from the exploitative. **(1) Build the cognition as a consistent system, then obey it** — decide what this mind foregrounds, what it cannot hold, how it makes causal links, what it finds unbearable, and never break the rules for plot convenience. **(2) The prose must be readable even where the perception is not.** Difficulty should live in the *content* of the perception, not in wilfully broken syntax; three hundred pages of shattered grammar is a stunt and readers leave. **(3) The character must be competent at something inside his own frame.** A mind that only fails is a symptom list, and a symptom list is not a viewpoint. **(4) Do the primary research** — first-person accounts written by people with the condition, not other novels about it. A cognition assembled from other fiction is how the same four clichés keep recirculating, and it is the reason so many of these viewpoints feel familiar on first meeting. For a cognition with no human referent at all — a machine, a hive, a god — the constraint discipline of `pov.nonhuman_pov` applies directly.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Feature Film, Limited Series, Audio Drama, Game Narrative | genres: Literary, Psychological, Science Fiction, Family Drama, Medical, Horror
**Avoid when:** You need the reader to track a complex plot at speed. Every rung of cognitive difference costs comprehension bandwidth, and a heist plot inside a dementia POV will lose both. Also avoid using the condition as the twist — "he was unreliable because he is ill" converts a person into a device.
**Example:**
> Outside: *He had dementia and became confused about which house he was in.*
> Inside, and systematic: *The kitchen was the right kitchen and the window was in the wrong wall. I have known this window forty years. Someone had moved it in the night, which was a great deal of work for a joke, and the woman making tea was being very kind about it, and I did not ask her name because you cannot ask twice.*

**Yields to:** `pov.nonhuman_pov` — The mind has no human referent at all.  
`pov.divergent_cognition`

### Positionality and Writing Across Difference

**Also called:** writing the other, standpoint and viewpoint, the outsider's POV, cultural distance, authenticity work
**What it is:** The craft problem created by the fact that a narrator's gender, class, faith, language and position in a community determine what she notices, what she can name, what she takes for granted and what she has to explain — and that a writer from outside that position will get those four things wrong by default, not by malice.
**Effect on the audience:** Readers inside the community detect the wrongness instantly and stop believing the book. Readers outside it absorb the errors as fact. Both outcomes are craft failures before they are anything else.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any project whose viewpoint sits outside the writer's own experience, which is most projects. What a writer actually does, in order. **First, name the default story you are carrying** about this group — the version you absorbed from film and news — and treat it as a list of things to complicate rather than a source. **Second, go to people rather than to fiction.** Other novels about a community encode the same errors, and a voice assembled from other books is a photocopy of a photocopy. Interviews, memoirs, letters, court records, recipes, arguments about money. **Third, work out what the position changes about *distance specifically*** — the piece most writers skip. A narrator inside a community does not explain its rituals to herself, so an insider POV that describes its own culture has silently become an outsider POV with an insider's name on it; that is `pov.tour_guide_narrator`. Conversely, an outsider focaliser makes explanation legitimate, which is often the honest structural answer: write the outsider you are, per `char.inside_outside_culture`. **Fourth, build the character from specifics, not from category** — `char.specificity_not_representation`. One person with a job, a debt and a bad habit will always read truer than a well-researched representative. **Fifth, bring in readers from the community while the draft can still change**, and pay them. A sensitivity or authenticity read is a craft note, not a permission slip; it arrives near the end and cannot substitute for any of the four steps above, and the notes worth most are usually not "this is offensive" but "nobody would say that", "you have the wrong grandmother doing the cooking", "this ritual happens in the morning". **Sixth, decide what you are not going to write.** Declining a viewpoint you cannot build is a craft decision like any other, not a defeat. Where material is sacred or contested, `theme.sacred_material` governs.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Limited Series, Heritage Documentary, Short Story, Stage Play | genres: Literary, Historical, Heritage, Social Drama, Migration, Faith
**Avoid when:** Nothing to avoid — the work is unavoidable the moment you write anyone other than yourself. The two real failure modes are worth naming plainly. One: the narrator who explains his own culture outward, which is a POV error that always announces a writer working from outside. Two: the voice built from other fiction, which produces a character every reader has met before and no reader has ever met.
**Example:**
> Outsider's cadence in an insider's mouth: *We celebrated Eid al-Fitr, the festival that marks the end of Ramadan, the holy month of fasting.*
> Insider: *Mama had the good plates down by six and we were not allowed near them. Thirty days of nothing and the first thing anybody ate was a date, standing up, out of the packet.*

**Yields to:** `theme.sacred_material` — The material is sacred or contested.  
`pov.positionality`

## POV Under Structural Pressure

### POV in the Short Story

**Also called:** short-form viewpoint, the single-POV rule, compressed POV, the story-length contract
**What it is:** Viewpoint handling in a form with no room to establish a contract — the reader must know who is telling it, how close, and how much they are to be trusted, inside the first two sentences, and there is no space to teach a second position.
**Effect on the audience:** When it works the reader never notices a decision was made. When it fails the reader spends the first page orienting, which in a 4,000-word story is a quarter of the total attention.
**Used for and where it works best:** Every story. The rules are harder than at novel length because the margin is smaller. **One viewpoint, and enter it in the first sentence** — not the first paragraph. **Choose your rung in the first line and stay inside a two-rung band**; the wide-to-close-to-wide arc of `pov.distance_slide` is a novel-scene move and reads as seasickness across 3,000 words. **Put the POV character at the point of maximum consequence** — a short story has room for one value turn, and whoever owns that turn owns the story. **Establish reliability in the first paragraph**, because there is no time to build a case for unreliability and then cash it; short fiction's unreliable narrators must announce their angle immediately, in the grain of a sentence. The two legitimate multi-position exceptions are the short story cycle, where each story owns one POV, and the true ensemble story where the group is the subject, per `pov.collective_we`. Flash fiction tightens all of this further: at 500 words the POV *is* the structure, and the entire piece is usually one perception arriving.
**Best in:** formats: Short Story, Novella, Reels/Shorts, Vertical Micro-Drama, Poetry, Short Film | genres: Literary, Horror, Crime, Slice of Life, Coming of Age, Surreal
**Avoid when:** The idea genuinely needs two incompatible accounts. Then it is a novella, a cycle, or a different story — do not compress `pov.same_event_twice` into 4,000 words, because the second pass will not have room to add a turn and will read as repetition.
**Example:**
> Slow entry — POV unresolved for three lines: *The house on Meridian Street had been empty for a year. The grass was high. A car pulled up at the kerb and a woman got out, carrying a folder.*
> Entered in the first sentence: *The grass had got away from them, which Nadia counted as the first thing in her favour, and she had counted nothing in her favour since March.*

**Yields to:** `pov.same_event_twice` — The idea needs two incompatible accounts.  
`pov.short_form_pov`

### The Prologue POV Problem

**Also called:** the orphan prologue, the one-off viewpoint, the doomed-character prologue, the prologue contract breach
**What it is:** A prologue narrated by a viewpoint the book then abandons — a murdered stranger, a soldier at a battle a thousand years earlier, a god — which silently teaches the reader a contract the novel does not intend to keep.
**Effect on the audience:** The reader signs the contract in good faith. She invests in a consciousness, learns the book's distance and tense from it, and then never sees it again. What she registers is not "that was a prologue"; it is a low-level distrust of the narration from chapter one onward.
**Used for and where it works best:** Prologues are fine; *orphan viewpoints* are the problem. Three ways to do it honestly. **(1) Make the prologue POV a real POV that returns** — once, ideally, at the climax, which converts the whole thing from a trick into a frame. **(2) Use a viewpoint that is openly a different order of thing** — an institutional voice, a document, a chronicle entry, a myth register — so no contract about *character* viewpoint is signed at all; `open.prologue_other_register` covers the mechanics, `pov.institutional_pov` and `pov.found_document` the positions. **(3) Cut it and put the information in chapter three, where it is usually better.** The blunt craft rule: **if your prologue's viewpoint character dies at the end of the prologue, you have written a hook, not a prologue,** and a hook belongs in the body of the book where it can be paid off. The same applies to the doomed-teenager horror cold open on screen, with one difference — screen audiences accept a discarded viewpoint far more readily than readers do, because `pov.screen_no_interiority` means they never entered a mind in the first place.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Series, Feature Film, Television Pilot, Game Narrative, Serialised Fiction | genres: Fantasy, Thriller, Horror, Crime, Historical, Epic
**Avoid when:** You are using the prologue to establish tone because chapter one has none. That is a chapter-one problem and a prologue will not fix it — it will simply make the reader wait twice. See `open.prologue_disguised_chapter`.
**Example:**
> Orphan: two thousand words in the close third of a fisherman who sees something in the water and drowns; the novel is thereafter in the close third of a detective, and the fisherman is never mentioned again.
> Repaired, same information, no contract signed: *Coroner's report, Marish District, entry 114. Male, approx. 60. Water in the lungs. Both hands broken, which the attending officer recorded as consistent with a fall and which the coroner, who had seen nine falls that year, did not.*

**Yields to:** `pov.institutional_pov` — Prologue needs a different order of voice.  
`pov.prologue_pov`

### POV Across a Series

**Also called:** long-form viewpoint management, series POV drift, the expanding cast problem, viewpoint inflation
**What it is:** The handling of viewpoint across multiple books or seasons, where the cast grows, the original POV character's ignorance gets used up, and the contract set in book one has to survive five more.
**Effect on the audience:** Readers of a series are contract experts — they have read a thousand pages under one set of rules and they feel a change in book three as a change in the *series*, not in the book.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any project planned beyond one instalment. The rules are about restraint. **Set the maximum number of viewpoints in book one and treat it as a hard budget**, because POV counts only ever inflate: every series that started with three ends with eleven, the rotation stretches, threads go cold for four hundred pages, and readers begin skipping chapters — which is the terminal symptom. **A new POV added later must arrive with a want that predates it**, so it reads as a promotion rather than a hire. **Solve the used-up-ignorance problem deliberately**: your protagonist learned the world in book one and cannot be a `pov.reader_surrogate` again, so book two must move him to a position where he is ignorant of something new — a new institution, a new city, a truth about himself — or hand the newcomer function to an actual newcomer. **Protect the distance band, not just the position** — a series that opened at rung 4 and has drifted to rung 2 by book four feels, to a reader, like the characters got colder; that is `voice.series_voice_match` and `voice.tonal_drift` measured in POV terms. And decide early whether a character can *lose* viewpoint status; retiring a POV is legitimate and much cleaner than keeping a token chapter for someone whose story finished two books ago.
**Best in:** formats: Series, Serialised Fiction, Novel, One-Hour Drama, Comics, Game Narrative | genres: Fantasy, Crime, Science Fiction, Epic, Thriller, Historical
**Avoid when:** Never avoided in a series; but avoid the specific temptation to open book two in a brand-new viewpoint to signal scale. Readers return to a series for a person, and making them wait sixty pages to find her is the single most common reason a second book is abandoned.
**Example:**
> Book one, three POVs, tight: chapters headed ANA, MAREK, THE CHAPLAIN.
> Book four, inflated, and the reader's experience of it: chapters headed ANA, MAREK, THE CHAPLAIN, ISKANDER, VERA, THE BOY, THE CHAPLAIN'S SISTER, DR VOSS — and Ana, who is the reason anyone bought book one, last appeared ninety pages ago and wanted nothing when she did.

`pov.series_pov`

### Ensemble Ownership in the Writers' Room

**Also called:** whose episode is it, A/B/C story assignment, episode POV, story area ownership, the runner
**What it is:** The television practice of assigning each strand of an episode to a character who owns it — the A story, the B story, the C story or runner — so that an ensemble show still has, every week, a person whose episode it is.
**Effect on the audience:** Weekly coherence. An audience cannot hold six equal strands, but it can hold one lead strand with two commenting on it, and it will remember an episode by whose it was.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any ensemble show, and useful in any multi-strand prose narrative. The working rules as rooms actually apply them. **The A story owns the episode's question and the most screen time; the B story should rhyme with it thematically rather than duplicate it; the C story or runner is usually comic and exists to break pressure.** In a true ensemble the room may break A/A/A instead — separate full-weight stories per lead, beaten out independently and then interleaved — and the price is that no episode belongs to anyone, which is why A/A/A shows lean on a strong series-level engine to compensate. **Track ownership across the season on a grid**, because the real failure is invisible in any single script: a regular who has not owned an A story in nine episodes has quietly become furniture, actors notice before writers do, and the fix is a season-shape fix, not a scene fix. `rel.rotating_spotlight` and `char.ensemble_grid` hold the cast-level version of the same bookkeeping. The prose equivalent is worth stealing: a chapter that serves three threads equally is usually a chapter that serves none, and assigning an owner per chapter is `pov.scene_ownership` scaled up.
**Best in:** formats: Series, One-Hour Drama, Half-Hour Comedy, Procedural, Soap, Television Pilot | genres: Ensemble, Workplace, Medical, Crime, Comedy, Family Drama
**Avoid when:** The show is a single-protagonist vehicle. Forcing B-story ownership onto supporting characters in a show that is structurally about one person dilutes the spine and produces the "why are we with these people" cutaway.
**Example:**
> No ownership: three strands of roughly equal weight, none of which turns a value, and an episode nobody can name afterwards.
> Owned: *A — Yusuf has to tell the family the clinic is closing (his episode: he chose the closure). B — Rana trains the replacement she knows is unnecessary (rhymes: teaching something with no future). C/runner — the vending machine takes everyone's money all week and is fixed in the last scene, badly.*

**Yields to:** `pov.scene_ownership` — Single-protagonist vehicle; own scenes, not strands.  
`pov.episode_ownership`

## POV in Other Deliveries

### POV in Audio Drama and Podcast Fiction

**Also called:** audio POV, sound-first viewpoint, the listener's position, binaural POV, podcast fiction narration
**What it is:** Viewpoint in a medium with no image, where position is built from three things only: who narrates, whose acoustic space the microphone occupies, and what the listener is allowed to hear.
**Effect on the audience:** Audio is the most intimate narrative medium and the most easily lost. A listener placed clearly inside one character's ears will follow anything; a listener with no fixed position spends the episode working out who is speaking and stops listening within two minutes.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any fiction podcast, audio drama, or audiobook-first work. Choose one of three positions and hold it for the episode. **Narrated**: a teller addresses the listener, with scenes played beneath — the most robust structure and the reason most successful fiction podcasts use it, because the narrator can fix any confusion in one clause. **Found-audio**: the fiction *is* recordings — tapes, voice memos, a call log, a broadcast — which is `pov.found_document` in sound, and gives you the strongest built-in reason for gaps, distortion and abrupt endings. **Dramatised**: pure scene, no narrator, position established acoustically. The craft rules that are specific to the ear: **the listener's POV is wherever the room reverb says it is**, so a character whose voice is dry and close while everyone else is roomy *is* the viewpoint character, with no line of narration required; **interiority is available and cheap** — a close, unprocessed inward voice against a reverberant scene is free indirect style for the ear, and it is the medium's best asset; and **name people in dialogue far more often than feels natural on the page**, because a listener cooking dinner has no chapter heading to check. Note the trap unique to audio: `pov.head_hopping` is inaudible as a defect and reads instead as *bad casting*, because the listener blames the voices for being hard to tell apart.
**Best in:** formats: Audio Drama, Podcast, Audiobook, Voiceover Script, Interview Podcast, Immersive | genres: Horror, Mystery, Science Fiction, Crime, Testimony, Drama
**Avoid when:** The story depends on simultaneous action in several places. Audio has no wide shot; a battle, a chase or a crowded room collapses into noise unless one person's experience of it is the whole scene.
**Example:**
> Positionless: *"We should go." "I'm not going." "Then I'll go on my own."* — three lines, and a listener cannot say whose scene this is.
> Positioned, no narrator needed: [close, dry, and breathing] *"We should go."* [the other voice arrives from across a hard room, half a second of tail on it] *"I'm not going."* [close again, and now the breathing is faster] *"Then I'll go on my own."*

**Yields to:** `pov.oral_narration` — Simultaneous action; a narrating teller must carry it.  
`pov.audio_pov`

### POV in Lyric and Song

**Also called:** the singer's position, lyric persona, songwriting point of view, who is the "you" in the song
**What it is:** The choice of who sings — the "I" as apparently the artist, an "I" who is plainly a character, a "you" being addressed, a "we" claiming a group — and the distance that choice sets between the listener and the feeling.
**Effect on the audience:** A song's POV decides whether the listener *identifies* or *watches*. A first-person song is a garment the listener puts on; a third-person song is a story she is told about someone else, and is far harder to make anyone cry to.
**Used for and where it works best:** Every lyric. The working rules. **First person for the feeling, third person for the story, second person for the address** — and the single most exportable move in commercial songwriting is the split: character or scene in the verses, direct feeling in the chorus, which lets the song narrate and still be singable. **The "you" must be one person and stay that person.** Drifting between "you, my lover" and "you, the listener" inside a song is the most common amateur error in lyric writing and it is catalogued at `lyric.pronoun_drift`. **A written-for-another-artist lyric has two positions at once** — the character's and the singer's — and a line the artist cannot plausibly say is dead on arrival regardless of how good it is, which is the whole of `lyric.written_for_the_voice`. In kids' songs the position is usually "we", because the point is that the room sings it, and a first-person kids' song quietly excludes the listener from the action. The category-level choice sits at `lyric.point_of_view_choice`; what belongs here is distance: **a song can only carry one rung**, so a lyric that starts inside the feeling and then describes it from outside has broken something the melody cannot repair.
**Best in:** formats: Song Lyric, Music Video, Lyric Video, Musical Theatre, Kids Song, Poetry | genres: Romance, Literary, Faith, Heritage, Kids, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The song is for a congregation, a crowd or a classroom and you have written it in a specific first person. A stadium cannot sing a lyric that belongs to one biography; see `lyric.congregational_lyric`. Also avoid third person in a song whose job is comfort — nobody is consoled by a report.
**Example:**
> Third person throughout, and inert: *She waits by the door for a man who won't call / She counts all the hours and she stares at the wall.*
> Verse in character, chorus in feeling: *She waits by the door for a man who won't call —* / chorus: *and I have been that woman, I have been that door, / I have counted every hour, I am not counting any more.*

**Yields to:** `lyric.congregational_lyric` — The song is for a congregation, crowd or classroom.  
`pov.song_pov`

### POV in Advertising and Brand Voice

**Also called:** brand person, the "we" problem, customer-centric copy, second-person marketing, corporate first person
**What it is:** The choice of who speaks in commercial copy — the brand as "we", the customer as "you", an unattributed third-person voice, or a named individual — and how much distance that puts between the message and the reader.
**Effect on the audience:** A brand talking about itself is background noise. A brand talking about the reader is information. The pronoun does most of that work before a single claim is made.
**Used for and where it works best:** All commercial copy, and the rule is dull and reliable: **lead in second person, use first person sparingly and only for authority or personality, and never open on "we".** The customer's question is what this does for her, and "you" gets there in one word while "we" makes her translate. Where the other positions genuinely win: **first person singular** for founder stories, creator ads and anything whose credibility comes from one person having actually done the thing — and it must then be a real named person, because an anonymous "I" reads as a lie; **first person plural** for institutional promises where the entity's continuity is the product — banks, hospitals, a hundred-year-old manufacturer; **third person** for spec, comparison and proof, where the absence of a speaker is what makes the numbers credible. Two craft rules that cost money when broken. **Fix the position per asset and never mix inside one** — a landing page that opens "We believe in better mornings" and closes "Get yours today" has changed speakers mid-paragraph and the reader feels the seam as insincerity. **Write the brand's POV into the voice document as a rule with examples, not as an adjective** — "friendly" is unusable; "we say *you*, we never say *we* before the third sentence, we do not use the customer's first name" is usable. The register side is `voice.advertising_register` and `voice.brand_voice_intake`; the position is here. UGC and creator formats invert all of the above deliberately: the speaker is a first-person individual and the brand is a third party in her story, which is exactly why it converts.
**Best in:** formats: Advertising, Brand Film, UGC Ad, Product Video, Corporate, UX Writing | genres: Lifestyle, Advocacy, Educational, Workplace, Journalism, Kids
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-03. Platform norms for brand address move fast: the founder-first-person and creator-voice conventions dominant now are a cycle, and the "we" register they displaced was itself dominant a decade ago.
**Avoid when:** The subject is an apology, a recall or a failure. There "we" is mandatory and "you" is offensive — the audience needs a responsible speaker, and second-person copy in a crisis reads as the brand explaining the customer's feelings to her.
**Example:**
> Brand-centred: *We've spent fifteen years perfecting our roasting process to bring you a richer cup.*
> Reader-centred, same fact: *Your coffee is bitter because it was roasted too fast. Ours takes fifteen years longer to explain and about four minutes to taste.*

`pov.brand_pov`

### Oral and Heritage Narration

**Also called:** the traditional teller, performed narration, the transmission chain, "I heard it from", storyteller's POV
**What it is:** Viewpoint in a story told aloud to a present audience, where the teller is visibly *relaying* rather than inventing, and the chain of transmission — who told him, who told her — is spoken aloud as part of the telling.
**Effect on the audience:** Authority of a different kind. The teller is not claiming to have seen it; he is claiming that it was handed to him, and the audience judges the chain rather than the witness. It also makes the audience a participant: they are the next link.
**Used for and where it works best:** Heritage documentary, narrated history, folk and religious material, and any prose that wants the texture of a told story rather than a written one. The craft rules. **Speak the attribution** — *my grandmother had it from the priest at Alqosh, and he had it wrong about the year* — which does three things at once: it dates the account, it licenses the gaps, and it makes disagreement between versions part of the content instead of an embarrassment, per `info.communal_version` and `info.disputed_history`. **The teller is present and may be corrected**, which is the position's great advantage over a scripted voiceover: a narrator who says *they say it was nine days; my uncle said four, and my uncle was there* has built credibility no omniscient narration can buy. **Use the formulas.** Openings, refrains, repeated epithets and the rule of three are not decoration; in a told story they are the listener's navigation, and stripping them for a written-feeling script removes the handholds. **Write to be spoken** — breath-length clauses, names repeated rather than pronouns, per `voice.write_for_the_ear`. For heritage work specifically, decide whether the teller is inside or outside the community before writing a line, because the explaining-outward failure of `pov.tour_guide_narrator` is endemic to the format and `theme.sacred_material` governs what a teller may claim at all.
**Best in:** formats: Heritage Documentary, Documentary, Audio Drama, Podcast, Stage Play, Kids Story | genres: Heritage, Myth, Historical, Faith, Testimony, Fable
**Avoid when:** Precision is the deliverable. A transmission-chain narrator is structurally unable to be authoritative about dates and numbers, and in an educational or journalistic piece the hedging that makes him honest makes the piece useless. Use `pov.reportage_stance` instead.
**Example:**
> Scripted omniscient voiceover: *In 1915, the villagers of Tur Abdin fled into the mountains over a period of nine days.*
> Teller: *Nine days, they say. My grandmother said nine days and she said it every year, and she was four years old at the time, so I give you the nine days the way she gave them to me, and I will tell you now that the records say eleven and I have never once corrected her.*

**Yields to:** `pov.reportage_stance` — Precision is the deliverable.  
`pov.oral_narration`

### AI-Authored Narration

**Also called:** prompt-authored POV, model narration, LLM fiction voice, the generated narrator
**What it is:** Narration produced by a language model, either as draft material or shipped, which carries a characteristic and identifiable viewpoint behaviour — and the craft problem of directing, detecting and repairing it.
**Effect on the audience:** Readers who cannot name the cause report the same three things: that it "reads smoothly but doesn't land", that everything is "explained", and that they never felt inside anyone. That is a distance failure, and it is the most reliable tell the form currently has.
**Used for and where it works best:** Use models for what they are good at — coverage, alternatives, structural passes, the twelfth version of a line — and direct the viewpoint explicitly, because the default is bad in a consistent and therefore fixable way. The measured defaults, as of the 2026 studies: generated fiction **states its theme rather than letting a reader infer it**, **resolves toward tidy single-track plots with low moral ambiguity**, **describes characters from outside** rather than perceiving from within, and clusters in a narrow region of narrative space compared with human-authored work. In POV terms all four are one fault: **the model narrates at rung 2 and summarises interiority instead of rendering it.** What actually works in the prompt: name the rung, name the focaliser, forbid the filter verbs of `pov.filter_word_removal`, specify the narratee, and give it the permission test from `pov.close_third` as an explicit constraint. What does not work: asking for "deep POV" as an adjective. On the repair side, the tells are diagnosable — summarised emotion (*she felt a wave of relief wash over her*), the perfectly balanced antithesis, every character noticing their own appearance, dialogue in which everyone is equally articulate, and paragraph shapes that all resolve. The register-level version is `voice.ai_default_register`; the interactive-generation version is `inter.guarded_generation`. The rule that matters most for anyone shipping this material: **a human must own the distance decisions**, because a model will hold a position you specify and will never notice that the position was wrong for the scene.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Serialised Fiction, Game Narrative, Conversational Agent, Explainer | genres: Literary, Science Fiction, Fantasy, Thriller, Kids, Educational
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-03. Every specific tell below is a property of current model behaviour and of current prompting practice; the underlying craft rules are durable, the diagnostics are not, and some of these will be obsolete within two model generations.
**Avoid when:** The piece's value is testimony, memoir, heritage or anything where a real person's authority is the product. Generated narration in first-person testimony is a category error regardless of quality, and audiences treat its discovery as fraud rather than as a craft complaint.
**Example:**
> Default output, rung 2, interiority summarised: *Leila felt a wave of grief wash over her as she realised that her mother was truly gone, and she knew that nothing would ever be the same again.*
> Same beat, directed to rung 5 with filters forbidden and a focaliser named: *The tea was still warm. Somebody had made it and put it down and that somebody had been her mother, and the cup was still warm, and Leila stood there holding it because the arithmetic of that would not come out.*

**Yields to:** `pov.oral_narration` — Testimony or heritage; a real teller's authority.  
`pov.ai_narration`

## The Workbench

### The Shifting-Distance Opening Page

**Also called:** the distance drill, the five-rung exercise, the Gardner exercise, opening-page calibration
**What it is:** A craft exercise: write the same opening page five times, once at each rung of `pov.psychic_distance_ladder`, changing nothing about the events — then choose, and then write a sixth version that moves deliberately from one rung to another.
**Effect on the audience:** On the finished page, the reader feels an opening that knows where it stands. On the writer, the effect is larger: distance stops being a vague quality and becomes a control.
**Used for and where it works best:** Run it at the start of any project, and again on any book whose opening "isn't working" when the events are fine. The instructions, precisely. **One page, one moment, five versions.** Version 1 is the world before the person; version 5 is unmediated sensation with no name attached. Do not improve the prose between versions — change only the distance, or the experiment is worthless. **Then read all five aloud and notice which one makes you want to write page two**; that is usually, though not always, the book's home rung. **Then write the sixth version, which starts one rung out from your choice and arrives at it by the end of the page** — because that is the shape almost every strong opening actually has, and it is `open.first_paragraph_contents` and `pov.distance_slide` doing their jobs together. Two diagnostics fall out of it for free: if versions 3, 4 and 5 are indistinguishable, your prose is not yet responsive to distance and the problem is filter verbs; if version 1 is the only one you can write, you do not yet know the character well enough to get inside her, which is a `char.what_they_notice` problem wearing a POV costume.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Memoir, Novella, Essay, Long-Form Non-Fiction | genres: Literary, Drama, Coming of Age, Historical, Crime, Psychological
**Avoid when:** You are mid-draft and moving. This is a start-of-project and repair tool; running it at chapter fourteen is a very sophisticated way of not writing chapter fifteen.
**Example:**
> Rung 1: *The road out of the town had been resurfaced twice since the mine closed and was still the worst road in the county.*
> Rung 3: *Sami hated this road.*
> Rung 5: *Third pothole. Fourth. The suspension going like something being asked a question it could not answer, and eleven miles of this, and at the end of it the house and whoever was in it now.*

`pov.opening_page_exercise`

### The Distance Audit

**Also called:** POV pass, the viewpoint revision toolkit, distance consistency check, the permission sweep
**What it is:** A structured revision pass over an existing draft that checks viewpoint and distance as their own layer — separately from plot, character and line editing — and repairs them.
**Effect on the audience:** Readers never see the audit; they see a draft that stopped feeling uneven. Most reader notes of "I lost interest in the middle", "I didn't connect with her" and "it felt like a summary" are distance problems that survived every other kind of edit because no other pass looks for them.
**Used for and where it works best:** Run it once, after structure is locked and before line editing — auditing distance in a draft whose scenes may still be cut is wasted labour. The pass, in order. **(1) Build the POV map.** One row per scene: owner, person, tense, rung, and one line on what only this owner could contribute. Gaps and inert rows show up immediately, and you are checking against `pov.inert_viewpoint` and `pov.scene_ownership`. **(2) Check the rotation.** Weight per POV in pages, longest absence per POV, and whether any thread goes cold for more than two chapters. **(3) Run the permission test on the descriptive lines** — every sentence in a limited POV must be perceivable or inferable by the owner; this catches `pov.impossible_observation` and it is the highest-yield hour in the whole pass. **(4) Mark the rung of each scene against its function.** Setup and transit at 1–2, value turns at 4–5. Any turn sitting at rung 2 is your flatness — unless the pull-back is the design, the moment of maximum pain held wide on purpose as `pov.distant_third` prescribes, with warmth earned nearby; the audit catches the accident, not the choice. **(5) Do the filter sweep** — search *saw, heard, felt, noticed, realised, watched, seemed, could see* and delete every instance where the act of perceiving is not itself the beat, per `pov.filter_word_removal`. **(6) Check the seams.** Every POV change should be at a break, and the new owner named in the first sentence, per `pov.handoff`. **(7) Check tense and contract consistency end to end**, including whether the narrator's knowledge of the ending stays constant. The related passes are `sub.draft_subtext_audit` and `theme.thematic_audit`; run them separately, because a combined pass reliably degrades into line editing.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Novella, Short Story, Serialised Fiction, Memoir, Feature Film | genres: Literary, Drama, Thriller, Crime, Historical, Fantasy
**Avoid when:** The structure is still moving. Also avoid doing it in the same pass as the prose polish — the moment you start improving sentences you stop seeing distance, which is a property of paragraphs.
**Example:**
> Two rows of a real POV map, and what they tell you:
> *Ch 14 — Marek — 3rd past — rung 2 — he learns the clinic is closing.* → the chapter's value turn is sitting at rung 2. That is the flat middle everyone flagged. Rewrite at 4.
> *Ch 15 — Vera — 3rd past — rung 4 — she watches Marek leave.* → she wants nothing, changes nothing, and last appeared in chapter 3. Give the scene to Marek or cut it.

`pov.distance_audit`

## The Failure Modes

### The Tour-Guide Narrator

**Also called:** explaining outward, the native informant voice, the anthropological insider, culture-splaining narration
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A viewpoint character presented as an insider who nonetheless explains her own culture, religion, family structure or language to an audience she cannot know is there — glossing her own terms, dating her own festivals, describing her own rituals as though seeing them for the first time.
**Effect on the audience:** Readers from inside the community put the book down, because nobody narrates their own life as a museum caption. Readers from outside it get an experience they mistake for authenticity and which is in fact a guided tour. Either way the POV has silently become external while claiming to be internal.
**Used for and where it works best:** It doesn't, and it is worth being precise about the mechanism, because it is a *distance* fault rather than a research fault — the writer has done the research and then put it in the wrong mouth. An insider's knowledge is invisible to the insider: she does not know the English name of the festival, she knows her aunt arrives late to it. The legitimate fixes, in order of usefulness. **Give the explaining to an outsider who is actually in the scene** — a spouse, a visitor, a child, a colleague; then the explanation is dialogue with a stake, per `char.inside_outside_culture` and `expo.newcomer_eye`. **Convert the gloss into friction**: instead of explaining what the thing is, show it going wrong, being argued about, or costing money. **Leave the untranslated word untranslated** and let context carry it, per `dial.untranslated_line` — readers tolerate far more unexplained vocabulary than writers believe, and the unexplained word is itself the strongest signal that we are inside. **Move the explanation to an apparatus the narrator is not responsible for** — a frame, an epigraph, a document — if it genuinely must be there. And accept the real trade: some of the research does not go in the book. It stays in the notes and shows up as confidence.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Always, in a viewpoint that claims insider status. It is not a failure in an outsider POV, where explanation is honest, and it is not a failure in a `pov.reportage_stance` or an explicitly educational piece, where the audience is declared. It hurts most in heritage, diaspora and historical fiction, which is exactly where writers reach for it hardest — and where, per `info.heritage_audience_correction`, part of the audience knows the material better than the author does.
**Example:**
> Tour guide: *Mama lit the Saturday-night lamp for Sahra d'Sawma, the Assyrian vigil that begins the Lenten fast, which our people have kept for sixteen centuries.*
> Inside: *Mama had the lamp out on Saturday and the argument started before it was lit, because Auntie Janet had brought a cake, and you cannot eat cake after tomorrow and everybody knew it, including Auntie Janet, who had brought it precisely for that reason.*

**Yields to:** `pov.reader_surrogate` — Give the explaining to an outsider in the scene.
