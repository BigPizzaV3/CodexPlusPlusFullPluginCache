## Reassurance and bad news

### Reassurance That Works on Adults

**Also called:** adult reassurance, crisis reassurance, bounded bad news, the frightened and literate reader
**What it is:** Reassurance assembled from three checkable things — what is known, what is not known yet, and what happens next with a name and a time on it. It is not comfort, and it contains no promise about the outcome.
**Effect on the audience:** The restored sense that a system is still running and someone is inside it. A frightened adult is not scanning for kindness; they are scanning for evidence of control, and specificity is the only evidence that survives being checked.
**Used for and where it works best:** Medical, safety, crisis and outage communication, and any drama that reproduces one. The rule that does all the work: **every sentence of reassurance must carry a number, a name or a next action.** "We are doing everything we can" carries none of the three and is heard as concealment, because it is the sentence people say when there is nothing to report. Two supporting rules. **State your uncertainty yourself, first** — an admitted unknown buys credibility, a discovered one destroys it. And **give them one thing to do**; crisis-communication practice is consistent that a specific action is what returns a sense of control, which is why the instruction matters more than the consolation. Pairs with `voice.authority_by_specificity` and `anta.bounded_threat`.
**Best in:** formats: PSA, Corporate, Explainer, Voiceover Script, UX Writing, Documentary | genres: Medical, Journalism, Advocacy, Social Drama, Educational, Disaster
**Avoid when:** You cannot keep the commitment you are about to make. A missed callback time costs more than never having given one, because the audience was counting. Also avoid inside fiction whose job is for the audience to be afraid — reassurance is a ceiling on dread and will lower it.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "Try not to worry. We're doing everything we possibly can, and we'll be in touch as soon as we know more."
> **Working:** "The scan showed something on your left lung. I don't know yet what it is. The biopsy is Thursday at ten, it takes about forty minutes, and I will phone you with the result by Monday afternoon. If you haven't heard from me by five on Monday, call this number and ask for me by name."

**Yields to:** `emo.dread_vs_fear` — Fiction whose job is for the audience to fear.  
`emo.adult_reassurance`

### Delivering the Bad News

**Also called:** breaking bad news, the notification scene, the warning shot, the doctor's line
**What it is:** The scene, or the piece, whose job is to say the unbearable thing and then stay in the room. Its shape is fixed: a sentence of warning, the fact in one plain sentence, then silence.
**Effect on the audience:** Written correctly it produces the real thing, which is not weeping but a stopped clock. Comprehension collapses at the fact, so everything after it is received as tone rather than as content — and an audience knows this from their own lives, which is why a faked version is so visible.
**Used for and where it works best:** Medical scenes, death notifications, dismissals, the police at the door, and the commissioned version — the script or the script note for someone who will have to do it. Four rules. **One:** fire a warning shot one beat before the fact ("I'm afraid this is difficult news"); it is the step of the medical protocols that patients most consistently say they wanted, and on the page it is what makes the deliverer a person rather than a device. **Two:** deliver the fact in one short sentence with no subordinate clause and no euphemism — *he died*, not *he didn't make it*. **Three:** then stop talking. The pause is the technique, and the reliable tell of a fake is a deliverer who keeps going. **Four:** anything the recipient needs to retain must be written down or repeated later, because nothing said in the following minute is kept.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Stage Play, Limited Series, Audio Drama | genres: Medical, Drama, Family Drama, Crime, War, Procedural
**Avoid when:** The scene's real subject is the deliverer's discomfort and the news is being used as a stage for it — audiences read that instantly and resent it. And never write the recipient's reaction as a stage direction; see `emo.emotion_written_as_direction` and `emo.leave_it_for_the_performer`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "I'm so sorry — we did everything we could, the team worked on him for nearly an hour, but in the end his heart just wasn't strong enough and he slipped away very peacefully, he wouldn't have felt any pain at all—"
> **Working:**
> "I have bad news."
> She waits. Two seconds, maybe three.
> "He died at ten past four."
> Nobody says anything. The radiator ticks. Eventually the woman asks whether there is something she needs to sign.

**Yields to:** `emo.leave_it_for_the_performer` — Writing the recipient's reaction.  
`emo.bad_news_delivery`

### Reassurance You Cannot Keep

**Also called:** false reassurance, the comforting lie, toxic positivity, "everything will be fine"
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** Reassurance offered as a guarantee about an outcome the speaker does not control — *it'll be fine*, *everything happens for a reason*, *you're going to beat this*.
**Effect on the audience:** In the moment it appears to work, because the listener goes quiet — which is why writers keep reaching for it and mistake it for a landed beat. What it actually does is close the conversation, and grief-support practice is unanimous that the quiet is compliance rather than comfort. It also spends credibility forward: when the outcome is bad, every earlier line is reread as management.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere in the piece's own voice. It is legitimate as characterisation — a character who says it is telling you what they themselves cannot tolerate hearing — and that is the only honest use.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (most damaging in medical, crisis and charity writing, and in any scene where a writer needs a character to be kind and reaches for the nearest kind-sounding line)
**Avoid when:** Always, where the piece is speaking for itself. The repair is mechanical: replace the guarantee about the outcome with a commitment you can actually keep, which is almost always a commitment about presence or about a next step. See `emo.adult_reassurance`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "Don't worry. She's going to be fine. I promise you she's going to be fine."
> **Working:** "I don't know if she'll be fine." He sits down on the floor next to the chair. "I'm not going anywhere."

**Yields to:** `emo.adult_reassurance` — always, where the piece speaks for itself. Replace the guarantee about the outcome with a commitment you can keep, which is almost always about presence or a next step.  
`emo.false_reassurance`

## Permission and company

### The Permission Piece

**Also called:** the legitimising work, the non-resolving piece, "you're allowed to feel this"
**What it is:** A work, or a movement inside one, whose entire job is to establish that a feeling the audience already has is a normal shape for a person to be — and which deliberately declines to resolve it.
**Effect on the audience:** A specific relief that is not the feeling lifting. What lifts is the private suspicion of being defective for having it. Readers keep books for this more often than for any other reason, and almost never describe it in those words.
**Used for and where it works best:** Essay, memoir, song, monologue, the kids' book about a hard feeling for readers past the preschool shape, the brand film about a life stage. For preschool and younger, naming a feeling and giving a usable action is instruction, which this form withholds, so `emo.kids_emotion` governs there. Three rules, and the first is the whole craft. **Show someone already feeling it; never instruct the audience to.** "It's okay to be angry at someone who died" is a poster. A character who is angry at someone who died, whom the piece does not punish and does not correct, is permission. **Do not resolve it** — resolution is a claim that the feeling was a problem, so the piece must end with the feeling still present and the person still intact. **Be exact enough to be recognised**: permission is granted by recognition, so the detail must be specific enough that one reader believes you were in their house. A general feeling legitimises nothing.
**Best in:** formats: Essay, Memoir, Monologue, Song Lyric, Short Story, Picture Book | genres: Literary, Social Drama, Coming of Age, Family Drama, Testimony, Slice of Life
**Avoid when:** The commission is reassurance or instruction. Both want a resolution and a next step; permission withholds both, and a piece attempting two of the three does none. Also avoid where the feeling is one the work would be endorsing acting on rather than having — that distinction carries the whole ethical load of the form. See `theme.descriptive_not_prescriptive` and `theme.tacked_on_moral`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "It's okay to not be okay. Your feelings are valid. You don't always have to be the strong one."
> **Working:** "Three weeks after the funeral I started hoping the phone would stop ringing. I wanted all of them to go away so that I could watch television. I have never told anybody that, and I have never met anybody who has lost someone who did not do it."

**Yields to:** `emo.adult_reassurance` — The commission is reassurance or instruction; and `emo.kids_emotion` where the audience is preschool, which needs the feeling named and one child-usable action.  
`emo.permission_piece`

### Naming the Unnamed Feeling

**Also called:** the word for it, the articulated feeling, the line people send to each other
**What it is:** One sentence that accurately describes a common experience the reader has been carrying without vocabulary for it.
**Effect on the audience:** Physical recognition — the reader stops, goes back a line, and sends it to somebody. The mechanism is not novelty but accuracy: they have had the experience and lacked the sentence, and the sentence arriving is experienced as being known.
**Used for and where it works best:** Essay, memoir, stand-up, lyric, the pull-quote of a brand film, the one line of a monologue that gets quoted back. Three rules. **Describe the behaviour, not the emotion** — feelings are located in what people do about them, never in adjectives. **Make it falsifiable**: precise enough that a reader could genuinely say *no, not me*, because a sentence nobody could disagree with is a horoscope and produces nothing. **Do not coin a word for it.** Inventing a compound noun converts recognition into a gimmick and hands the reader a label instead of a mirror; write the sentence and let them do the naming. Pairs with `comedy.observational_truth` and `emo.specific_detail`.
**Best in:** formats: Essay, Memoir, Stand-Up Set, Monologue, Song Lyric, Podcast | genres: Literary, Slice of Life, Comedy, Coming of Age, Social Drama, Testimony
**Avoid when:** The piece needs the feeling to stay unexamined. Naming a feeling ends the scene it is in, because a character who can articulate what they feel has finished changing — so in drama give the line to the wrong person, or place it after the turn rather than before it. See `sub.subtext_announced`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "She felt a profound ambivalence about her own success, a strange mixture of pride and dread."
> **Working:** "She kept the good news to herself for four days. As long as nobody knew, nobody could be disappointed by what she did with it."

`emo.naming_the_feeling`

### The Company of the Same Experience

**Also called:** "this happened to me too", the witness offer, solidarity without advice
**What it is:** A first-person account offered for its resemblance rather than for its lesson — enough ordinary detail that a reader with the same experience recognises theirs, and no attempt to repair it.
**Effect on the audience:** The end of being the only one. It is distinct from sympathy, which places the reader below the writer, and from advice, which places them behind; company places them alongside, and that is the only one of the three that does not sting.
**Used for and where it works best:** Memoir, illness and grief writing, testimony documentary, first-person social video, the patient-story section of a health explainer. Three rules. **Report, do not counsel** — the sentence where the piece turns to the second person ("what you have to remember is") is the sentence where the company ends and the reader is alone again. **Include the unflattering detail**: the resentment, the boredom, the relief. A cleaned-up account resembles nobody's real experience and therefore grants nobody anything. **No arrival** — the account may end with where the writer is now; it may not end with where the reader should get to. Works with `emo.testimony_emotion` and `emo.specific_detail`.
**Best in:** formats: Memoir, Essay, Documentary Interview, Podcast, Reels/Shorts, Short Story | genres: Testimony, Medical, Social Drama, Addiction, Migration, Faith
**Avoid when:** The commission is advocacy with an ask, which needs the second person and a next step. Also when the writer is still inside the experience — the piece then reads as a request for help rather than an offer of company, which is not a moral failing but is a different job, and readers feel the difference immediately.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "Caring for a parent with dementia teaches you patience. It reminds you what really matters, and it changes you for the better."
> **Working:** "By the second year I had a system. Forty minutes with her, then ten minutes sitting in the car. In the car I would look at my phone without reading anything on it. I told my sister the traffic had been bad."

**Yields to:** `rhythm.call_to_action_line` — Advocacy with an ask; second person and next step.  
`emo.this_happened_too`

## The commissioned address: to a group, and to a buyer

### The Commemorative Register

**Also called:** epideictic writing, the ceremonial address, the monument speech, the anniversary piece
**What it is:** Writing addressed to a group about itself on a marked occasion — a dedication, a memorial, a founding anniversary, the closing narration of a heritage film. Its business is values rather than information, and its mode is praise.
**Effect on the audience:** At best, the sense of standing inside a *we* that preceded them and will outlast them. At worst — and this is the default outcome, not an unlucky one — a warm nothing that nobody can quote an hour later.
**Used for and where it works best:** Commissioned heritage and civic work, national-day films, closing narration, the anniversary episode. Four strict rules. **One named person or one dated event carries the whole piece**; the abstractions this form attracts — courage, resilience, sacrifice, unbreakable spirit — are its failure state and not its content. **Say what this group did that no other group did**: a *we* that could belong to anyone belongs to nobody, which is `expo.heritage_specificity`. **Praise a cost, not a quality** — a community will accept being told what it paid and will resent being told what it is like. **Keep it short**, because the register makes no argument and therefore has no engine; it runs on goodwill, and goodwill is finite.
**Best in:** formats: Monologue, Heritage Documentary, Brand Film, Live Event, Voiceover Script, Essay | genres: Heritage, Historical, Faith, Political, Biography, Advocacy
**Avoid when:** The occasion is contested and the group is not in fact one group; praise then reads as a ruling. Also avoid where a family wants a person remembered rather than a value celebrated — a different job with a different shape, belonging to `dial.the_eulogy` and `voice.eulogy_register`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "Today we remember the courage, the sacrifice and the unbreakable spirit of a people who never surrendered."
> **Working:** "In the winter of 1915 this village took in four hundred people it could not feed. The register is still in the church and the names are still in it. Nobody here is descended from the four hundred. We are descended from the ones who wrote the list."

**Yields to:** `dial.the_eulogy` — where a family wants a person remembered rather than a value celebrated. That is a different job with a different shape.  
`emo.commemorative_register`

### The Communal Address That Excludes

**Also called:** the constitutive enemy, us-and-them commemoration, the we defined by its outside
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A communal or commemorative piece that manufactures belonging by naming, or clearly implying, who does not belong. The group is constituted by its outside rather than by anything it did.
**Effect on the audience:** Inside the room it is the most reliable technique in this file, and that is precisely the problem — it works instantly, costs nothing, and is therefore what a writer reaches for when the positive material has not been researched. Outside the room it reads exactly as written: everyone excluded hears the line correctly, and the piece acquires a permanent second audience it never planned for.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere. The diagnostic takes five minutes: delete every sentence that refers to anyone outside the group, and read what is left. If the piece has collapsed, the exclusion was its structure.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (most damaging in heritage, national and faith work, where the commission is usually sincere and the outside gets named because nobody found the specific thing the group itself did)
**Avoid when:** Always. Note that the repair is research rather than tone — softening the line leaves the structure intact. See `emo.commemorative_register`, `theme.propaganda` and `theme.applied_only_to_others`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "They tried to erase us. They failed. We are still here — and they are not."
> **Working:** "We are still here. This is the list of what we kept: the calendar, the alphabet, the bread we make on the third day, and the tune, which we have changed twice."

**Yields to:** `emo.commemorative_register` — Name what the group did, not its outside.  
`emo.we_that_excludes`

### Manufactured Inadequacy

**Also called:** the invented lack, the created insecurity, agitating a problem that was not there, the agitation step
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** Persuasion that installs a deficiency in the audience so that the product can relieve it — the middle step of Problem–Agitate–Solve performed on a problem the audience did not have until the copy proposed it.
**Effect on the audience:** Conversion, in the short run, which is why the technique survives every argument made against it. The cost is paid by the brand and paid late: the claim an audience retains from an advert is never the product claim, it is the claim about their own life, and a brand that told people they were lacking is remembered as having said so.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere — but be precise about what the failure is, because the trade argument is usually run badly. Agitating a **real** problem the audience arrived with is legitimate and often kind; it is the naming of something they could not articulate, and it is what `emo.naming_the_feeling` does in another register. The failure is agitating a **manufactured** one. The whole test is a single question asked before a line is written: *would this person have described this as a problem yesterday?* If the answer is no, the copy is not solving anything, it is opening something.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (endemic in beauty, wellness, supplement and financial advertising, and in the performance-marketing hook that opens on an insecurity because insecurity measurably holds the swipe)
**Avoid when:** Always. Note that the alternative is not softness but a different claim shape: assert a competence the product serves rather than a lack it fills. See `anta.problem_as_enemy`, `theme.implicit_ad_claim` and `emo.manipulated_beat`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "Nobody tells you that your hands are the first thing people notice. By thirty-five, they're already giving you away."
> **Working:** "You use your hands about nine hundred times a day. This is for the twenty seconds afterwards."

**Yields to:** `emo.naming_the_feeling` — The problem is real and the audience arrived with it.  
`emo.manufactured_inadequacy`

## The three Part 2 named as cut

### Tenderness

**Also called:** the tender register, attentive care, concern for the fragile
**What it is:** Attention paid to something *because* it is fragile and will not last. It is not love: love is directed at a person and wants something back, and tenderness is directed at a condition and wants nothing.
**Effect on the audience:** It lowers a reader's guard faster than anything else in this category, because it makes no demand of them. Tokarczuk's definition is the usable one — concern for another being's fragility, its uniqueness, and its lack of immunity to suffering and to time.
**Used for and where it works best:** Caretaking scenes; writing about the old, the ill, the very young, animals and objects; the last movement of a piece that has been hard; and as a narrator's standing stance rather than a scene's content, which is the strongest version. Four rules. **Tenderness is shown by attention, never by touch** — the tender line is the one that notices something nobody would have bothered to notice. **It points downward in power**; tenderness directed at someone stronger than the viewpoint is admiration, which is a different thing. **No adjective may do the work** — this is the register most easily tipped into `emo.sentimentality`, and `emo.specific_detail` is the only safeguard. **It cannot be announced**: a narrator who describes themselves as tender has stopped being it.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Picture Book, Memoir, Feature Film, Animation Feature | genres: Literary, Family Drama, Slice of Life, Heritage, Nature, Coming of Age
**Avoid when:** The two characters are equals in a live fight. Tenderness inside that scene reads as condescension, and the character receiving it will say so, correctly. Also avoid as a default narratorial stance in satire and noir, where it disarms the entire instrument.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "He looked at his father with a tender, overwhelming love, struck by how terribly frail the old man had become."
> **Working:** "His father's shirt was done up wrong. He talked about the football for a while. Then he said hang on, and did the buttons again from the bottom, which is the only way they go right."

**Yields to:** `emo.love_without_declaration` — Care directed at a person, wanting something back.  
`emo.tenderness`

### Writing Emotion for a Child Performer

**Also called:** the seven-year-old's line, writing to a young cast, the child-actor constraint
**What it is:** Writing an emotional beat inside what a child can actually deliver on a shooting day — which is an action and an intention, and never a state.
**Effect on the audience:** On the page the scene looks like less. On screen it is the difference between a real child and a stage-school child, which an audience detects in under a second and does not forgive for the rest of the film.
**Used for and where it works best:** Kids' drama, family film, animation with a child voice cast, advertising with children, and any scene at all with a child in it. Five practical rules. **Children play actions, not emotions** — write "she puts her shoes by the door and keeps checking they are still there", not "she is anxious"; a director will convert your emotional note into a physical one anyway, so write it converted. **The line must be sayable by a child who is not acting**: a seven-year-old does not say *I feel left out*, she says *can I play* twice and then stops asking. **Short lines, no subordinate clauses** — delivery and retention both fall apart past roughly a dozen words. **Put the emotional weight on the adult opposite**, who can carry it, which is `emo.someone_else_feels_it` used as a production constraint. **Never write crying as a requirement**; write the moment before crying, which a child can reach and which is the better beat in any case.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Animation Feature, Kids Story, Short Film, Advertising, One-Hour Drama | genres: Family Drama, Kids, Coming of Age, Drama, Comedy, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The part is written for a specific exceptional child already cast — then cast first, write second, and ignore the ceiling. Note also that this constrains the *line* and not the *scene*: the scene may carry any weight you like, provided the child in it has something to do. Pairs with `char.age_specific_logic` and `pov.child_pov`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:**
> SARA: I know you and Mum are getting divorced. I've known for ages. I just wish somebody had asked me how I felt about it.
> **Working:**
> SARA: Are you coming Saturday?
> DAD: I don't think so, love.
> SARA: Okay. (a beat) Are you coming the Saturday after?

`emo.child_performer_emotion`

### The Procedural Emotional Reset

**Also called:** the weekly reset, the episodic baseline, the returning lead's clean slate
**What it is:** The mechanism that lets a returning lead be genuinely moved by this week's case and still open next week at the same emotional baseline, without the restoration reading as amnesia.
**Effect on the audience:** It buys the thing episodic formats are sold on — enter anywhere, and the lead still feels something real. Done badly it produces the particular coldness of a series in which nothing appears to matter, which audiences describe as thin writing when it is actually a reset nobody paid for.
**Used for and where it works best:** Procedurals, preschool and kids' series, any format that must survive being watched out of order. Four rules. **The case changes, the lead does not** — the person transformed by the episode is the guest, and the lead's feeling is real but is *about* them. **The reset is paid for on screen**: end on the small private act that closes it — the file into the drawer, the drive home, the one line to the partner. A feeling simply absent next week reads as a continuity error; a feeling visibly put away reads as a person with a job. **Keep one narrow permanent channel**, a single running relationship or habit that does accumulate, so the audience reads the reset as a choice rather than a limitation. **Reset the feeling, never the consequence**: facts that happened stay happened. See `anta.recurring_vs_episodic`, `char.protected_static_role`, `info.between_episode_memory` and `expo.re_entry_exposition`.
**Best in:** formats: Procedural, Series, Animated Series, Preschool, Half-Hour Comedy, Soap | genres: Crime, Medical, Procedural, Kids, Preschool, Workplace
**Avoid when:** The show is serialised and its promise is accumulation — a reset there is the commonest single audience complaint, and no amount of craft makes it land. Avoid entirely in limited series, where every feeling is meant to be carried to the end.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** Episode 11 ends with the detective crying in the station car park. Episode 12 opens with her making a joke about the coffee machine. Nothing sits between the two.
> **Working:** Episode 11 ends with her in the car park. She does not start the car. She takes the photograph of the boy out of the file, looks at it, and puts it in the glovebox. Episode 12 opens with the joke about the coffee machine. The glovebox is not mentioned again for two seasons, and then it is.

**Yields to:** `emo.series_arc` — Serialised show whose promise is accumulation.

## Coverage

Everything the brief and Part 2 named is written. The three topics Part 2 recorded as cut for space are now cards: `emo.tenderness`, `emo.child_performer_emotion` and `emo.procedural_reset`. The gaps `intents.md` reported are closed — adult reassurance (`emo.adult_reassurance`, with `emo.bad_news_delivery` and the failure `emo.false_reassurance` beside it), the Permission intent (`emo.permission_piece`, plus `emo.naming_the_feeling` and `emo.this_happened_too`, which are the two mechanisms every Permission route was previously borrowing from other categories), the civic and commemorative register (`emo.commemorative_register` and its named failure `emo.we_that_excludes`), and the Purchase Intent failure that entry names but could not cite (`emo.manufactured_inadequacy`).

**What the research showed that the brief did not anticipate.** Three things.

First, **adult reassurance and bad-news delivery are not one card, and they are not two halves of one technique** — they are opposed in their first move. Reassurance narrows uncertainty by naming what is known. Bad-news delivery widens it deliberately, with the warning shot, before narrowing to a single sentence. Written as one card the advice contradicts itself, which is probably part of why the database had neither.

Second, **false reassurance earns a failure card rather than a paragraph inside the reassurance card.** The grief-support literature is unusually unanimous on the mechanism, and the mechanism is counter-intuitive enough to need naming: writers keep using it because it *appears* to work in the scene, the recipient going quiet. An "avoid this" note inside another card would not have carried that reason, and the reason is the whole card.

All 12 cards are durable; none carries a review date.

Named failures in this file: `emo.false_reassurance`, `emo.we_that_excludes`, `emo.manufactured_inadequacy`.
