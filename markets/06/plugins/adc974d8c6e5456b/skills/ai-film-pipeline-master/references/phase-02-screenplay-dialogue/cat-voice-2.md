## The Essayistic Voice

### Are You Writing the Voice or Casting It

**Also called:** the voice selector, write or cast, the two-decision fork on `voice.`
**What it is:** The card at the head of the `voice.` shelf, which lives in three files across two phases. It does not describe a voice. It asks **one fork and then three questions on whichever side of the fork you landed**, and hands back a named shortlist for each answer. The fork is *are you deciding what register this is written in, or who performs it and how*, because `SHELVES.md` §2b says these are two decisions and the shelf's own pair graph says it harder than `SHELVES.md` §2b does: **not one `Yields to:` edge on this shelf crosses between the halves.** The axes were not invented. On the writing half they were read off every condition the cards carry in their `Yields to:` and `Avoid when:` fields, and the conditions that repeated became the questions. On the casting half they were read off the `Example:` profile line, where the three questions are already written as numbers. Every card cited here is cited by id.
**Effect on the audience:** None. This card never reaches the screen. It exists so that the voice that does reach the screen was chosen against the page, the listener and the thing on the other side of the claim, rather than picked because it was the register the writer had been reading that month.
**Used for and where it works best:** **Settle the fork out loud before opening any file.** The test is mechanical: *does this decision survive if nobody ever performs the piece aloud?* If it does, it is a writing decision and lives in Phase 2; if the decision only exists because someone has to say the words, it is a casting decision and lives in Phase 4. Then answer that half's three questions in one sentence each. On the writing half the first answer eliminates roughly half the file and the second cuts what is left by a third; on the casting half the three answers together normally leave one or two profiles, because that half is small and its axes are near-orthogonal. **Where a piece is both — a heritage VO you are writing and casting — they are two decisions and you make them in order**, writing first, casting second, never at once. A register chosen at casting is a register nobody wrote.
**Best in:** formats: all | genres: all
**Avoid when:** **You cannot answer the fork honestly**, because the commonest error on this shelf is settling a register by picking a performer — you hear the take, you like it, and the piece acquires a voice no draft ever argued for. A wrong fork cannot be repaired by re-answering questions two and three; the map under it is laid out wrong. Also **distrust the writing half's third axis whenever your honest answer is *a reader on a page*: forty-four of the sixty-eight live there**, so on ordinary prose this map has two questions left and is doing two-thirds of the work it does on a voiced or translated piece. And **this card stops at the boundary of the second question's fourth value.** Where the thing on the other side is a real person, a real death, a real faith or a real record, the shelf can tell you which register is available and it cannot tell you whether you are entitled to it — that is settled reference-first, against sources, and no selector settles it. Finally, do not bring this to a draft whose voice already exists and is failing: that is a diagnosis, it is the writing half's fourth value, and `voice.voice_vs_style` is the door.
**Prompt:** `[Selector — produces no prompt. It returns card ids. The chosen card carries the prompt.]`

**The default, and the one against it**

**The default on the writing half is `voice.narrating_persona` settled first, then `voice.free_indirect_style` as the engine.** The file argues for it in its own first card — every sentence afterwards inherits the persona's four answers — and `voice.free_indirect_style` calls itself the correct default for most contemporary commercial and literary fiction. The graph half agrees: it is one of the two most-yielded-to cards on the half, at four inbound, ahead of `voice.understating_voice` at three.

**But it only half agrees, and the other half is the finding.** The card tied with it at four inbound is **`voice.suppressing_your_voice`**. Asked where to fall back to, this half answers *close the distance to the character* exactly as often as it answers *take the writer out of the prose altogether* — and the four conditions that route there are a reference doc, an instruction, a shooting script and a heritage or memorial piece. **Most of the work Yokhanna actually ships lands on the second answer, not the first.** Use the default; do not mistake it for the thing the shelf votes for.

**The one against it on the writing half is `voice.voice_of_a_place`.** Nothing routes to it. Every other card on this half assumes a human being is speaking and then argues about how close that human stands; this one deletes the human and keeps the voice, assigning the narration to a city, a river, a house or a coat. Its limits then come from physics rather than from psychology — a house cannot follow anyone outdoors, a river knows only what floats — which is a constraint no persona card can give you, because a person can always be somewhere else. Its own `Yields to:` admits the cost in one line, *"The story needs interior psychology from a character"*, handing over to `voice.free_indirect_style`, which is the shape of a card that knows what it is for. On heritage work, where the subject outlives every possible narrator, it is the obvious answer and the shelf never offers it.

**The default on the casting half is `voice.cast_authoritative_historian`** — the museum read, and the only profile on the half that nothing yields to and that yields only to the hook narrator. It is also the most worn voice in the medium, and its own `Effect on the audience` line is a warning dressed as a boast: *"the audience assumes every statement has been verified"*. That assumption is the reason the default is dangerous rather than the reason it is good.

**And the casting graph falls back somewhere else entirely.** The two most-yielded-to profiles are `voice.cast_intimate_memoir` and `voice.cast_corporate_visionary`, four inbound each — the two ends of C1. **Asked fifteen times where to go when a voice is wrong, this half answers *closer* or *louder* and never once answers *warmer*, *colder* or *less composed*.** The whole graph is one axis.

**The one against it on the casting half is `voice.cast_folkloric_elder`.** Nothing routes to it, and it is the only profile here whose instability is the credential rather than the fault — low steadiness, a weathered rasp, deliberate pauses. Every other heritage-capable option on the half buys authority by projecting it; this one offers memory instead, and memory is allowed to be uncertain in a way an authority is not. Its near cousin further against the grain is `voice.cast_frenzied_eyewitness`, lower still, the only voice on the shelf that is not in control of itself.

### The Essayistic Voice

**Also called:** the thinking-aloud register, discursive narration, the Montaigne voice
**What it is:** Prose that performs the act of thinking rather than reporting a finished thought — the voice arrives at its position on the page, in front of the reader, and lets them watch the arriving.
**Effect on the audience:** They are recruited as a companion rather than an audience. A conclusion they watched being reached is far harder to argue with than one they were handed.
**Used for and where it works best:** Personal essay, video essay, documentary narration, long-form journalism, and the digressive chapter inside a novel. The craft rule: the thought must be genuinely in motion, which means the voice has to enter the piece not yet knowing something and leave knowing it. Write the last paragraph, then delete every earlier sentence that already knew it. What remains is the essay. A voice that had the answer on line one is a lecture in essay costume, and readers can hear the difference within a paragraph.
**Best in:** formats: Essay, Memoir, Documentary, Voiceover Script, Podcast, Novel | genres: Literary, Journalism, Heritage, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The reader came for an instruction or a fact. Thinking aloud in a manual, a brief or a safety notice is a tax on someone who needs one sentence. It also fails in short form — under about ninety seconds there is no room for a mind to change, so the register reads as throat-clearing.
**Example:**
> I wanted to say the house was the last thing my grandfather owned. It is a good line and I have used it before. But he also owned the shop, and the shop is what he talked about, and I think I have preferred the house because a house is easier to be sad about than a business. That is the sort of edit grief makes without asking.

**Yields to:** `voice.suppressing_your_voice` — The reader came for an instruction or fact.  
`voice.essayistic_voice`

### The Argumentative Voice

**Also called:** the case-making register, the arguing essay, forensic prose
**What it is:** A voice organised around a claim it intends to win — it states a position, anticipates the objection, concedes what is true in it, and comes back harder.
**Effect on the audience:** They brace, then relax. A voice that names the strongest counter-argument out loud buys more credibility in one sentence than three paragraphs of evidence.
**Used for and where it works best:** Op-ed, criticism, polemic that wants to persuade rather than rally, explainer video, courtroom summation, and any heritage piece correcting a popular error. The craft rule: concede something that costs you. A fake concession — "of course the situation is complex" — is recognised instantly as a rhetorical move. A real one names a fact that genuinely damages your case and then explains why you hold the position anyway. Pair it with `theme.controlling_idea`: the argument is the theme with its sleeves rolled up.
**Best in:** formats: Essay, Explainer, Documentary, Podcast, Voiceover Script | genres: Journalism, Social Drama, Heritage, Historical, Satire
**Avoid when:** The piece is meant to move rather than convince. Argument and grief do not share a register; a eulogy that argues is a defence, and everyone in the room will hear it as one. Also avoid where the audience already agrees — argument aimed at the converted curdles into the tone of `voice.polemic_register` without the honesty of choosing it.
**Example:**
> The strongest version of the other case is this: the wall was rebuilt in 1872, and almost everything a visitor photographs today is Ottoman brick. That is true, and the guidebooks should say so. It does not make the site modern. It makes the site a place that people kept deciding was worth rebuilding for fifteen hundred years, which is a harder and better thing to have proved.

**Yields to:** `voice.polemic_register` — Audience already agrees; choose the rallying register honestly.  
`voice.argumentative_voice`

---

### The Controlled Digression

**Also called:** the productive detour, associative structure, the ramble that isn't
**What it is:** A deliberate departure from the subject that returns carrying something the main line could not have got on its own — and which is placed, not stumbled into.
**Effect on the audience:** Relief from the argument's pressure, then a jolt when the detour turns out to have been the argument. It makes a piece feel generous rather than efficient.
**Used for and where it works best:** Personal essay, memoir, video essay, documentary VO, and the chapter-length aside in literary fiction. The craft rule: a digression is licensed by its return, not by its charm. Before you keep one, write the sentence that brings it back and check that the sentence changes the meaning of what came before it. If the return is only a transition — "anyway, back to the house" — the digression was self-indulgence and the reader already knew. Second rule: one digression per movement, and never two deep. A digression inside a digression is where essays go to die.
**Best in:** formats: Essay, Memoir, Podcast, Documentary, Novel, Voiceover Script | genres: Literary, Journalism, Heritage, Comedy
**Avoid when:** Anything on a clock — a two-minute explainer, a thirty-second ad, a broadcast package. And avoid it in a first draft you have not yet structured, because the digressive voice is the easiest register in which to mistake fluency for progress.
**Example:**
> My father mended the fence every spring. This is also, I have to say, the only sport my family has ever followed: watching a man fail to defeat a hedge over four decades. He lost. The hedge is still there and he is not, and I understand now that the mending was never about the fence, which is a stupid thing to have taken fifty years to notice about a fence.

`voice.controlled_digression`

## Direct Address and Second Person

### Addressing the Reader

**Also called:** dear reader, the broken fourth wall in prose, apostrophe to the audience
**What it is:** The narrator turning from the story to speak to the person reading it — acknowledging that there is a book, a page, a telling, and someone on the other end of it.
**Effect on the audience:** A small electric shock. The illusion is interrupted and, done well, replaced by something better: the sense of a living teller who has been watching them read.
**Used for and where it works best:** Comic novels, Victorian pastiche, picture books, narrated comedy, and the last page of a first-person novel. The craft rule: the address must cost the narrator something or tell the reader something they cannot get inside the fiction. "Dear reader, you will not believe what happened next" is free and therefore worthless. "I am going to tell you this part badly, and I want you to know I know" is not. Use it three times in a book at most, and put one of them in the first two pages so the reader knows the device exists before it arrives at a moment that matters. The screen equivalent is `pov.licensed_break`.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Picture Book, Comics, Stage Play, Podcast | genres: Comedy, Literary, Satire, Kids, Historical
**Avoid when:** Immersive genre work where the reader's continuous dream is the whole product — thriller, horror, most commercial romance. Breaking it once costs you twenty pages of rebuilt tension for a single wink. Also avoid it as a rescue: writers reach for direct address when a scene is not working, and the reader can feel the hand come through the page.
**Example:**
> "I have put the trial early in this account, and you should know it happened last. I did that because I could not bear to make you wait the way we waited, and because if you had liked him first you would not have forgiven me."

**Yields to:** `pov.licensed_break` — The same break, on screen rather than page.  
`voice.addressing_the_reader`

### Second Person as a Voice Choice

**Also called:** the you-narrative, direct-address narration, the conscripted reader
**What it is:** Narration in "you", where the pronoun is doing one of three different jobs: it is the reader being conscripted, or a narrator addressing an absent person, or a first-person narrator holding themselves at arm's length.
**Effect on the audience:** Depends entirely on which job. Conscription creates unease and complicity; address to an absent person creates intimacy and loss; self-distancing reads as dissociation and is the most reliable of the three.
**Used for and where it works best:** Trauma narration, addiction and dissociation stories, second-person lyric essay, and any piece where a character cannot say "I" about what they did. The craft rule: decide which of the three "you" is, write it on the persona card, and never let it drift between them — a "you" that is sometimes the reader and sometimes the protagonist is not ambiguity, it is a fault. Hold it under about six thousand words in prose; readers report fatigue reliably past that. The POV machinery is `pov.second_person`.
**Best in:** formats: Short Story, Essay, Poetry, Game Narrative, Novel, Reels/Shorts | genres: Literary, Social Drama, Noir, Crime
**Avoid when:** The story needs the reader to judge the protagonist. You cannot condemn someone you are being made to act as. Also avoid in advertising, where "you" is the house register and carries no charge at all — it is the sound the reader has learned to skip.
**Example:**
> Self-distancing: "You put the keys on the table. You do not look at the hall. You have been in this house nine years and you have never once, until tonight, wondered how long it takes to pack."

**Yields to:** `voice.psychic_distance_ladder` — The reader must be able to judge the protagonist.  
`voice.second_person_voice`

### The Implied Listener

**Also called:** the addressee, who is being told this, the second chair
**What it is:** Building into the voice the specific person it is being said to — their knowledge, their patience, their relationship to the speaker — even when that person never appears.
**Effect on the audience:** It fixes everything else. The listener decides what gets explained, what gets skipped, what gets defended before it is attacked, and how much the voice is allowed to show off.
**Used for and where it works best:** Every first-person piece, every VO, every podcast, and the fastest available fix for narration that feels unanchored. The craft rule: name the listener concretely — not "the audience" but "my daughter, at thirty, after I am dead" or "the officer taking this statement" or "the one person from that town who will know I am lying". Then cut every sentence that listener would not need. This is the single highest-yield revision pass in first-person work, and it is almost never done. It is the missing half of `voice.narrating_persona`.
**Best in:** formats: Memoir, Novel, Podcast, Voiceover Script, Audio Drama, Essay | genres: Memoir, Literary, Crime, Heritage, Journalism
**Avoid when:** Nothing, really — but do not let the listener become a character in a piece that has no room for one. If you find yourself writing the listener's reactions, you have started a dialogue and should either commit to it or delete it.
**Example:**
> Listener unnamed: "The war changed the town. Many families left during those years."
> Listener = a nephew born abroad: "You have seen the square in photographs, so you think it was always that size. It was twice that. I am telling you this because nobody else who remembers it is going to phone you."

`voice.implied_listener`

### The Confessional to Camera

**Also called:** talking head, the interview register, the piece to camera, the confession booth
**What it is:** A voice that is being recorded and knows it — speaking to one lens, performing candour while managing exactly how much it gives, with the awareness of an audience baked into every sentence.
**Effect on the audience:** Double vision. They hear the confession and they watch the performance of confessing, and the gap between the two is where all the interest lives.
**Used for and where it works best:** Mockumentary, documentary interview, reality-format scripting, creator content, and any drama that uses a recorded-testimony frame. The craft rule: write the self-editing. Real people to camera start a sentence, decide against it, and restart in a safer register — and the abandoned sentence is the information. Also decide who the speaker thinks is watching, because a witness statement, a video diary and a piece to camera for eleven million strangers are three different voices with three different amounts of shame in them. Pair with `sub.dual_audience`.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary, Reels/Shorts, One-Hour Drama, Feature Film, Podcast, Brand Film | genres: Comedy, Satire, Social Drama, Crime, Journalism
**Avoid when:** A scene would carry it. Confessional-to-camera is the laziest available exposition device and a whole film of it becomes radio with faces. And avoid it in period work before the camera existed unless you have designed an in-world reason for a recorded voice.
**Example:**
> "We're a family business. I'd say — no. I'd say we're a business, and we're also, some of us, related. My sister does the books. My sister has done the books since 2009 and I'd like that on the record before anyone asks me anything else."

**Yields to:** `voice.the_letter_voice` — Period work before the camera existed.  
`voice.confessional_to_camera`

### The Letter

**Also called:** epistolary voice, written-to-one-person register, the unsent letter
**What it is:** A voice shaped by the fact that it is being written, by hand or by keyboard, to a named recipient who will read it later and alone.
**Effect on the audience:** A letter is believed. The reader is looking at something that was never meant for them, and the register's small formalities — the salutation, the apology for not writing sooner, the sign-off — do more characterisation than a page of description.
**Used for and where it works best:** Historical fiction, migration and diaspora stories, romance, horror, and the single chapter that has to carry a jump in time. The craft rule: the letter's voice is set by what the writer is *not* saying to that specific recipient, and every letter has at least one sentence written to be re-read. Get the furniture period-correct — a 1930s letter does not open the way a 1990s one does — and choose deliberately between the letter that was sent and the letter that was not, because an unsent letter is a different genre: it is a confession with a stamp it never got. The diary is its sibling and the opposite: no recipient, so no performance, so different lies. See `voice.found_document` for presenting it as an object.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Stage Play, Audio Drama, Podcast, Documentary | genres: Historical, Heritage, Literary, War, Crime, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The information would be more alive in a scene, or the letter is being used to summarise plot the reader missed. Readers skim inserts and skim harder each time. Also be careful with long stretches of letters in screen work — a voiced letter is a monologue over pictures, and it stops a film dead.
**Example:**
> "Dear Hanna — I am writing on the good paper because I want you to keep this one. I have started it four times. We are well. Butrus has work. There is no news from the village and I would like you to stop asking Marta for news from the village, because she tells you what she thinks you can hold, and then I have to carry the rest of it by myself. I am well. Write to me about the children."

**Source:** assumption — the package's illustrative pair of decades, not a published standard; check the letter's conventions against real letters of its period.  
**Yields to:** `voice.found_document` — Screen work, where a voiced letter stops the film.  
`voice.the_letter_voice`

## Register Families

### The Polemic

**Also called:** the manifesto register, rallying prose, the fighting voice
**What it is:** A register that has abandoned the pretence of weighing — short declaratives, no hedges, moral vocabulary, and the assumption that the reader is already on the right side or should be ashamed not to be.
**Effect on the audience:** Energising for the aligned, alienating for everyone else. Polemic converts existing agreement into action; it almost never converts disagreement into agreement.
**Used for and where it works best:** Manifestos, protest writing, satire with a target, character monologue for a true believer, and the third-act speech in a political drama. **What it promises:** clarity and courage. **What it costs:** your claim to fairness, permanently — a reader who catches one unfair move in a polemic discounts the whole piece and the next one too. The craft rule: polemic survives only on verifiable specifics. One checkable fact per paragraph buys the right to the next sentence of heat. Heat without facts is just volume, and the modern reader has been shouted at professionally for twenty years.
**Best in:** formats: Essay, Stage Play, Voiceover Script, Reels/Shorts, Podcast | genres: Satire, Social Drama, Journalism, War, Historical
**Avoid when:** You want to be trusted about a disputed fact, or you are writing a character you want the audience to like — sustained polemic reads as a person who has stopped listening, which is accurate characterisation and rarely sympathetic. The subtler tool is `voice.argumentative_voice`.
**Example:**
> "They call it a consultation. Four meetings, two of them in the afternoon, all of them in English, none of them minuted. A consultation is a thing you can lose. This was an announcement with chairs."

**Yields to:** `voice.argumentative_voice` — You need trust about a disputed fact.  
`voice.polemic_register`

---

### The Eulogy

**Also called:** the funeral register, praise of the dead, the tribute voice
**What it is:** A register organised around one job: making a specific dead person present in a room full of people who each knew a different version of them.
**Effect on the audience:** Communal grief, if it is specific; embarrassment, if it is general. There is no third outcome.
**Used for and where it works best:** Funerals, memorial episodes, obituaries, heritage films about a lost place, and the scene where a character must speak about someone they did not love simply. **What it promises:** the dead person, briefly returned. **What it costs:** you cannot be clever. Any sentence that draws attention to the writing is heard as the speaker enjoying the occasion. The craft rule: one small physical habit, one thing they said that was slightly wrong, one thing they did for someone in the room. Abstractions about legacy and love are what the register defaults to and are precisely what nobody remembers. The dramatic version, with the speaker's own agenda underneath, is `dial.the_eulogy`.
**Best in:** formats: Stage Play, One-Hour Drama, Feature Film, Documentary, Essay, Voiceover Script | genres: Drama, Heritage, Social Drama, Literary, War
**Avoid when:** The death is recent and the audience is raw and the piece is fiction. A eulogy register applied to a character the audience has not been given time to lose reads as the writer harvesting an emotion, which is `emo.sentimentality` with a black tie on.
**Example:**
> "He could not pronounce 'espresso'. Forty years in this country and he said it with an X, and he said it loudly, and he said it in a way that made the girl behind the counter write it down wrong on purpose because she liked him. That cup is still on their board. I have seen it."

**Yields to:** `dial.the_eulogy` — The speech carries the speaker's own agenda underneath.  
`voice.eulogy_register`

### The Legal Register

**Also called:** legalese, the drafting voice, statutory prose, contract register
**What it is:** A register built for adversarial reading — defined terms, exhaustive enumeration, no pronoun that could have two antecedents, and a deliberate refusal of rhythm in favour of precision.
**Effect on the audience:** Distrust and fatigue, which is the register working. It signals that every word will be argued over by someone who is paid to argue.
**Used for and where it works best:** Contracts and notices quoted in fiction, courtroom drama, dystopia, the will that opens an inheritance plot, and any scene where a document out-ranks a person. **What it promises:** that nothing has been left to interpretation. **What it costs:** the reader — comprehension falls off a cliff, and the register's real social function is often to make refusal unappealable. The craft rule: it lands dramatically only in contrast and only in short bursts. Quote nine words of it against one plain human sentence. And get the actual furniture right, because a lawyer in the audience will spot an invented clause number before they spot anything else. Distinguish it from `voice.bureaucratic_register`: legal drafting anticipates a fight, bureaucratic prose anticipates a filing.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Stage Play, Documentary | genres: Crime, Social Drama, Satire, Historical, Noir
**Avoid when:** You are the party that wants to be understood, and in any instructional or public-safety writing, where the register actively causes harm. Never use it to make a fictional institution sound serious; it makes it sound unreadable, which is a different note.
**Example:**
> "'Occupier' means any person in actual occupation on the relevant date, excluding for the avoidance of doubt any person whose occupation arises solely by reason of familial relationship to the tenant."
> Her mother had lived there since 1961.

**Yields to:** `rhythm.legal_sentence` — You want to be understood; instructional or public-safety.  
`voice.legal_register`

### The Commentary Register

**Also called:** sports commentary, live-call register, play-by-play
**What it is:** Present-tense narration of something happening now, to people who can see it or are imagining it in real time — short subject-verb bursts, names as complete sentences, and a rising line that has to be able to break into shouting without warning.
**Effect on the audience:** Urgency and co-presence. It is the only register in common use that can make an audience's heart rate rise purely through syntax.
**Used for and where it works best:** Sports sequences, heist and chase scenes, live-event drama, and comic writing that applies the call to something small. **What it promises:** that you are not missing it. **What it costs:** interiority — nobody thinks in commentary, so the moment you need a feeling you must leave the register entirely. The craft rule: the call is nouns and verbs in the order the eye takes them, and the biggest moment gets the *fewest* words, not the most. A commentator who is already shouting has nowhere to go. The comic inversion — commentary applied to a domestic trivial event — is `voice.mock_heroic_apparatus`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Reels/Shorts, Audio Drama, Podcast, Voiceover Script, Comics | genres: Drama, Comedy, Social Drama, Heritage
**Avoid when:** Sustained past about ninety seconds without a break to a normal register — the ear saturates and stops hearing peaks. And avoid it in anything that will be subtitled tightly; the register's speed is its meaning and subtitles delete the speed.
**Example:**
> "Ali. Ali still. Ali turns — he's turned him inside out — and the ball is square and there is nobody, there is *nobody* back."

**Yields to:** `voice.mock_heroic_apparatus` — The call applied to something domestic and small.  
`voice.commentary_register`

### The Liturgical Register

**Also called:** the shared-text voice, call and response, the congregational register
**What it is:** A voice speaking words the audience already knows, in a form that expects them to answer — fixed phrasing, a response line, and the assumption that the text predates everyone in the room.
**Effect on the audience:** Belonging or exclusion, with nothing in between. Those who know the response are inside; those who do not are watching a door close.
**Used for and where it works best:** Faith scenes, funerals and weddings, heritage documentary, oath and induction scenes, and any story about a community with a membership test. **What it promises:** continuity — the sense that this has been said before by the dead and will be said after by the unborn. **What it costs:** individuality; nobody in a liturgical register is speaking as themselves, which is exactly the tool when a character wants to hide inside a form. The craft rule: the drama is in the one person not saying it, or saying it a half-beat late, or saying the old words when the room has moved to the new ones. Never write the whole liturgy — write four lines of it and one person's face. Distinguish from `voice.incantatory_register`, which is the *sound* of ritual used in narration; this is the *social contract* of a text a group holds in common.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Stage Play, Documentary, Novel, Audio Drama, Voiceover Script | genres: Heritage, Historical, Drama, War, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** You do not have the actual text or a defensible reconstruction of it. Invented liturgy for a real living tradition is the single fastest way to lose a heritage audience, and they will correct you publicly — see `info.heritage_audience_correction`. In invented worlds, keep it short: readers skip made-up prayers.
**Example:**
> "The priest said the words for the departed and the room said them back, and my uncle, who had not been inside a church since his brother's wedding, said them back too, in the old pronunciation, which nobody under sixty uses any more, and three people turned around."

**Yields to:** `voice.incantatory_register` — No real text, or an invented world.  
`voice.liturgical_register`

### The Bureaucratic Register

**Also called:** form language, administrative prose, the reference-number voice
**What it is:** The register of an organisation addressing one person as a case — a reference number, a defined process, a decision reported as though it occurred by itself, and a deadline with no author.
**Effect on the audience:** Helplessness. There is no one in the sentence to appeal to, and the reader recognises that immediately even if they cannot say why.
**Used for and where it works best:** Immigration, benefits, medical and housing stories; dystopia; satire; and the letter that turns a plot. **What it promises:** fairness through procedure. **What it costs:** every human fact about the case, which is precisely the point and precisely the drama. The craft rule: the horror is in the mismatch of scale — nine words of process against one enormous consequence — so put the reference number in the same paragraph as the child. Get the real furniture: a case number format, a form code, a "please quote this in all correspondence". Where `voice.institutional_voice` is an organisation describing itself, this is an organisation issuing an instruction; the second is crueller because it has a named victim.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Documentary, Novel, Stage Play | genres: Social Drama, Satire, Crime, War, Historical
**Avoid when:** You are writing for the organisation and want compliance. People do not follow instructions they cannot parse, and public-service writing in this register is a failure with a budget behind it. Also avoid more than a short paragraph of it at a time in fiction.
**Example:**
> "Your application (ref. HO/4412887/B) has been refused. You have 14 days from the date of this letter in which to leave the United Kingdom. Where a period of 14 days is specified, this is calculated from the date of posting."
> The date of posting was nine days earlier.

**Yields to:** `rhythm.instruction_step` — Writing for the organisation and wanting compliance.  
`voice.bureaucratic_register`

### The Advertising Register

**Also called:** the sales voice, copy register, the promise voice
**What it is:** A register engineered to move someone to an action: second person, present tense, benefit before feature, short lines, and a promise carried by rhythm rather than by evidence.
**Effect on the audience:** Instant recognition, which is now its central problem. Modern audiences identify the sales register within about four words and deploy a learned skepticism against everything that follows.
**Used for and where it works best:** Advertising, brand film, product explainer, and — far more interestingly — fiction, where a character speaking in this register is characterising themselves as someone selling. **What it promises:** change, quickly, for money. **What it costs:** credibility on anything not directly purchasable; the moment a documentary or a charity film drifts into it, the audience reclassifies the whole piece as a pitch. The craft rule in the real thing is that specificity outperforms enthusiasm — one concrete number, one real objection answered, one thing the product does not do. The craft rule in fiction is that nobody should ever be *only* selling; the pitch that also confesses is `dial.the_pitch`.
**Best in:** formats: Advertising, Brand Film, Reels/Shorts, Explainer, Stage Play, Novel | genres: Comedy, Satire, Social Drama, Noir, Crime
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-09. The audience's tolerance for the sales register, and the specific phrasings that trip it, move with platform conventions; the underlying mechanism does not.
**Avoid when:** Heritage, memorial, health and public-service work. The register's promise-shape is structurally incompatible with subjects where the honest answer is "this may not help", and audiences punish it hard there.
**Example:**
> Selling: "Three weeks. That's how long it took. And no, it won't fix your posture — go and see someone about your posture."
> The same register as characterisation: "He talked about his father's funeral the way he talked about the flat. Great location. Very emotional day. Really, really strong turnout."

**Yields to:** `voice.suppressing_your_voice` — Heritage, memorial, health and public-service work.  
`voice.advertising_register`

## Comic Registers

### The Deadpan Contract

**Also called:** the straight face, dry delivery, no-wink comedy
**What it is:** A voice that refuses, for the whole length of the piece, to acknowledge that anything unusual is occurring — reporting absurdity in precisely the register it would use for a weather forecast.
**Effect on the audience:** The laugh is entirely theirs, which makes it bigger and makes them loyal. They also start reading forward for the next absurdity, which is free attention.
**Used for and where it works best:** Comic prose, mockumentary, dry VO, workplace comedy, and any joke whose content is dark enough that acknowledging it would be crass. The craft rule: deadpan is a contract, not a move — once the voice signals, even once, that it knows, every earlier absurdity is retroactively re-read as a joke being told rather than a fact being reported, and the piece loses its best quality. Precision is the second rule: a wordy deadpan sentence is a dead one. The sentence-level version inside serious writing is `voice.understating_voice`; the character who plays it in a scene is `comedy.straight_man`.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Documentary, Reels/Shorts, Voiceover Script, Comics | genres: Comedy, Satire, Noir, Crime, Literary
**Avoid when:** The audience does not yet know enough to see the absurdity. Deadpan requires the reader to hold the true scale of the thing; deliver the fact first, then be flat about it. It is also the weakest register in translation and in subtitles, where flatness reads as flatness — see `comedy.translation_resistance`.
**Example:**
> "The company's new offices had been designed with no internal walls, so that ideas could move freely between departments. By March, so could a rumour, a chest infection, and a man from Procurement who had not been seen at a desk since he arrived."

**Yields to:** `voice.understating_voice` — You need the move at sentence level, inside serious writing.  
`voice.deadpan_register`

### The Overexplainer

**Also called:** the over-clarifying narrator, the pedant voice, unnecessary precision
**What it is:** A voice that supplies far more specification than the situation requires — parentheticals, corrections, footnoted qualifications — so that the *quantity of care* becomes the joke and, underneath it, the characterisation.
**Effect on the audience:** Comic on the surface, quietly sad underneath. Nobody explains at that length unless they are frightened of being misunderstood.
**Used for and where it works best:** Comic first person, workplace satire, the narrator who is a specialist in something nobody asked about, and children's books where the joke is an adult being needlessly exact. The craft rule: the over-explanation must be *accurate*. A pedant who is wrong is a different joke and a cheaper one. And the funniest qualifications are the ones that damage the speaker — the detail they could not stop themselves adding. Note the hard limit: this is a voice, not a licence, and it is one semicolon away from `comedy.explained_joke`, which kills the material it lands on.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Essay, Comics, Podcast, Picture Book | genres: Comedy, Satire, Literary, Kids
**Avoid when:** Any scene that needs pace, and any moment of genuine feeling — the register's whole function is to keep feeling at a distance, so it cannot be used to deliver it. It is also exhausting past about three pages without relief.
**Example:**
> "I told her I would be there at seven. I want to be clear that I said seven and not 'about seven', which is a different undertaking, and which is what she later told her sister I had said. I arrived at 6:52 and waited in the car until 7:00 because arriving early is also a kind of pressure. She was not there until 7:40. I am not making a point. I have simply kept the parking receipt."

`voice.the_overexplainer`

### The Mock-Heroic Apparatus

**Also called:** mock-epic, the epic machinery, high style on low material
**What it is:** Not just an elevated word here and there but the whole epic kit sustained across a piece — invocation, formal epithets, catalogues, extended similes, the language of fate — applied to something entirely domestic.
**Effect on the audience:** Cumulative delight. The joke gets funnier the longer the apparatus holds, because the audience starts anticipating which epic convention is coming for the washing machine next.
**Used for and where it works best:** Comic narration, family memoir, sports writing about amateurs, kids' books, and satire of institutions that take themselves seriously. The craft rule: commit to the full machinery, not the vocabulary — one genuine extended simile does more than a page of "thus did he". Give the trivial subject a real stake underneath, or the piece is a long joke with nothing at the bottom of it. Where `voice.mock_formality` is a tonal choice available in a sentence, this is a structural commitment for a whole piece.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Essay, Poetry, Picture Book, Comics, Voiceover Script | genres: Comedy, Satire, Literary, Kids, Heritage
**Avoid when:** The subject is someone's real humiliation or a real defeat. The apparatus is inherently a little cruel even when affectionate, because it measures the subject against Achilles and reports the result. Also avoid it in short form, where there is no room to build the machinery and it reads as one stray fancy word.
**Example:**
> "Sing, then, of the queue at the post office on the twenty-third of December, and of Aneeta, who had three parcels and a printed label, and who had chosen — as the doomed choose — the left-hand queue. And as a river, meeting a fallen tree, divides and goes around it and rejoins itself downstream and is not delayed at all, so did the right-hand queue move."

**Yields to:** `voice.mock_formality` — Short form, with no room to build machinery.  
`voice.mock_heroic_apparatus`

### The Faux-Naive Voice

**Also called:** the innocent register, strategic simplicity, the wide-eyed narrator
**What it is:** A voice that reports plainly what it sees without appearing to understand its significance, while the writer arranges for the reader to understand it perfectly.
**Effect on the audience:** The reader does the moral work and therefore owns the conclusion. It is the most efficient satirical device in prose because the target is never named and cannot be defended against.
**Used for and where it works best:** Satire, child narrators, outsider and newcomer narrators, and any piece where stating the point directly would make the reader defensive. The craft rule: the naivety must be consistent and *structural* — the voice must lack a specific frame of reference, not general intelligence. A narrator who is stupid is tiresome; a narrator who has simply never been told what this institution is for is devastating. And the voice must never wink. The information architecture underneath it is `info.naive_narrator`.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Picture Book, Comics, Reels/Shorts, Stage Play | genres: Satire, Comedy, Kids, Social Drama, War
**Avoid when:** The reader lacks the context to supply the missing judgement. Faux-naivety over an unfamiliar subject produces confusion, not irony. And it is genuinely risky in heritage and testimony work, where a naive narrator over real atrocity can read as the writer arranging a literary effect out of someone else's death.
**Example:**
> "The new machine at the border reads your face and decides. Baba said it decided wrong about him, and I said he should tell them, and he said you cannot tell a machine, and I said then tell the man beside the machine, and he said the man beside the machine is there to say that the machine is working."

**Yields to:** `voice.understating_voice` — Heritage or testimony over real atrocity.  
`voice.faux_naive`

### The Unreliable Enthusiast

**Also called:** the delighted narrator, the cheerful witness, enthusiasm as camouflage
**What it is:** A narrator whose energy and warmth never falter, applied to material the reader can see is a disaster — the voice sells the situation hard while the facts leak out from underneath it.
**Effect on the audience:** Comic dread. The reader starts rooting against the narration, which is a strange and extremely sticky form of attention.
**Used for and where it works best:** Comic memoir, cult and start-up stories, scam narratives, brand-voice satire, and the friend who is telling you about the investment. The craft rule: the enthusiasm must be *earned by the narrator's logic* — they are not lying to the reader, they are winning an argument with themselves, and the reader is overhearing it. Plant one fact per paragraph that the narrator hurries past. And they must never get sadder; the moment the voice admits the situation, the device is over and you had better be at the end. Related but distinct: `voice.unstable_authority` leaks contradictions, this one leaks consequences.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Memoir, Essay, Podcast, Reels/Shorts, Documentary | genres: Comedy, Satire, Crime, Noir, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The material is grief or abuse. Relentless cheer over genuine suffering reads as the writer being pleased with the device, and readers will not forgive it. Also avoid it in long form without an escape hatch — a whole novel of unbroken brightness is wearing, and most successful examples break the voice once, late, for about a paragraph.
**Example:**
> "Month four and we were down to eleven people, which honestly is the right size — you lose the politics. Tom took the sofa home because it was technically his sofa, and that freed up the whole back area for standing meetings, which are better for you anyway. The landlord's been great. We've agreed to talk again in January."

**Yields to:** `voice.unstable_authority` — The material is grief or abuse.  
`voice.unreliable_enthusiast`

## Borrowed Authority

### Writing in a Real Person's Register

**Also called:** the impression, voice capture, writing as them
**What it is:** Reconstructing an identifiable living or historical person's speech well enough that people who know them hear them — their sentence length, their hesitations, their favourite structures and the things they never say.
**Effect on the audience:** Uncanny recognition when it lands, and instant rejection when it does not. There is no partial credit; an audience that knows the person detects a wrong cadence faster than a wrong fact.
**Used for and where it works best:** Biopic and docudrama, heritage documentary reconstructions, ghostwriting, and impressionistic monologue. The craft rule: build from transcripts, not from quotes. Quotes are what a person said memorably; transcripts are how they actually talk — the repetitions, the abandoned starts, the two or three structures they use for everything. Extract five signature habits, not fifty, and write those. Second rule: their silences are part of the register. What subject do they always turn away from? Composite and fictionalising practice lives in `char.real_person_composite`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Documentary, One-Hour Drama, Stage Play, Podcast, Audiobook | genres: Historical, Heritage, Journalism, Drama, Satire
**Avoid when:** You are putting words in a living person's mouth in a way that could be mistaken for a record, and in any context where the audience will not be told it is a reconstruction. Beyond the ethics, it is also a craft failure: a voice the audience thinks is real is being read for evidence, and it cannot bear that weight.
**Example:**
> Quote-built: "We shall fight for what is right, whatever the cost."
> Transcript-built: "Now — look. I'm not going to — yes. Yes, we'll do it. I said we'd do it. Nobody's told me how, but I said we'd do it, so there we are."

**Yields to:** `char.real_person_composite` — Words could be mistaken for a record.  
`voice.real_person_register`

### Pastiche Against Parody

**Also called:** homage versus send-up, the two imitations
**What it is:** Two different jobs that look identical on the page. Pastiche imitates a style faithfully and asks to be enjoyed as that style; parody exaggerates the same style so the audience laughs at it. The difference is entirely in whether the imitation is *affectionate and unexaggerated* or *amplified until it tips*.
**Effect on the audience:** Pastiche produces the warmth of recognition; parody produces the release of ridicule. An audience that cannot tell which one you are doing produces neither and leaves.
**Used for and where it works best:** Continuation novels, genre homage, satirical sketch, franchise writing, and any piece that needs to invoke a tradition. The craft rule: decide before you draft, and mark the decision on the page in the first paragraph — parody declares itself early with one clear exaggeration, pastiche declares itself by being *better behaved than the original*. The other rule is legal and practical both: imitate the conventions, never the actual sentences. Pastiche borrows syntax, rhythm, narrative distance and image habits; it does not borrow lines.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Comics, Stage Play, Reels/Shorts, Feature Film | genres: Comedy, Satire, Crime, Noir, Literary, Historical
**Avoid when:** The audience does not know the original well enough for either to register. Pastiche of an unfamiliar style is just a slightly odd voice; parody of one is nothing at all. Check the reference is genuinely shared before you spend a piece on it.
**Example:**
> Pastiche: "It was raining the way it rains in that part of the city, which is to say without conviction, and she came into the office carrying the kind of trouble that pays in cash."
> Parody: "The rain was crying. I was crying. Even my hat, which I had never trusted, was crying. She walked in like a subpoena with legs and I knew — the way a man knows his own liver — that I would be dead by Thursday."

`voice.pastiche_vs_parody`

### The Voice of a Period

**Also called:** period diction, archaic pastiche, historical register, timeless prose
**What it is:** Writing so that the narration and speech belong to a past era — either by genuine period idiom, or by the more useful trick of a neutral register with every anachronism removed.
**Effect on the audience:** Immersion when it is invisible, costume when it is not. The reader does not consciously credit correct period diction; they only ever notice the wrong word.
**Used for and where it works best:** Historical fiction, heritage documentary, period drama, and any Mesopotamian or ancient-world VO. **What archaic pastiche promises:** instant atmosphere. **What it costs:** comprehension and dignity — *thee*, *hath* and inverted syntax buy nothing a modern reader values and make most characters sound like a Christmas card. The craft rule is subtraction, not addition: write plain, contemporary-neutral English, then remove every word, metaphor and concept the period did not have. That single pass does ninety percent of the work. Watch the metaphor source hardest — a character cannot compare anything to a photograph, a train or a clock face before those exist, and `voice.lexical_field` decides what they *can* reach for. Keep one or two genuine period terms per scene as anchors, and gloss them through action, never in line.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Documentary, Voiceover Script, Comics | genres: Historical, Heritage, War, Drama, Literary
**Avoid when:** The period's real register would be unreadable or would encode values you are not equipped to handle deliberately. And be careful in ancient-world work: there is no "authentic" English for Akkadian, so the honest choice is a defensible register, chosen and held — usually plain and slightly formal — not an approximation of one.
**Example:**
> Pastiche: "Hark, brother, for I have grave tidings of the king."
> Subtracted: "Come inside. Shut it. The king is dead and I have been walking since the second watch."

**Yields to:** `voice.register_setting` — Ancient-world work; choose a defensible band and hold it.  
`voice.period_voice`

### The Voice of a Document Type

**Also called:** genre-of-document register, form fidelity, the shape of the paper
**What it is:** Writing something that is not prose — a police report, a recipe, a school report card, a ship's log, a WhatsApp thread, a product listing — in the register that document type actually uses, including its conventions of length, order and omission.
**Effect on the audience:** Total credibility, cheaply. Readers apply their real-world trust in the form to the fiction inside it, and they read documents faster and more literally than they read prose.
**Used for and where it works best:** Horror, mystery, epistolary novels, ARGs, comedy, and any story that needs a fact to arrive without a narrator. The craft rule: form before content. Get the *order of fields* right — a real incident report front-loads the administratively important and buries the terrifying, a real recipe never explains why, a real chat thread has an unanswered message. Then hide the story in the field the form does not consider important. Presenting it inside a narrative is `voice.found_document`; the plot mechanics of what it discloses are `info.the_document`.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Comics, Game Narrative, Podcast, Reels/Shorts | genres: Horror, Crime, Satire, Comedy, Historical, Heritage
**Avoid when:** You have not seen a real example of the document. Invented form conventions are visible immediately and cost you the credibility the technique exists to borrow. Also avoid stacking more than two or three document types in a piece; past that, the reader is doing administration rather than reading.
**Example:**
> "**Nursery daily sheet — Thursday**
> Lunch: ate well. Nap: 40 mins. Nappies: 3.
> Activities: painting, garden, story corner.
> Notes for parents: Sama asked staff twice today when the man in the yellow coat is coming back. Please advise — no yellow coat on our visitor log."

`voice.document_type_voice`

## Voice in Production

### The House Style

**Also called:** editorial style, the publication voice, the style guide
**What it is:** A fixed set of mechanical and register decisions that everyone writing for an outlet follows — serial commas, numerals, honorifics, contractions, what the audience is called, which words are banned — so that many writers produce one recognisable surface.
**Effect on the audience:** Unconscious trust. A publication that is mechanically consistent reads as competent, and readers extend that judgement to the facts inside it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Publications, channels, documentation teams, brands, and any project with more than two writers or any language model in the loop. The craft rule: the house style should settle only the decisions that would otherwise be *visibly* inconsistent, and settle nothing else. A style guide that legislates sentence rhythm produces identical, dead copy — that is `dial.writer_voice_uniformity` applied to a whole masthead. Write it as decisions plus examples, and keep a live banned-word list, because banned words are the fastest-moving part of any house style. Its narrative sibling is `voice.voice_bible`.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary, Explainer, Advertising, Brand Film, Podcast, Essay | genres: Journalism, Educational, Heritage, Kids
**Avoid when:** A single-author literary project, and any piece whose value is one person's idiosyncrasy. Applying a house style to a personal essay sands off the reason anyone wanted it.
**Example:**
> "Numerals from 10 up; spell out below. No honorifics after first mention. 'Died', never 'passed away'. We say 'people', not 'individuals'. We do not say 'delve', 'landscape', 'tapestry', 'journey' or 'unpack'. Contractions in features, not in obituaries."

**Yields to:** `voice.voice_bible` — Narrative or series work rather than a publication.  
`voice.house_style`

### Writing to an Existing Brand Voice

**Also called:** voice intake, brand voice capture, writing in their register
**What it is:** Reconstructing a client's or organisation's established voice from their existing material well enough that new work is indistinguishable from old — before writing a word of your own.
**Effect on the audience:** Continuity they never notice. The failure is noticed instantly, usually by the client, usually as "this doesn't sound like us" with no further explanation available.
**Used for and where it works best:** Agency work, ghostwriting, channel and social copy, and every freelance brief where someone already has a voice. The craft rule: do the intake as extraction, not as description. Collect ten pieces they are proud of and two they rejected, then pull out the mechanical facts — average sentence length, contractions or not, how they open, how they close, their three most-used verbs, whether they use questions, what they call the customer. Adjectives like "warm" and "confident" are worthless; the rejected pieces are worth more than the approved ones because they define the boundary. Then write one paragraph and get it approved before writing the rest — a full draft in a wrong voice is a full draft wasted.
**Best in:** formats: Advertising, Brand Film, Explainer, Reels/Shorts, Podcast, Documentary | genres: Journalism, Educational, Comedy, Heritage
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-09. The intake procedure is durable; the specific platform formats a brand voice has to survive in are not.
**Avoid when:** The existing voice is bad and the brief is actually to replace it. Say so at intake rather than discovering it in round three. And do not do this to your own work — writers who spend years in other people's registers lose the ability to find their own quickly, which is the real occupational cost of the job.
**Example:**
> Intake note, extracted: "Never opens with a question. Average sentence 11 words. Contractions always. Calls them 'you', never 'our customers'. Ends on an instruction, not a summary. Uses 'made' and 'built' constantly; has never once used 'crafted'."

**Yields to:** `voice.voice_bible` — The brief is actually to replace the voice.  
`voice.brand_voice_intake`

### Matching an Established Series Voice

**Also called:** writing into a running show, the staff-writer problem, franchise voice
**What it is:** Joining a project whose voice was set by someone else and writing material that sits inside it — the narration's register, the show's joke rate, its tolerance for sentiment, its silences.
**Effect on the audience:** Invisibility, which is the whole job. Audiences cannot name the episode that felt wrong, but they can feel it, and long-running-series audiences leave over voice mismatch more readily than over plot.
**Used for and where it works best:** Episodic TV, animation series, channel content, continuation novels, and any hand-off between writers. The craft rule: build a calibration sample from the *best* existing material, not the most recent, and read it aloud before every session. Then identify the show's three hard rules — the things it never does — because those are what a new writer breaks first and they are almost never written down. Do the first episode as deliberate imitation and earn variation later. `theme.series_consistency` covers the argument-level version of the same discipline, and `voice.tonal_drift` is what happens when nobody does this.
**Best in:** formats: One-Hour Drama, Podcast, Comics, Game Narrative, Documentary, Novel | genres: Crime, Drama, Comedy, Kids, Heritage
**Avoid when:** You were hired to change it. Then get the mandate explicit and in writing, because "freshen it up" and "make it sound like us" are the same sentence in most briefs and only one of them is survivable.
**Example:**
> Calibration note: "This show never explains a joke, never scores a death with music, and never lets the lead be right twice in a row. Episode 6 did all three. That is the episode people cite when they say it went off."

`voice.series_voice_match`

### Taking Over Another Writer's Character

**Also called:** inherited character voice, the continuation problem, writing someone else's person
**What it is:** Writing dialogue and interiority for a character created by someone else, so that existing readers hear the same person rather than a competent impostor.
**Effect on the audience:** Fierce loyalty or fierce rejection. Readers own characters in a way they do not own plots, and they detect a wrong line before they can articulate it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Franchise fiction, staff-written television, comics, licensed games, and estate-authorised continuations. The craft rule: extract the character's *grammar*, not their catchphrases. Catchphrases are what imitators copy and what makes continuations feel like impressions. The grammar is: how long are their sentences, do they answer questions directly, do they use names, what do they do when they are frightened, what will they not say. Write six lines of dialogue for a scene from the original book and check them against what is actually there — you will be wrong about at least two, and those two are your real lesson. Then find the character's `char.the_secret` and never contradict it.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Comics, One-Hour Drama, Game Narrative, Audio Drama, Feature Film | genres: Crime, Drama, Comedy, Kids, Historical, Noir
**Avoid when:** The original writer's voice and the character's voice are inseparable — some characters are a prose style with a name attached, and those do not transfer. Recognise it early rather than producing a book-length impression.
**Example:**
> Catchphrase-built: "Right then. Let's have a look, shall we? Ha! Classic."
> Grammar-built: "No." A pause of exactly the wrong length. "Ask me the other thing. The thing you came to ask."

`voice.inherited_character_voice`

### The Voice Note and the Transcript

**Also called:** transcript register, verbatim voice, the recording as source
**What it is:** Two related things — writing a voice that is being spoken into a phone with no audience present, and working from real transcribed speech as raw material for a written voice.
**Effect on the audience:** Startling authenticity, because real speech is far stranger than written dialogue: it repeats, abandons clauses, answers questions that were not asked, and corrects itself constantly.
**Used for and where it works best:** Audio drama, podcast, documentary, contemporary fiction, and any interview-derived work. The craft rule for the voice note: it is speech with no listener yet, so it is looser and more honest than a conversation and it always over-explains the practical while underplaying the emotional — the person says where they are and what time it is, then mentions the diagnosis last. The craft rule for the transcript: never use verbatim speech at length, and never clean it up completely. Cut ninety percent, keep the two disfluencies that carry the meaning, and keep the abandoned sentence. The dialogue-level card is `dial.voice_note`.
**Best in:** formats: Audio Drama, Podcast, Documentary, Novel, Reels/Shorts, One-Hour Drama | genres: Crime, Journalism, Social Drama, Drama, Comedy
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-09. Voice-note conventions and automatic-transcription artefacts both track platform behaviour; the verbatim-editing rule underneath is durable.
**Avoid when:** The piece will be subtitled or translated, where disfluency reads as error and gets cleaned out by whoever does the pass — brief them, or write it clean. And never publish a real transcript as though it were tidy speech; that is a misrepresentation with a quotation mark around it.
**Example:**
> "Hi, it's me, I'm just — sorry, I'm just parking. It's about twenty past. Anyway you don't need to call back, honestly, I only rang because I'm on the road and it's easier. Um. They did say the results came. Anyway. Tell Marta the thing about Sunday is fine."

**Yields to:** `rhythm.write_for_translator` — The piece will be subtitled or translated.  
`voice.transcript_voice`

## Voice Across Formats

### Voice in Action Lines

**Also called:** scene-description voice, the screenwriter's register, the unfilmable line
**What it is:** The register of a screenplay's description — technically a production document, actually the only place a screenwriter's own voice is allowed to exist, and the thing a reader is really being sold on.
**Effect on the audience:** The script's first reader is a professional who reads sixty a month. A page with a voice gets read; a page of camera instructions gets skimmed to the dialogue.
**Used for and where it works best:** Every screenplay, especially the specs and the first ten pages. The craft rule: the voice lives in verb choice, paragraph length and white space, never in adjectives or in telling us what cannot be photographed. Short paragraphs, one image each; a one-line paragraph is a cut. You are allowed one unfilmable sentence per ten pages and it should be the best sentence on the page — the industry pretends this rule does not exist and every produced writer uses it. Rhythm mechanics are `rhythm.action_line_rhythm`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Reels/Shorts, Brand Film, Comics | genres: Drama, Crime, Comedy, Noir, War, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** A shooting script or a production draft, where the department heads need clean information and a personality in the description costs someone time. Also avoid it in any format where the "script" is really a spec for a machine — an AI video prompt is not a place for voice, it is a place for unambiguous nouns.
**Example:**
> Flat: "Interior, kitchen, night. John enters the room looking upset and sits down at the table where his wife is already sitting."
> Voiced: "The kitchen. Too many lights on for this time of night.
> John comes in. Doesn't take the coat off. Sits.
> Marta has been sitting there long enough for her tea to have stopped steaming."

**Yields to:** `voice.suppressing_your_voice` — Shooting script, production draft, or an AI prompt.  
`voice.action_line_voice`

### Voice in Comics Captions

**Also called:** the caption box, narration boxes, the comics narrator
**What it is:** The narrating voice in the box, running alongside pictures that are already doing the description — which means the caption's only legitimate jobs are time, interiority, irony and rhythm.
**Effect on the audience:** Read almost simultaneously with the image, so a caption that contradicts the panel produces an effect available in no other medium: two truths at once, at zero cost.
**Used for and where it works best:** Literary comics, crime and noir comics, memoir comics, and any sequence compressing years. The craft rule: never caption what the panel shows. The caption that says "she was furious" over a picture of a furious woman is the format's signature failure. Instead run the caption *against* the picture, or ahead of it, or in a different time from it. Keep them under about twenty-five words — the box eats art, and a letterer will tell you so. Panel-level POV mechanics are `pov.comics_caption_panel`.
**Best in:** formats: Comics, Picture Book, Game Narrative, Reels/Shorts | genres: Noir, Crime, Literary, Heritage, Historical, War
**Avoid when:** Action sequences, where the eye wants to move and a box stops it, and any page where the art has been given the emotional work. Silence is a caption choice — see `scene.wordless_channel_discipline`.
**Example:**
> Panel: a man setting a table for two, carefully, in an empty flat.
> Caption: "By then he had got very good at it. Forks on the left. He was, everyone agreed, doing remarkably well."

**Yields to:** `scene.wordless_channel_discipline` — Action pages, or where art carries the emotion.  
`voice.caption_voice`

### The Podcast Host Voice

**Also called:** the host register, the mic persona, the in-ear voice
**What it is:** A voice built for someone alone with headphones, doing something else — warmer and more conversational than broadcast, more structured than actual conversation, and carrying the show's promise in how it sounds rather than what it says.
**Effect on the audience:** Parasocial attachment, which is the format's actual product. Listeners stay for a host they feel they know long after they have stopped caring about the topic.
**Used for and where it works best:** Interview, narrative and educational podcasts, and audio-first heritage work. The craft rule: written-to-be-spoken, not written and then spoken — contractions, the listener addressed as one person, and every scripted line short enough to be said on one breath. Signpost constantly, because a distracted listener who loses the thread does not scrub back, they leave. And script the host's *fallibility*: the small admission, the mispronunciation acknowledged, the question they did not know to ask — each said as a plain sentence of its own, not as a line revised mid-flight, which a listener on one pass cannot follow (`voice.self_correcting_narrator`). Polish is the enemy of intimacy here in a way it is not in any other narration format. Build on `voice.write_for_the_ear` and `rhythm.vo_breath_line`.
**Best in:** formats: Podcast, Audiobook, Voiceover Script, Documentary, Explainer | genres: Journalism, Educational, Crime, Heritage, Comedy
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-09. Host-voice conventions move with the platform and with audience tolerance for polish; the one-listener principle underneath does not.
**Avoid when:** Ceremonial or memorial audio, where warmth reads as familiarity with someone else's loss. And avoid the register in anything a listener will use as reference — a conversational host voice is very hard to search, scan or cite.
**Example:**
> "So I want to tell you about a wall. And before you switch off — I know. I said the same thing when the producer pitched it. Give me two minutes, because I got the date wrong on this for about a year, and I got it wrong in public."

**Yields to:** `voice.eulogy_register` — Ceremonial or memorial audio, where warmth intrudes.  
`voice.host_voice`

### Voice in Interactive Text

**Also called:** the responsive narrator, parser voice, branching narration
**What it is:** Narration that must stay in one register across every path the reader can take, including the paths where they do something stupid, do nothing, or repeat an action forty times.
**Effect on the audience:** The narrator becomes the one constant in a story the player is deforming, and its consistency is what makes their choices feel like they landed somewhere real.
**Used for and where it works best:** Interactive fiction, narrative games, chat fiction, and any branching format. The craft rule: the register has to survive failure states and repetition, so write the refusal messages and the "nothing happens" lines in the same voice as the best scene — those lines are read more often than any scene and they are where amateur work drops its register entirely. Second rule: the narrator's relationship to the player must be decided up front — accomplice, jailer, historian, or the player's own later self — because an inconsistent one reads as a bug. Related: `inter.player_character_voice`, `inter.presence_writing`, `inter.paraphrase_drift`.
**Best in:** formats: Game Narrative, Reels/Shorts, Podcast, Novel | genres: Crime, Noir, Literary, Historical, Comedy, Kids
**Avoid when:** The interaction is mechanical rather than narrative — an inventory screen does not need a voice, and a narrator commenting on menu navigation ages within one playthrough. And be careful with a strongly-flavoured narrator in a long game; a voice that is delicious for an hour can be intolerable at twenty.
**Example:**
> Dropped register: "You can't do that."
> Held: "You try the door again. It is the same door. I have watched better men than you negotiate with this door and I have buried all of them on the other side of it."

`voice.interactive_voice`

## The Writer's Own Voice

### Finding Your Own Voice

**Also called:** developing a voice, the writer's signature, what only you sound like
**What it is:** The set of habits that recur across everything a writer produces regardless of subject — their sentence length preference, their distance from their material, what they find funny, what they refuse to say.
**Effect on the audience:** Recognition across works. It is the only thing that makes a reader follow a writer rather than a genre.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any career, and the answer to "why does my work feel generic". The craft rule: your voice is not found by looking inward, it is found by *elimination in public*. Write a great deal, then read three years of your own work at once and mark what recurs when you were not trying — those recurrences are the voice, and most of them will surprise you. The second and harder rule: voice comes from what you actually notice and what you are willing to admit, so it is limited by your honesty rather than by your vocabulary. Writers with small voices are usually not underskilled; they are being careful. See `char.what_they_notice` — the same mechanism, applied to yourself.
**Best in:** formats: Essay, Memoir, Novel, Short Story, Poetry, Podcast | genres: Literary, Memoir, Journalism, Comedy
**Avoid when:** You are on deadline for someone else's brief. Voice-finding is a long-horizon activity and it is not what a client is paying for this week. Also, do not go looking for it in your first fifty thousand words; there is nothing there to find yet.
**Example:**
> Three years of one writer's drafts, marked for recurrence: short paragraphs; almost never describes faces; always describes hands and floors; sets everything at the end of an event rather than during it; has used the word "anyway" as a paragraph opener 61 times. That is the voice. Nobody chose it.

**Yields to:** `voice.brand_voice_intake` — On deadline for someone else's brief.  
`voice.finding_your_voice`

### Voice Against Style

**Also called:** the voice/style distinction, identity versus habit
**What it is:** The working separation between them. Style is the set of surface techniques a writer uses and can change deliberately — sentence length, punctuation, imagery density. Voice is who is speaking and with what authority, and it is largely not adjustable per project.
**Effect on the audience:** Getting this wrong is why most "find your voice" advice fails. A writer who changes their punctuation and diction believes they have changed voice; the reader hears the same person with a new haircut.
**Used for and where it works best:** Diagnosing a piece that "does not sound right", and deciding what a revision can actually fix. The craft rule: if the problem is that sentences are flabby, or the imagery is stale, or the rhythm is monotonous, it is style and it is fixable this week — `rhythm.monotone_length`, `rhythm.overwritten_sentence`, `voice.sentence_rhythm`. If the problem is that nobody seems to be speaking, or the piece has no stake in its own subject, that is voice and no amount of sentence work will touch it. Go back to `voice.narrating_persona` and `voice.implied_listener`. Third term for completeness: tone is the mood of a single passage and is the most adjustable of the three.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Essay, Memoir, Short Story, Documentary, Podcast | genres: Literary, Journalism, Drama, Memoir
**Avoid when:** As a live drafting concern. This is a revision and diagnosis distinction; thinking about it while drafting produces self-consciousness, which flattens both.
**Example:**
> Style fixed, voice untouched: the sentences got shorter, the adverbs went, the semicolons went. It still reads like a competent person with no reason to be telling us this.
> Voice fixed: one line added on page one — "I was the one who signed the form." Nothing else changed. The whole chapter now has a floor under it.

`voice.voice_vs_style`

### Suppressing Your Own Voice

**Also called:** transparent prose, getting out of the way, service writing
**What it is:** Deliberately writing so that no personality is detectable — the deliberate opposite of every other card in this file, and a professional skill in its own right.
**Effect on the audience:** They get the information, the character or the client's message with nothing standing between them and it. The highest compliment this work receives is that nobody mentions it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Technical documentation, subtitles, ghostwriting, safety and public-service copy, character-driven fiction where the narration must disappear behind the character, and any heritage piece where the material is more interesting than any commentary on it. The craft rule: suppression is active work, not the absence of work. The marks of a writer are still there — your favourite sentence shape, your habitual metaphor source, your instinct to be funny at the second paragraph — and you have to find and remove them, which means you must know what they are first. That is why `voice.finding_your_voice` comes before this card and not after it.
**Best in:** formats: Explainer, Documentary, Audiobook, Novel, Voiceover Script, Advertising | genres: Educational, Journalism, Heritage, Kids
**Avoid when:** The audience came for you specifically, and in any piece that needs someone to take responsibility for a judgement. Suppressed voice cannot make an argument — with nobody speaking, there is nobody to believe.
**Example:**
> Voiced, and wrong for the job: "The eastern wall — and here is where the story turns strange — had been under repair since autumn."
> Suppressed: "The eastern wall had been under repair since autumn. In March it came down."

**Yields to:** `voice.narrating_persona` — The audience came for you, or a judgement needs owning.  
`voice.suppressing_your_voice`

### Imitation as Training and as a Trap

**Also called:** the copying exercise, writing through someone, apprenticeship imitation
**What it is:** Deliberately writing in another writer's voice as practice — either by copying their prose out longhand, or by writing new material to their specifications — in order to learn techniques that description cannot transmit.
**Effect on the audience:** None, directly: the output of this exercise should usually never be published. The effect is on the writer, and it is large.
**Used for and where it works best:** Learning any technique you can feel but not name — a particular rhythm, a way of handling time, a distance you cannot achieve. The craft rule: imitate *one* named mechanism at a time and write your own material through it, not a copy of theirs. "Write your childhood in his sentence rhythm" teaches; "write a scene like his" teaches nothing. The trap: imitation is a phase and it has an exit, and the exit is imitating a second and third writer whose habits contradict the first. Writers who imitate one master for years do not acquire a voice, they acquire an accent. Second trap: what you can imitate is the surface, so imitating a writer whose power is in their judgement produces a hollow copy — you will get the sentences and not the reason.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Essay, Poetry, Memoir | genres: Literary, Comedy, Crime, Historical
**Avoid when:** The output is going anywhere near a reader, unless it is declared pastiche — see `voice.pastiche_vs_parody`. And avoid it as a deadline tool: reaching for someone else's voice under pressure is how a project acquires a register it cannot sustain.
**Example:**
> Useless exercise: "Write a paragraph in the style of Hemingway." Produces short sentences and a drink.
> Useful exercise: "Take your own worst scene. Rewrite it using only concrete nouns, no interiority, and no sentence over twelve words. Now put one interior sentence back, in the last line only, and see what it costs."

**Yields to:** `voice.pastiche_vs_parody` — The output is going near a reader.  
`voice.imitation_as_training`

## Named Failures

### The AI-Default Register

**Also called:** model voice, assistant register, AI slop, the flat house style of a language model
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** The register a language model produces when nobody has specified one — evenly hedged, structurally balanced, list-prone, relentlessly parallel, and pitched to offend no one. It is not a style choice; it is the average of an enormous amount of training text plus a reward signal that favoured helpfulness, safety and completeness over commitment.
**Effect on the audience:** In 2026, immediate recognition and a drop in trust — often before the reader has consciously identified why. The prose is fluent, and readers have learned that fluency is no longer evidence of anything. Editors now over-correct against it, striking legitimate triads and legitimate em dashes out of human copy.
**Used for and where it works best:** Diagnosis. The recognisable markers, in rough order of how reliably they give it away: **structural balance** — presenting multiple perspectives when the piece only needed one, and closing on a both-sides summary nobody asked for; **the hedge stack** — *it is worth noting*, *generally speaking*, *while this may vary*, *it could be argued*, applied to the paragraph instead of to a claim; **the symmetrical negation** — *it's not X, it's Y*, and its cousin *not just X but Y*, which sound profound and commit to nothing; **low burstiness** — paragraphs of near-identical length and sentences clustered within a few words of each other, where real prose puts a three-word sentence next to a forty-word one; **the reflexive triad**, three adjectives or three clauses where one precise word was available; **markdown leakage** — bulleting prose that wanted to be a paragraph, headers on a piece that has no sections, mid-sentence bold; **the vocabulary set** — *delve*, *leverage*, *foster*, *underscore*, *seamless*, *robust*, *multifaceted*, *tapestry*, *landscape*, *realm*, *testament*, *journey*, *unpack*, *navigate the complexities of*; **the opening cliché** — *in today's fast-paced world*, *in the ever-evolving landscape of*; and **transitional filler** — *furthermore*, *moreover*, *additionally*, *ultimately*, *in essence*.

The repair is not a banned-word list, because the list is a symptom. Banned words are treated first because they are cheap, and they are also the fastest-moving part of the problem — a model retrained tomorrow drops *delve* and picks up something else, while the structural tells persist. The cards that actually fix it, in order: `voice.narrating_persona` (there is no one speaking, which is the root cause and every other marker is downstream of it), `voice.implied_listener` (specify who is being addressed and most hedging becomes obviously absurd), `voice.sentence_rhythm` and `rhythm.monotone_length` (count the words per sentence; the numbers will cluster, and the fix is to put the shortest next to the longest), `voice.register_setting` (pick a band and write the three test words in it before drafting), `voice.authority_by_specificity` (one exact unshowy detail per section outranks every stylistic fix on this list), `voice.the_hedge` (hedge the specific claim or not at all), and `voice.voice_bible` or `voice.house_style` built from twenty before/after pairs — because a model will obey examples and will ignore adjectives. The single highest-yield instruction is the one most people skip: tell it *who is speaking and to whom*, not *what tone to use*.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-03. This card describes the default output of current models and the state of reader sensitivity as of September 2026. The specific vocabulary and punctuation markers will be stale within a year — the em dash already is, having been the signature tell of 2024-25 and now measured as unreliable. The structural markers (balance, hedging, low burstiness, parallelism) come from how these systems are trained rather than from a word list and will outlive the vocabulary. Re-verify the marker list before relying on it.
**Avoid when:** Do not use this card as a purity test on human writers. Hedging, balance, triads and em dashes are all legitimate craft, and several of the markers above are literally other cards in this database used well. The failure is *all of them at once, with nobody behind them*. Also note the direction of the error: an editor who strips every triad and every dash from human copy produces prose that is worse and still voiceless.
**Example:**
> Default: "It's not just a building — it's a testament to the enduring resilience of a community. While opinions differ on the restoration, it's worth noting that the site remains a pivotal landmark, offering visitors a multifaceted journey through the region's rich tapestry of history."
> Repaired (persona: the site's foreman, to a journalist, twenty years after): "I laid some of that brick. Not the good part — the part above the door, which you can tell, because it's the part that's wrong. People come and photograph the door. Nobody's ever once asked who fixed it, and I'd have told them, and I'd have told them what it cost."

**Yields to:** `voice.narrating_persona` — Root repair: nobody is speaking in the prose.  
`voice.ai_default_register`

### Purple Prose and the Thesaurus Voice

**Also called:** overwriting, elevated diction disease, the synonym reflex
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** Two failures that always travel together. Purple prose is ornamentation exceeding what the moment can carry — stacked adjectives, elaborate metaphor, sustained lyricism over an ordinary event. The thesaurus voice is its engine: reaching for the less common synonym on the assumption that rarity equals quality, so characters *ambulate* and *vociferate* and never walk or shout.
**Effect on the audience:** They stop seeing the scene and start seeing the writing, which means they start assessing the writer. That assessment is rarely favourable, and it does not reset — once a reader has caught you performing, they read the next page as performance too.
**Used for and where it works best:** Diagnosis. The craft rules: first, a word you would not say out loud is a word that does not belong in your prose, with one exception — a genuinely technical term used by someone who really has that job, which is `char.trade_vocabulary` and reads as authority, not decoration. Second, ornament must be *earned by stakes*: three lines of lyricism on a character's death is grief, three lines on a coffee cup is a writer who liked their own sentence. Third, the tell is modifier density — count the adjectives and adverbs in your favourite paragraph, because favourite paragraphs are where this lives. See `rhythm.overwritten_sentence` and `rhythm.adverb_discipline`. Fourth, and least popular: cut the line you are proudest of and read the passage again. If nothing was lost, it was decoration, and you now know what your particular decoration looks like.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Do not flatten deliberately ornate registers. Gothic, high fantasy, mock-heroic, liturgical and some literary voices are *supposed* to be dense, and applying a plain-prose rule to them removes the thing the reader came for. The test is not density, it is whether the ornament is doing work: in real gothic every excessive image carries dread. And do not confuse this with a second-language or period voice, where formality is characterisation — see `voice.second_language_voice` and `voice.period_voice`.
**Example:**
> Purple: "The crepuscular luminescence cascaded through the fenestration, bathing her alabaster visage in a coruscating effulgence that seemed to whisper of secrets long interred beneath the mouldering earth of ages past."
> Fixed: "The last of the light came through the window and found her face. She did not move out of it."

**Yields to:** `voice.mock_heroic_apparatus` — Deliberately ornate registers where density is the point.  
`voice.purple_prose`

### The Narrator Who Outsmarts the Story

**Also called:** the superior narrator, the writer showing their marks, narration that condescends to its own material
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** Narration that consistently knows more, sees more and judges better than anyone inside the story — commenting on characters' limitations, explaining their self-deceptions, and arriving at the meaning of a scene before the scene has finished playing it.
**Effect on the audience:** They side with the characters against the narration. It also removes their job: if the narrator has already reached the conclusion, there is nothing for the reader to do but agree, and readers do not become attached to work they were not needed for.
**Used for and where it works best:** Diagnosis, and a common and serious failure in literary fiction, essay-inflected novels and heritage documentary alike. The craft rule: the narrator may be wiser than the characters — that is a legitimate and ancient position — but they may not be *pleased about it*. The tell is the explanatory clause after the action: *she said it lightly, not understanding yet what it would cost her*. Delete the clause. If the scene does not survive the deletion, the problem is the scene. Second tell: the narrator has never once been wrong. Give them one visible misjudgement early, and their later judgements become evidence instead of decree. Related: `voice.register_creep` is the vocabulary symptom of the same disease, and `dial.on_the_nose` is what it does to dialogue.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: —
**Avoid when:** Do not apply this to a genuinely ironic or satirical omniscient narrator, where superiority to the characters is the form and the audience has signed up for it — but check that the superiority is aimed at institutions and pretensions rather than at someone's private pain, because that is where affectionate irony turns into contempt and audiences feel the difference immediately.
**Example:**
> Outsmarted: "She told him it was fine, in the bright voice she used for these things, unaware that she had just made the decision that would define the rest of her life."
> Held: "She told him it was fine. She used the bright voice. He believed her, and she watched him believe her, and neither of them said anything else about it that year."

**Yields to:** `voice.the_hedge` — Give the narrator a visible limit, not decree.  
`voice.narrator_outsmarts_story`
