## More of the specific emotions

### What Pays for the Feeling

**Also called:** the emotion selector, room register and ledger, what is paying for this
**What it is:** The card at the head of the `emo.` shelf. It does not describe a feeling and it cannot choose one for you — the situation does that before you arrive. It asks the three questions the shelf's own pair graph and `Avoid when:` lines ask over and over: **how much room has the feeling got before it must land, where in the text is it allowed to live, and is the piece building the investment, spending one it already made, or borrowing one the audience walked in holding.** The axes were not invented. They were read off the forty-nine `Yields to:` conditions the shelf carries and cross-checked against every `Avoid when:` on it, and the conditions that repeated became the questions. Every card cited here is cited by id.
**Effect on the audience:** None. This card never reaches a reader. It exists so that the card that does reach them was chosen against the page count, the register and the ledger, rather than picked because it was the last emotional technique that worked.
**Used for and where it works best:** Answer the three **in order and out loud, one sentence each, before you open the shelf.** The first answer eliminates most of the file; the second and third leave a shortlist of three to six. If you cannot answer question one — *how much room has this got* — you do not have a scene yet, you have a mood, and the shelf cannot help with a mood. Where two answers fight over one passage, that is two beats and one of them is `emo.emotional_turn`.
**Best in:** formats: all narrative | genres: all narrative
**Avoid when:** **The person is real.** In testimony, in a crisis address, in a room with a bereaved family, the craft question and the ethical question are the same question, and this map answers only the craft half; `emo.testimony_emotion` and `emo.extracted_emotion` are read first and they outrank every shortlist below. **And distrust axis two whenever your honest answer is its middle value** — nearly half the shelf lives in behaviour, so on an ordinary drama scene this map has two questions left and is doing two-thirds of the work it does on a commissioned piece. **It also cannot pick the feeling, and it will not tell you it has failed to.** Only three edges on this whole shelf run from one feeling to another, and every one of them is a correction of a misnaming rather than a choice — so if your answer to *which feeling is this* needs more than one word, the map will hand you a clean, confident shortlist for the wrong emotion. Finally, a **declared heightened register** — melodrama, telenovela, farce, vertical micro-drama — overrides axis two wholesale, and the genre contract decides that, not this card.
**Example:**
> *A father learns at the hospital that his son has died; the film is a ninety-minute drama.*
> **Room:** a run of scenes — the news is not the beat, the eleven days after it are. **Where it lives:** in a behaviour; nobody in this family says a feeling out loud. **Ledger:** spend — forty minutes of him being ordinary have already been bought.
> Shortlist: `emo.delayed_reaction` · `emo.withhold_reaction` · `emo.grief`. The scene at the hospital is `emo.bad_news_delivery`; the scene that is actually about it happens in a car park eleven days later.

**The axes**

**Axis 1 — How much room has the feeling got before it must land?**
Asked first, because it is the condition the shelf repeats most and the only one it states as an arithmetic rather than a preference. Nineteen boundaries turn on it: *"Short form cannot build a situation; borrow a carried one"* (`emo.earn_the_situation`), *"Short form with one beat"* (`emo.contrast_sets_level`), *"the format cannot hold two scenes"* (`emo.delayed_reaction`), *"plant and payoff sit too close"* (`emo.deferred_payoff`), *"No loss and no clock"* (`emo.joy`), *"one shot, not one act"* (`emo.horror_aftermath`), *"a ceiling of roughly one scene before they leave"* (`emo.numbness`), *"the episode is genuinely a bridge and the series is short"* (`emo.episode_emotional_shape`), *"Serialised show whose promise is accumulation"* (`emo.procedural_reset`). Emotion is the one thing in this package that cannot be brute-forced into less space than it costs, and the shelf says so on card after card.

**Axis 2 — Where is the feeling allowed to live: in a word, in a behaviour, or in an arrangement?**
Fourteen conditions turn on this, and it is the shelf's own governing sentence read as a fork rather than as advice. The pair graph moves readers across it in both directions: from a word to a behaviour — *"at any emotional peak"* (`emo.telling_the_emotion` to `emo.physical_register` and `emo.specific_detail`), *"Middle grade and above; behaviour, not labels"* (`emo.kids_emotion`), *"The declaration itself is the event"* (`emo.love_without_declaration`), *"Writing the recipient's reaction"* (`emo.bad_news_delivery`) — and from a behaviour to an arrangement — *"detail substituting for structure"* (`emo.specific_detail`), *"No earlier investment for the audience to spend"* (`emo.withhold_reaction`), *"play the cut with the sound off"* (`emo.score_does_the_work`). Note which way those repairs run: **nothing on this shelf ever sends a reader from an arrangement back to a word.**

**Axis 3 — Is the piece building the investment, spending one, or borrowing one?**
Eighteen conditions turn on the ledger, and the shelf supplies all three words itself. *Build:* the situation is constructed on the page, and `emo.earn_the_situation` is the whole of it. *Spend:* *"This technique spends earlier investment; with no investment, turning away reads as evasion"* (`emo.withhold_reaction`), *"The audience has paid for release"* (`emo.withheld_catharsis`), *"The achievement has not been costed"* (`emo.pride`), *"The witness has no relationship to the audience"* (`emo.someone_else_feels_it`). *Borrow:* *"borrow a carried one"* (`emo.earn_the_situation`), *"a word for a state they already had"* (`emo.kids_emotion`), *"the thing the product's users actually put up with"* (`emo.brand_emotion`), *"a feeling the audience already has"* (`emo.permission_piece`). It is asked last because it is the only axis that reaches the commissioned block at all — and because the shelf names a failure on each side of its borrow boundary: `emo.borrowed_feeling` takes a carried feeling it has no right to, and `emo.manufactured_inadequacy` installs one nobody was carrying.

**The default, and the one against it**

**The shelf's own graph refuses to name a default, and that refusal is the measurement.** `emo.earn_the_situation` takes more inbound edges than anything else here — `emo.specific_detail`, `emo.withhold_reaction`, `emo.sentimentality` and `emo.score_does_the_work` all send a reader to it — but count the conditions rather than the edges and they collapse to one sentence: *the surface is carrying what the situation never paid for*, said four times by four different failures. Under the `tens.` rule that is the shape of a drain, not of a fallback. Nothing on this shelf is reached from five conditions. Nothing is reached from three.

**So the default is argued, not counted, and it is `emo.earn_the_situation` carrying the structure with `emo.specific_detail` carrying the line.** The ground is that `emo.earn_the_situation` is the only working card on this shelf whose `Avoid when:` reads *never avoid* — every other card here has a place it stops, and this one does not; what it has instead is a price, page count, which is axis one restated. And `emo.specific_detail` is the only card the shelf routes to from two independent conditions, *stock symptoms* and *at any emotional peak*, and it is named inside three other cards' bodies as the safeguard that keeps them off `emo.sentimentality`. Build plus build, a run of room and a line of room, the feeling never once named: it is the shelf's centre of mass and it is also, exactly, the most ordinary emotional writing there is.

**The one against it is `emo.emotion_against_type`.** Nothing routes to it and it routes to nothing; the pair audit files it with no alternative at all. It is the only card here that produces the right feeling by writing the wrong one — laughing at the funeral, going practical at the disaster, asking about the rice after the word *liver*. Every other withholding card on the shelf still points the scene at the correct feeling and merely lowers the volume: `emo.understate_at_peak` says the plain line, `emo.withhold_reaction` turns the camera, `emo.emotion_through_task` gives the hands something to do, `emo.cold_anger` stores instead of spending. All four are subtractions the audience can feel being performed, and an audience that feels restraint is being managed by it. `emo.emotion_against_type` never makes the promise, so it never visibly withholds — the audience does the entire conversion themselves and believes it was theirs. Its only stated cost is frequency, *it becomes the house style*, which a single use pays outright. Its near cousin against the grain is `emo.someone_else_feels_it`, likewise with no edge in either direction, which moves the feeling off the person who owns it and gives it to whoever has the least claim to it.

### Fear Written From Inside

**Also called:** the frightened character, fear as experience, subjective fear
**What it is:** Fear rendered as the character's own experience — narrowed attention, lost vocabulary, the body acting before the mind agrees — as opposed to the audience's apprehension about what is coming.
**Effect on the audience:** Identification rather than suspense. The reader is inside a shrinking mind rather than watching a threatened body.
**Used for and where it works best:** First-person and close-third horror, war, survival, and any scene whose point is what fear does to a person rather than what the threat does. Two rules carry it. **One:** frightened people do not think about being frightened — they think about something small and wrong, so give the character one trivial, specific preoccupation at the worst moment. **Two:** fear removes syntax. Shorten the sentences, drop the subordinate clauses, let `rhythm.fragment` take over. Pairs with `char.frightened_alone` and `char.panic_behaviour`.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Feature Film, Audio Drama, Game Narrative, Comics | genres: Horror, Thriller, War, Survival, Psychological, Crime
**Avoid when:** The scene's job is audience dread rather than character experience. A fully terrified viewpoint character can *reduce* dread, because the audience outsources the worrying to them — see `tens.dread_over_shock`, which wants the opposite arrangement.
**Example:**
> **Outside:** She was terrified. Her heart hammered as the footsteps came closer down the hall.
> **Inside:** The footsteps stopped. She was thinking about the tea, which she had left on the arm of the chair, and which would leave a mark.

**Yields to:** `tens.dread_over_shock` — where the scene's job is audience dread rather than character experience. A fully terrified viewpoint character *reduces* dread, because the audience outsources the worrying to them, and the two cards want opposite arrangements of the same scene.

### Dread, Which Is Not Fear

**Also called:** anticipatory fear, objectless fear, the long approach
**What it is:** Fear has an object; dread does not yet. Dread is the emotional state of certainty that something is coming, held against complete ignorance of its shape.
**Effect on the audience:** A sustained low discomfort they cannot discharge, because there is nothing to look at. Fear peaks and resolves; dread accumulates.
**Used for and where it works best:** Long approaches — the drive to the hospital, the week before the verdict, the first act of a horror film. The craft rule is a rationing rule: the audience may know **that** and never **what**. The moment the thing has a face, dread converts irreversibly to fear and cannot be recovered in the same scene, so spend it late. It also works entirely without the supernatural — see `tens.real_condition_dread`. `tens.sustained_low_dread` is the tension-side engineering; this card is what the character's interior is doing while that machine runs.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Audio Drama, Short Story, Game Narrative | genres: Horror, Psychological, Thriller, Drama, Cosmic Horror, Medical, War
**Avoid when:** The threat is already named and present. Writing dread at someone holding a knife is a category error; that scene wants fear, and fear wants speed.
**Example:**
> **Fear:** He knew the man in the blue car was following him and would be waiting at the corner.
> **Dread:** The blue car had been outside on Tuesday as well. He had not mentioned it to anyone, because there was nothing to mention.

**Yields to:** `emo.fear_from_inside` — The threat is already named and present.

### Joy, and Why It Resists the Page

**Also called:** happiness writes white, writing contentment, the pleasure scene
**What it is:** The deliberate construction of joy or contentment as a scene, against the fact that prose and drama are both driven by want, and joy is the absence of want.
**Effect on the audience:** When it works, the strongest attachment available — audiences protect what they have enjoyed watching. When it fails, nothing at all; Montherlant's line is that happiness writes in white ink on a white page.
**Used for and where it works best:** Before a loss, at the end of a romance, and at the midpoint of anything long, where the audience needs one unit in the opposite register. Three routes work and almost nothing else does. **One: put a clock on it** — the audience knows the afternoon ends, and the joy becomes legible against its limit. **Two: give it a witness who is outside it** — joy seen by someone excluded acquires shape. **Three: write it as logistics** — who cooked, who is late, who is on the bad chair. Joy is specific activity; it is never an adjective.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Animation Feature, One-Hour Drama, Short Film, Picture Book | genres: Family Drama, Romance, Coming of Age, Heritage, Drama, Comedy, Slice of Life
**Avoid when:** You have no loss to set it against and no time limit to give it. An unbounded happy scene is the most cuttable material in any draft, which is why it is nearly always the first thing an editor removes.
**Example:**
> **White:** It was a wonderful afternoon. Everyone was happy and the sun was out and nobody wanted it to end.
> **Legible:** The ferry back was at six. Nobody said so, but by four everything anyone did was something they wanted to have done before six.

**Yields to:** `emo.joy_as_montage` — No loss and no clock; compress instead of scene.

### Envy

**Also called:** invidious comparison, writing jealousy, the measured life
**What it is:** Envy is a comparison rather than a feeling: it requires a specific other person, a specific thing they have, and the envier's private conviction that the gap is unjust.
**Effect on the audience:** Uncomfortable complicity. Envy is the emotion readers recognise fastest and admit to least, so a character who states it plainly buys enormous trust.
**Used for and where it works best:** Workplace, artistic, sibling and migration stories, and any ensemble where two characters started level. The proximity rule decides whether it lands: nobody envies a king, they envy the person one rung up who began where they began. Two further rules. **One:** envy must sit in the viewpoint, or it reads as villainy rather than as an emotion. **Two:** write the arithmetic, not the feeling — the envious mind is a calculator, and the specific sum it keeps doing is the characterisation. See `char.deserving_setting`.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, One-Hour Drama, Feature Film, Stage Play, Short Story, Limited Series | genres: Workplace, Drama, Literary, Family Drama, Satire, Social Drama, Migration
**Avoid when:** The envied character is presented as simply better. Envy needs the audience to half-agree with the envier, which requires the other person's advantage to contain something arbitrary — inheritance, luck, timing.
**Example:**
> **Named:** He was consumed with jealousy over his brother's success.
> **Calculated:** They had started the same year. He had done the harder posting, the one nobody wanted, and he had done it for four years. He could say that sentence in his head at any speed.

### Loneliness as a Written State

**Also called:** isolation on the page, unspent behaviour, writing solitude
**What it is:** Loneliness rendered as a surplus of behaviour with no recipient — habits, conversation, care and cooking produced at a scale the character's actual society cannot absorb.
**Effect on the audience:** A recognition that works even on people who have never been lonely, because the mechanism is visible in the arithmetic rather than in the mood.
**Used for and where it works best:** Migration, old age, illness, the first act of a romance, and any character living alone. The operative rule: **loneliness is not solitude, it is unspent behaviour** — food for more people than are present, a rehearsed anecdote, a routine performed with no witness. Render the environment too: what a room does when only one person uses it. And give the lonely character a plan to end it that they sabotage, or the story is a mood rather than a story.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Short Story, Stage Play, Short Film, Memoir | genres: Literary, Drama, Migration, Coming of Age, Social Drama, Psychological, Romance
**Avoid when:** The character is alone for plot reasons rather than social ones. Survival isolation is a different emotion — it is fear with a logistics problem — and treating it as loneliness flattens both.
**Example:**
> **Stated:** The flat was lonely and the silence pressed in on him every evening.
> **Enacted:** He cooks for three and freezes two. He has been doing it long enough that the freezer is now the problem, and he has started giving portions to the downstairs neighbour, who does not want them.

**Yields to:** `emo.fear_from_inside` — Survival isolation is fear with a logistics problem.

### Guilt, Which Is Not Shame

**Also called:** remorse against shame, "I did something bad" against "I am bad"
**What it is:** Guilt attaches to an act; shame attaches to the self. The two produce opposite behaviour, and a writer who conflates them produces a character whose conduct does not track.
**Effect on the audience:** Legibility. A guilty character generates plot — they confess, repair, return. An ashamed character generates interiority — they hide, lie, and remove themselves.
**Used for and where it works best:** Decide which one your character has before writing any scene in which they meet the person they wronged. **Guilt walks toward the injured party; shame walks away from them.** Guilt is therefore the engine of redemption arcs and courtroom drama; shame is the engine of addiction, secrecy and collapse. The sharpest scenes put one of each in the room. Pairs with `emo.shame`, `arc.the_need` and `char.the_secret`.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Stage Play, Memoir | genres: Drama, Crime, Redemption, Family Drama, Addiction, Psychological, Courtroom
**Avoid when:** You want the character to do both at once without deciding. They can carry both across a work, but each individual scene is driven by one, and the audience reads the behaviour, not the intention.
**Example:**
> **Guilt:** He drives to the house four times before he knocks. When she opens the door he says, "It was me who told them."
> **Shame:** He drives past the house four times and then goes home, and when she calls, he lets it ring, and then he changes the number.

`emo.guilt_vs_shame`

### Awe and the Sublime

**Also called:** the small self, vastness and accommodation, wonder
**What it is:** The emotion produced when something exceeds the categories a character has for it — psychology names its two components as *vastness* and *need for accommodation*, and its effect as the small self.
**Effect on the audience:** A momentary widening; measurably, a reduced sense of the importance of one's own concerns. It is also the only positive emotion that reliably survives being written about scale.
**Used for and where it works best:** Heritage, nature, science, faith, and any first sight of the story's big object — the city, the ship, the ruin. The rule that separates awe from adjectives: **write the failure of the category, not the size.** A character reaching for a comparison and not finding one produces awe; a narrator supplying superlatives produces none. Always keep a human-sized measuring stick in the frame — a person on the steps, a hand against the stone.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Documentary, Heritage Documentary, Novel, Animation Feature, XR | genres: Heritage, Epic, Science Fiction, Nature, Faith, Historical, Adventure
**Avoid when:** The awe is the writer's rather than the character's. A narrator who is impressed on the audience's behalf converts awe into tourism, which is the commonest defect in heritage voice-over.
**Example:**
> **Superlative:** The ziggurat rose before them, vast and magnificent, an awe-inspiring monument to a lost age.
> **Accommodation:** He had been told it was the size of a hill. It was not the size of a hill. He kept looking for the thing he could say it was the size of.

`emo.awe`

### Disgust

**Also called:** revulsion, the moral recoil, the physical turn-away
**What it is:** The recoil response, in its two forms — physical disgust at a substance or a body, and moral disgust at conduct.
**Effect on the audience:** The fastest and cheapest emotional response available, and the least durable. Physical disgust spikes and habituates within a page; moral disgust accumulates and can carry a whole work.
**Used for and where it works best:** Moral disgust is the load-bearing one — it powers satire, true crime, political drama and advocacy, and it is what lets an audience keep watching an antagonist. Use physical disgust **once per work, briefly, at low resolution**: one detail, no dwelling, and let smell carry it rather than sight (`tens.smell_before_sight`). The reliable move is to attach a small physical detail to a moral one, so the body's recoil transfers to the judgement.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Feature Film, Documentary, One-Hour Drama, Short Story, True Crime | genres: Horror, Satire, Crime, True Crime, Social Drama, Political, Dark Comedy
**Avoid when:** It is standing in for fear. Gross-out escalation reads as a work that has run out of dread — this is exactly `tens.disgust_not_fear`, and audiences clock it as a downgrade.
**Example:**
> **Physical, dwelt on:** Three paragraphs on the state of the kitchen, the bin, the smell, the flies.
> **Transferred:** The kitchen was as you would expect. What stayed with her was that he had made himself a sandwich in it, and eaten half.

`emo.disgust`

### Pride

**Also called:** the earned pride, authentic against hubristic pride, the parent's pride
**What it is:** Satisfaction in something done, or in someone belonging to you. Craft divides it in two: *authentic* pride, attached to an act, which is sympathetic; and *hubristic* pride, attached to the self, which is status defence and reads as arrogance.
**Effect on the audience:** Warmth that is almost impossible to produce any other way, because pride is the one positive emotion with a visible cause.
**Used for and where it works best:** Sports, migration, family, workplace and coming of age. One rule does most of the work: **the proud character must not claim it.** Either another person observes it, or the proud character displays it in a detail they believe is unwitnessed — the saved clipping, the phone shown to a stranger, the detour past the building. Pride is also the most reliable route into a stoic character, because it is the feeling such characters leak.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Short Film, Documentary, Heritage Documentary | genres: Sports, Family Drama, Migration, Coming of Age, Underdog, Heritage, Workplace
**Avoid when:** The achievement has not been costed. Pride in something the audience did not watch being hard is the positive twin of `emo.unearned_attachment`.
**Example:**
> **Claimed:** "I'm so proud of you. You've no idea how proud."
> **Leaked:** He tells the man at the petrol station. He tells the woman who does the accounts. He has not yet said anything to his daughter, who passed the exam on Tuesday.

`emo.pride`

### Rage That Discharges

**Also called:** hot anger, the irreversible outburst, losing it
**What it is:** Anger written as an eruption that spends itself — the counterpart to `emo.cold_anger`, and legitimate only when the discharge costs something that cannot be recovered.
**Effect on the audience:** Release followed by dread. A shouted scene is finished the moment it ends; what keeps it alive is the damage left standing.
**Used for and where it works best:** The single scene in a long work where a controlled character stops being controlled, and in the genres that contract for discharge — melodrama, farce, vertical micro-drama, some sports and family drama. Two rules. **One: fewer words, not more.** Real rage loses vocabulary; a character who becomes eloquent when furious is a writer enjoying themselves. **Two: write the sentence that cannot be taken back**, and let the audience understand before the character does that it cannot.
**Best in:** formats: Stage Play, Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Vertical Micro-Drama, Novel, Half-Hour Comedy | genres: Family Drama, Melodrama, Drama, Sports, Social Drama, Farce, Tragedy
**Avoid when:** It is the character's standing register. Shouting is characterisation the first time, noise the third time, and by the fifth the audience is managing the character rather than watching them.
**Example:**
> **Eloquent:** "You have spent thirty years making everyone in this family smaller so that you could feel large, and I am done pretending otherwise."
> **Spent:** "You're not coming. To any of it." Beat. "That's it, that's the — no. That's it."

**Yields to:** `emo.cold_anger` — Shouting has become the character's standing register.  
`emo.hot_rage`

### Anticipation and the Pleasure of Waiting

**Also called:** looking forward, the countdown to a good thing, pleasurable delay
**What it is:** The positive emotion of a known good approaching — structurally the mirror of dread, and the cheapest emotional investment a short form can buy.
**Effect on the audience:** Forward lean and a specific want. Unlike hope, which is a wish, anticipation is a *schedule*, and schedules make audiences plan alongside characters.
**Used for and where it works best:** Kids' content, weddings, homecomings, sports, seasonal work, and the whole preparation half of any heist. It needs two things: **a date the audience can name** and **a preparation ritual to watch**. Anticipation is also the fastest way to make a short piece feel structured, because the preparation supplies the middle. `tens.anticipation_vs_anxiety` is the tension-side distinction; this card is the pleasure.
**Best in:** formats: Kids Story, Preschool, Picture Book, Feature Film, Reels/Shorts, Animated Series | genres: Kids, Family Drama, Comedy, Sports, Heritage, Coming of Age, Romance
**Avoid when:** You do not intend to show the event. Cutting away from an anticipated occasion is legitimate exactly once, and only when the absence is the point; otherwise it is `end.unpaid_setup` wearing a party hat.
**Example:**
> **Vague:** She was looking forward to her aunt's visit very much.
> **Scheduled:** Her aunt comes on Saturday. It is Wednesday. The good glasses have been out since Monday and nobody is allowed to use them.

`emo.anticipation`

## Extremes and aftermath

### Numbness and Dissociation

**Also called:** flat affect, shock on the page, the absent narrator, depersonalisation
**What it is:** The state in which feeling is unavailable, rendered by **removing** material from the narration rather than by describing absence — interiority drops out, the body is reported from outside, the sentences become short and correct.
**Effect on the audience:** Unease they cannot locate, followed by the realisation that something is missing. Numbness is a hole in the text, and the reader falls into it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Immediately after catastrophe, in trauma writing, and in first-person accounts of violence or diagnosis. The technique is subtractive: slide `pov.psychic_distance_ladder` outward, stop naming feelings, describe the character's own hands as if they belonged to someone else, and let them answer questions accurately while doing nothing. Trauma writing's strongest version goes further and shifts to third person mid-passage, so the narrator is describing a woman rather than herself.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Memoir, Short Story, Feature Film, Audio Drama, Limited Series | genres: Psychological, Drama, War, Literary, Survival, Medical, Crime
**Avoid when:** For longer than one scene or chapter without relief. Numbness is the only emotional state that costs the reader attention rather than buying it, and it has a ceiling of roughly one scene before they leave.
**Example:**
> **Described:** She felt numb, as though everything were happening to someone else, far away and underwater.
> **Enacted:** They asked if there was anyone they could call. She gave them her brother's number. She gave them the old one first and then corrected it. The chairs were bolted to the floor in rows of four.

`emo.numbness`

### What Is Left After the Horror

**Also called:** survivor aftermath, the return to ordinary life, horror's residue
**What it is:** The emotional material of the period after the threat is over — not fear, not relief, but the mismatch between what happened and a world with no procedure for it.
**Effect on the audience:** The sense that the story was real. Horror that ends at the escape is a ride; horror that shows the following week is an experience.
**Used for and where it works best:** Final acts and codas in horror, war, crime and disaster, and in any work whose subject is survival rather than danger. The rule: **the survivor's problem is administrative, not psychological.** Write the form they have to fill in, the people being normal at them, the wrong condolences, the insurance call. The mismatch does the emotional work and needs no interiority at all. `tens.aftermath_not_act` is the tension-side use of the same principle; `end.horror_coda` is the ending shape.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Short Film, Audio Drama, Documentary | genres: Horror, War, Survival, Crime, Disaster, Drama, Psychological
**Avoid when:** The genre contract is the ride. A ninety-minute creature feature that stops for aftermath has broken its own promise; put the residue in one shot, not one act.
**Example:**
> **Interior:** She would never be the same again. The memory of that night would haunt her for the rest of her life.
> **Administrative:** The insurance form has a box for "cause of damage to property" that allows forty characters. She has had it open for two days.

**Yields to:** `end.horror_coda` — Genre contract is the ride; one shot, not one act.  
`emo.horror_aftermath`

### Laughter, Then Grief

**Also called:** the comic run before the loss, the last good afternoon, laugh-then-cut
**What it is:** A genuinely funny sequence deliberately sited immediately before the loss it makes unbearable, using the same people in the same room.
**Effect on the audience:** The single strongest emotional structure available in drama. An audience that has just laughed is unarmoured, and grief arriving into an open posture lands about twice as hard.
**Used for and where it works best:** Any death, diagnosis or departure in a work with a comic register available. Three rules make it work rather than merely jolt. **One: same people, same room** — the laugh must belong to the world that is about to be damaged. **Two: the joke must not be about the coming loss**, or it becomes foreshadowing and the audience re-arms. **Three: the gap is short** — thirty seconds to two minutes; beyond that they rebuild the guard. See `comedy.comedy_in_tragedy` and `tens.laugh_raises_ceiling`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, One-Hour Drama, Novel, Stage Play, Limited Series, Short Film | genres: Family Drama, Drama, Dark Comedy, War, Tragedy, Medical, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The laugh is the writer's rather than the family's. A joke only the audience can hear produces `voice.tonal_whiplash` instead of this card — the test is whether a character in the room laughed too.
**Example:**
> Four of them arguing about whether the oven has ever worked. Nobody can remember it working. Somebody claims to have baked a cake in it in 2009 and nobody believes her and she cannot produce a witness.
> Then the phone, and her father's voice saying her full name, which he has not used since she was a child.

`emo.laugh_then_grief`

### Humiliation as a Comic Engine

**Also called:** cringe comedy, vicarious embarrassment, the awkwardness engine
**What it is:** Comedy generated by sustained social humiliation, where the laugh comes from the audience's own physical discomfort rather than from wit.
**Effect on the audience:** A measurable bodily response — vicarious embarrassment produces close to the physiology of being humiliated oneself, which is why a substantial minority of any audience cannot watch it at all.
**Used for and where it works best:** Half-hour comedy, mockumentary, sketch and vertical drama. Two settings exist and you pick one per scene: **oblivious**, where the character does not know (comfortable, repeatable, sympathetic), and **aware**, where they do (excruciating, and spendable roughly once per episode). The structural requirement is a gap between the character's self-image and the room's reading of them, plus an audience positioned to see both. Every cringe sequence also needs a **visible exit** — a door, an interruption, an ally — so the audience can endure the middle. See `comedy.cringe` and `tens.escalating_humiliation`.
**Best in:** formats: Half-Hour Comedy, Web Series, Sketch, Vertical Micro-Drama, Stage Play, Reels/Shorts | genres: Comedy, Satire, Dark Comedy, Workplace, Social Drama, Coming of Age
**Avoid when:** The character is the audience's point of attachment and there is no relief in sight. It stops being comedy and becomes an endurance test — see `emo.cringe_without_exit` and `comedy.sympathy_cost_joke`.
**Example:**
> **Aware:** He realises, mid-toast, that the man he is describing as "the least reliable person any of us know" is standing behind him. He does not stop. He gets slower, and he finishes it.
> **Oblivious:** He gives the same toast, does not realise, sits down pleased, and asks his wife whether it went well. She says it went well.

`emo.humiliation_comedy`

## Emotion at length

### The Emotional Arc of a Series

**Also called:** the season's feeling, emotional serialisation, the register map
**What it is:** Planning a season or a novel sequence around one emotional question rather than only one plot question, and assigning each episode a distinct register so the whole has a shape.
**Effect on the audience:** The reason people describe a season as "about" something they cannot summarise. Plot makes them return; emotional shape makes them recommend.
**Used for and where it works best:** Before writing episode two. Write the season's emotional question as a sentence with a person in it — *can she be in the same room as her mother* — not as a theme. Then map the register per episode and look for the standard defect: a flat middle where four consecutive episodes are all sad. **Reserve at least one episode per season for the opposite register**; a comedy season needs its grief episode and a bleak season needs its wedding. `tens.season_long_pressure` is the tension-side map, `rel.relationship_arc` the relationship-side, and `end.season_ending` is where it is paid.
**Best in:** formats: Series, Limited Series, One-Hour Drama, Serialised Fiction, Animated Series, Podcast | genres: Drama, Family Drama, Crime, Medical, Workplace, Social Drama, Coming of Age
**Avoid when:** The format resets by contract. A procedural or a preschool series maintains an emotional baseline on purpose, and imposing an arc on it breaks the thing the audience came for — see `char.protected_static_role`.
**Example:**
> **Plot question:** Will the restaurant survive the year?
> **Emotional question:** Will he ever let his brother be better at something than he is?
> Episode 4 is the one where nobody argues, and it is the episode the audience will name.

**Yields to:** `char.protected_static_role` — on a format that resets by contract. A procedural or a preschool series holds its emotional baseline on purpose, and an arc imposed there breaks the thing the audience came for.  
`emo.series_arc`

### The Emotional Shape of One Episode

**Also called:** the episode's feeling, per-episode register, the emotional through-line of a chapter
**What it is:** Giving a single episode or chapter one emotion it is about, with its own small turn, even when it sits inside a heavily serialised season.
**Effect on the audience:** Satisfaction from a unit rather than from a fragment. It is the difference between an episode and forty-five minutes of a long thing.
**Used for and where it works best:** Streaming drama, where serialisation has weakened the episode as a unit, and novels whose chapters have stopped ending. The method: name the episode's emotion in one word, then find the scene where it turns — use `scene.value_turn` with that emotion as the value. Place the emotional trough around two-thirds in rather than at the end (`tens.trough_placement`), and let the last beat belong to the emotion rather than to the plot cliffhanger.
**Best in:** formats: Series, One-Hour Drama, Limited Series, Half-Hour Comedy, Serialised Fiction, Podcast | genres: Drama, Family Drama, Crime, Workplace, Medical, Comedy
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-06. Episode length and the binge-release convention set how much emotional shape a single unit is expected to carry, and both have moved twice in five years.
**Avoid when:** The episode is genuinely a bridge and the series is short. Four- and six-part series can run one emotion across two episodes; eight and above cannot.
**Example:**
> Episode 6 is about embarrassment. The A-plot is a funding meeting, the B-plot is a school concert, and the turn is at minute 31, when she stops apologising in the meeting — because the concert scene has already taught her how.

**Yields to:** `emo.series_arc` — Short series; one emotion may span two episodes.  
`emo.episode_emotional_shape`

### What Catharsis Actually Requires

**Also called:** pity and fear, the earned discharge, tragic release
**What it is:** The structural preconditions for the emotional discharge Aristotle named — not a strong ending, but the release of something the audience has been carrying for the length of the work.
**Effect on the audience:** Physical release, then calm. It is the only ending effect audiences describe in bodily terms, and the one they misattribute to the last scene when it was built in the first act.
**Used for and where it works best:** Tragedy, and any long work whose ending is being described in notes as "sad but not moving". Three conditions, all met before the final act. **One: a morally mixed protagonist** — neither blameless nor villainous, because pity requires undeserving and fear requires resemblance. **Two: a foreseen outcome** — the audience must have seen it coming and hoped against it; surprise blocks catharsis, because nothing was carried. **Three: recognition** — a moment where the character sees what the audience already saw. `end.catharsis` is the ending-side card; this one is the entry requirement.
**Best in:** formats: Stage Play, Feature Film, Novel, Limited Series, Musical Theatre, Audio Drama | genres: Tragedy, Drama, War, Family Drama, Historical, Literary, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The ending turns on a twist. A twist ending and a cathartic ending are incompatible by construction, and attempting both produces an audience that is impressed and unmoved.
**Example:**
> **Blocked:** The audience learns in the final scene that he had been ill all along. They are surprised. They are not moved, because they were carrying nothing.
> **Discharged:** The audience has known since minute twenty. They spend the film watching everyone else find out, and the release comes when he finally says it and is told, gently, that they knew.

**Yields to:** `end.twist_ending` — The ending turns on a twist; incompatible by construction.  
`emo.catharsis_requirements`

### The Withheld Catharsis

**Also called:** anti-cathartic ending, the Brechtian refusal, the unreleased audience
**What it is:** Building the full machinery of emotional discharge and deliberately not firing it, so the feeling stays with the audience after the work ends instead of being spent inside it.
**Effect on the audience:** Discomfort that persists. Brecht's argument was that release completes the transaction and sends the audience home settled — fine for tragedy, fatal for a work that wants them to do something.
**Used for and where it works best:** Advocacy, political theatre, social drama, testimony and any documentary whose subject is ongoing. Two conditions make it work rather than merely disappoint. **One: the machinery must be visible** — the audience has to feel the ending they were denied, which means building it properly first. **Two: substitute something** — a direct address, an unanswered question, a task, a name. Pairs with `end.open_ending` and `end.documentary_ending`.
**Best in:** formats: Stage Play, Documentary, Essay Film, Limited Series, Immersive, Short Film | genres: Political, Social Drama, Advocacy, Testimony, Journalism, Migration, Arthouse
**Avoid when:** The audience has paid for release. Withholding from a genre that contracts for catharsis is not a statement, it is `end.genre_contract_broken`. And withholding while offering nothing in its place is just an unfinished ending.
**Example:**
> **Released:** The verdict is read. The family embraces on the courthouse steps. Cut to black.
> **Withheld:** The verdict is read. We stay in the corridor while the next family is called in, and the film ends on them going through the doors.

**Yields to:** `emo.catharsis_requirements` — The audience has paid for release.  
`emo.withheld_catharsis`

## Performance, the page and the song

### Leave the Emotion for the Performer

**Also called:** writing playable action, actionable not emotional, giving the actor a verb
**What it is:** Writing a scripted emotional scene as a set of *wants and obstacles* a performer can play, instead of as emotional states, which are not playable.
**Effect on the audience:** Performances that look discovered rather than instructed. Acting craft is unanimous on the mechanism: an actor told to be sad has nothing to do; an actor trying to get someone to stay produces sadness as a by-product.
**Used for and where it works best:** Every screenplay and stage script, and the first repair when a performer calls a scene unplayable. Method: for each emotional beat name the transitive verb — *to reassure, to trap, to get forgiven, to be let off* — and write the line as an attempt at it. Then strip: cut emotional adverbs, cut parentheticals (`sub.no_parentheticals`), and leave one gap you do not fill, because the actor will put the emotion there. Voice-over is the same discipline measured in breath — see `rhythm.vo_breath_line`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Stage Play, One-Hour Drama, Short Film, Audio Drama, Monologue | genres: Drama, Family Drama, Tragedy, Romance, Social Drama, Psychological
**Avoid when:** Prose. There is no performer, so interiority you withheld is simply missing — the prose equivalent is a different decision entirely, and it is the next card.
**Example:**
> **Unplayable:**
> ANNA *(devastated, fighting back tears)*: I can't believe you'd do this to me.
> **Playable:**
> ANNA: Have you eaten?
> DAVID: Anna —
> ANNA: Because there's some left, and it doesn't keep.

**Yields to:** `emo.page_only_interiority` — in prose, where there is no performer, so interiority withheld is simply missing. The prose decision is a different one entirely.  
`emo.leave_it_for_the_performer`

### The Emotion Only Prose Can Deliver

**Also called:** interior emotion, the self-deceiving narrator, page-only feeling
**What it is:** The emotional effects that depend on simultaneous access to what a character says, what they do, and what they tell themselves — available in prose and in almost nothing else.
**Effect on the audience:** A doubled reading. The reader holds the character's account and the truth at once, and the gap between them is the feeling.
**Used for and where it works best:** Novels, memoir and short fiction, using `pov.close_third` and `pov.free_indirect_style`. The instrument is the **unreliable interior**: the narration states a feeling the reader can see is standing in for another one. Screen cannot do this without voice-over, which is why it is what most often disappears in adaptation. **The adaptation rule: for every interior emotional beat, budget a behavioural substitute before you start, not during the third draft.** `pov.screen_no_interiority` is the constraint from the other side.
**Best in:** formats: Novel, Short Story, Memoir, Novella, Serialised Fiction, Audiobook | genres: Literary, Psychological, Drama, Romance, Coming of Age, Family Drama
**Avoid when:** The narration's self-deception is not signposted at all. Subtext nobody can find is `sub.buried_subtext`, and an interior that is merely wrong without evidence reads as a writer who has lost track of the character.
**Example:**
> He was relieved, mostly. It had been a long time coming and there was no sense pretending otherwise, and he found he was quite calm about it, and he went out for the paper at half past nine, and again at eleven, and once more after lunch.

**Yields to:** `emo.leave_it_for_the_performer` — Screen or stage; budget a behavioural substitute.  
`emo.page_only_interiority`

### The Emotional Beat of a Lyric and Its Picture

**Also called:** song emotional mapping, cutting to the arrangement, lyric beat sheet
**What it is:** Treating a song's existing structure — verse, pre-chorus, chorus, bridge, last chorus — as a finished emotional map, and writing the words or the pictures onto it rather than against it.
**Effect on the audience:** Inevitability. A music video or lyric that turns where the song turns feels composed; one that turns elsewhere feels like two works playing at once.
**Used for and where it works best:** Music video, lyric video, musical theatre and song-scored sequences. The map is fixed and reliable: the **pre-chorus lifts** (`lyric.prechorus_lift`), the **chorus states**, the **bridge is the only place the emotional position may change** (`lyric.bridge_turn`), and the **last chorus is where exactly one thing is different** (`end.last_chorus`). So: put the story's turn on the bridge, put the reveal on the last chorus, and never put a plot event on a chorus, which is a statement and not an event. For kids' songs the contract inverts — repetition is the pleasure (`lyric.kids_repetition_contract`).
**Best in:** formats: Music Video, Lyric Video, Song Lyric, Musical Theatre, Reels/Shorts, Animated Series | genres: Musical, Romance, Kids, Heritage, Drama, Coming of Age
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-03. Where a video's emotional hook must land, and how much of a song a platform will play before drop-off, are both set by current short-form convention.
**Avoid when:** The song is through-composed or has no chorus (`lyric.through_composed`). Then there is no map to borrow and the picture must supply its own shape.
**Example:**
> **Fighting the song:** the reunion happens in verse two, and the chorus arrives on people walking down a corridor.
> **On the map:** verse two is the search; the bridge is where she stops searching; the last chorus is the same street as the first chorus, with one extra person in it.

**Yields to:** `lyric.through_composed` — Song is through-composed or has no chorus.  
`emo.song_emotional_beat`

## Commissioned and interactive work

### Emotion in Advertising and Brand Work

**Also called:** emotional brand building, the feeling sell, long-term advertising effect
**What it is:** The construction of a single emotional response in a commercial film, on the evidence that emotionally-led campaigns outperform rationally-led ones across every brand metric and produce roughly twice the long-term profit effect.
**Effect on the audience:** Attention, memory and sharing — the three mechanisms the effectiveness literature credits for the gap. Rational claims are evaluated; emotional ones are stored.
**Used for and where it works best:** Brand films, PSAs and anything whose payback is longer than a quarter. Three rules separate the ones that work. **One: one emotion per film**, named on the brief in a single word. **Two: the emotion must come from the category's real friction** — the thing the product's users actually put up with — not from a borrowed occasion. **Three: the brand must cause the turn, not sponsor it**; if the film's emotion survives with the logo removed and a competitor's added, it is not the client's film. See `voice.advertising_register`, `voice.brand_voice_intake` and `end.brand_film_close`.
**Best in:** formats: Advertising, Brand Film, PSA, Product Video, Trailer, Reels/Shorts | genres: Lifestyle, Advocacy, Family Drama, Comedy, Social Drama, Sports
**Shelf life:** dated — review 2027-03. Ad lengths, skip windows and the platforms' first-seconds conventions decide how much emotional runway exists, and they move every year — see `tens.first_five_seconds`.
**Avoid when:** The brief is a price or a feature. An emotional wrapper on a promotional message produces the worst available result: the audience feels the manipulation and does not receive the offer.
**Example:**
> **Borrowed:** Grandfather and grandchild at Christmas. Snow. Piano. A supermarket logo.
> **From the friction:** The film is about the fifteen minutes before visiting hours end, because the product is a phone tariff and that is the only thing anyone in that corridor cares about.

**Yields to:** `voice.advertising_register` — The brief is a price or a feature.  
`emo.brand_emotion`

### Emotion in Kids' and Preschool Content

**Also called:** emotional literacy writing, feelings-have-names content, social-emotional storytelling
**What it is:** Writing emotion for children as a teachable, nameable object — the reverse of the adult craft rule — because the deliverable is the child's ability to identify the feeling, not the audience's experience of it.
**Effect on the audience:** Recognition and vocabulary. The child gains a word for a state they already had, which is the whole product.
**Used for and where it works best:** Preschool and younger kids' content, where the standard emotional-literacy shape is three beats: **name it, normalise it, give a usable action.** This makes `emo.telling_the_emotion` legitimate here and almost nowhere else — the feeling is stated plainly, by the character, in the first person. Three further rules. **The child character feels it, and the adult does not fix it in one line.** The resolution must be one a child can carry out of the room (`end.child_carry_ending`). And the difficulty belongs to the child's scale — a lost toy, not a parent's job. Subtext still exists but must be readable at that age (`sub.kid_readable_subtext`); see also `voice.kids_register` and `comedy.kids_comedy`.
**Best in:** formats: Preschool, Kids Story, Picture Book, Animated Series, Kids Song, Educational | genres: Kids, Preschool, Family Drama, Educational, Fable, Slice of Life
**Avoid when:** The content is for middle grade or above. From roughly eight, named feelings read as being talked down to and the adult rules resume — the same story then wants behaviour, not labels.
**Example:**
> **Adult register, wrong here:** She said nothing and put the broken piece in her pocket.
> **Preschool register:** "I feel sad. My bird broke." — "It is okay to feel sad about a broken thing. Shall we hold it together while we look for the glue?"

**Yields to:** `emo.emotion_through_task` — Middle grade and above; behaviour, not labels.  
`emo.kids_emotion`

### Emotion When the Audience Acts

**Also called:** interactive emotion, player-authored feeling, emotion under agency
**What it is:** Emotion in work where the audience takes the actions, which changes which emotions are available, because the player's authorship is now part of the cause.
**Effect on the audience:** Where it works, the strongest attachment any medium produces, because the feeling is about something they did. Where it fails, it fails harder than in linear work, because the player can feel that they were steered.
**Used for and where it works best:** Games, interactive fiction, visual novels and immersive work. The palette differs from linear work: **guilt, pride and regret are the interactive-native emotions**, because each requires authorship and none can be fully produced by watching. **Grief is the hard one** — a player's instinct on meeting loss is to look for the solution, so grief requires a point where the work itself refuses, and the refusal must be honest (a choice with no good option, `inter.no_good_option`) rather than a fake branch. Delay the emotional consequence so it cannot be undone (`inter.delayed_consequence`), then check the result against `inter.ludonarrative_dissonance`.
**Best in:** formats: Game Narrative, Interactive Fiction, Visual Novel, XR, Immersive, Conversational Agent | genres: Drama, Psychological, Survival, Horror, Science Fiction, Adventure, Coming of Age
**Avoid when:** The emotional beat requires the player to be powerless and you have given them a button. An emotional cutscene in a game that has trained agency reads as the work taking the controller away, which produces irritation exactly where you wanted grief.
**Example:**
> **Steered:** A cutscene in which the character you have been protecting dies while you watch.
> **Authored:** Two doors, both openable, and you can only reach one in time. The game never tells you afterwards which was the right one, and on a second run the timing is identical.

**Yields to:** `inter.no_good_option` — Beat needs powerlessness in a game that trained agency.  
`emo.interactive_emotion`

### Emotion in Documentary and Testimony

**Also called:** the interview's feeling, testimony craft, real-subject emotion
**What it is:** Handling emotion when the person is real and the suffering is theirs — where the craft question and the ethical question turn out to be the same question.
**Effect on the audience:** Trust, which in documentary is the whole currency. An audience that senses a subject was worked on stops believing the film, including its facts.
**Used for and where it works best:** Interview documentary, heritage work, reportage and true crime. The reliable sequence is **attachment first, hard material second** — name, room, job, one ordinary competence — because a stranger's tears are information and a person's tears are an event (`emo.unearned_attachment` is the fiction-side statement of the same law). Then: **do not cut in on the tears, cut away** (`emo.withhold_reaction`); **show the person doing something other than suffering**, or the film has reduced them to their worst week; and treat continuous consent and aftercare as part of the edit rather than as paperwork that preceded it. Pairs with `pov.reportage_stance` and `end.documentary_ending`.
**Best in:** formats: Documentary, Documentary Interview, Heritage Documentary, Reportage, Podcast, Documentary Series | genres: Testimony, Journalism, Heritage, Advocacy, Social Drama, Migration, True Crime
**Avoid when:** The emotional moment is the only reason the person is in the film. That is the failure below, and it is visible on screen more often than editors believe.
**Example:**
> **Extractive:** The question is repeated four ways until she cries, and the lens pushes in.
> **Earned:** Twelve minutes on how the bakery worked, who came in at what hour, which oven was unreliable. Then one question about 2014, and the camera stays exactly where it is.

`emo.testimony_emotion`

### Cultural Registers of Emotional Display

**Also called:** display rules, emotional decorum, who may cry in front of whom
**What it is:** Writing emotion according to the rules a specific culture, family or profession has about *displaying* it — which vary enormously — while treating the experience of the emotion as shared.
**Effect on the audience:** Characters who behave like people from somewhere rather than like people from a screenwriting manual.
**Used for and where it works best:** Heritage, migration and any cross-cultural scene. The underlying research is stable and useful: the private experience of emotion varies little between cultures; the **display** varies with who is present. Collectivist display rules suppress negative expression in company and amplify positive expression, and the same person alone behaves differently. The practical instruction: **decide who is in the room before deciding what the character shows.** In high-context settings the emotional information is not in the face at all — it is in a **deviation from ritual**, and the audience reads the deviation (`sub.high_context_indirection`). Professions have display rules too: doctors, soldiers and clergy are each their own culture for this purpose. See `char.inside_outside_culture`.
**Best in:** formats: Feature Film, Novel, Heritage Documentary, One-Hour Drama, Stage Play, Short Film | genres: Heritage, Migration, Family Drama, War, Faith, Historical, Social Drama
**Avoid when:** The restraint has not been established as a rule. Composure only reads as effort if the audience has already seen the rule operating on somebody else; otherwise it reads as a character who does not care.
**Example:**
> At the funeral the widow is composed, greets everyone, and serves. The tell is that she pours the coffee before the water, which she has never once done, and three women in the room notice at the same moment.

`emo.cultural_display_register`

## Failure modes

### Joy Delegated to a Montage

**Also called:** the happiness montage, the good-times sequence
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A period of happiness delivered as a compressed run of pleasant images over music, because no scene was written for it.
**Effect on the audience:** Nothing — and, worse, the loss of the loss. The montage is a receipt for joy rather than joy, so when the story takes it away, the audience has nothing to be deprived of.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere as a substitute for the scene. Legitimate where the montage itself turns (`scene.montage_value_turn`), or where compression is the point and the emotion is sited elsewhere.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (worst in feature film and romance, where the montage is almost always covering for a missing second-act relationship scene)
**Avoid when:** Any time the joy is load-bearing for a later loss. The repair is one ordinary scene with a clock on it — see `emo.joy`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** Ninety seconds of the two of them at markets, on bicycles, laughing in a kitchen, over a licensed track.
> **Working:** One scene. They are assembling a piece of furniture badly, the instructions are in the wrong language, and neither of them wants to be the one who suggests stopping.

**Yields to:** `emo.joy` — wherever the joy is load-bearing for a later loss. The repair is one ordinary scene with a clock on it rather than a sequence.  
`emo.joy_as_montage`

### Humiliation With No Way Out

**Also called:** the endurance test, cringe without relief, sustained mortification
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** An escalating humiliation of a sympathetic character with no visible exit, no ally and no interruption, sustained past the point where an audience can still find it funny.
**Effect on the audience:** They leave — literally, in streaming data, and emotionally in a room. Vicarious embarrassment is a genuine physiological response, so this is a physical limit rather than a matter of taste.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere in comedy. It is legitimate in drama, where the humiliation is the injury and is meant to be unbearable — but then it is not being played for laughs and must not be scored as though it were.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (most damaging in half-hour comedy and mockumentary, whose whole engine this is when calibrated and whose whole audience it loses when it is not)
**Avoid when:** The character is the audience's point of attachment. The repair is cheap: give the scene a door the audience can see, or an ally who knows, or a cut. See `emo.humiliation_comedy` and `comedy.when_to_stop`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** Six minutes of the presentation going wrong, alone, with no cutaway and nobody on his side.
> **Working:** The same six minutes, and at minute two we see his colleague quietly get up and go to find the technician.

**Yields to:** `emo.humiliation_comedy` and `comedy.when_to_stop` — where the character is the audience's point of attachment. The repair is cheap: a door the audience can see, an ally who knows, or a cut.  
`emo.cringe_without_exit`

### Borrowed Emotion

**Also called:** the hijacked feeling, emotional free-riding, the unrelated occasion
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** Attaching a work — usually commercial — to a feeling it has no relationship with: a national event, a season, a bereavement, a returning soldier, used because the emotion is pre-loaded in the audience.
**Effect on the audience:** A short-lived response followed by a durable suspicion, which attaches to the brand rather than to the beat. Advertising pre-testing catches the response and misses the residue.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere. It is distinct from the legitimate case, which is a work whose subject genuinely *is* the occasion — the test is whether the thing being sold or said has any causal relationship to the feeling being raised.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (endemic in advertising, brand film and PSA work, and increasingly in short vertical video, where a borrowed audio track performs the same trick in three seconds)
**Avoid when:** Always, and hardest to resist when the client has asked for it by name. The repair is `emo.brand_emotion`: find the category's own friction, which always exists and is always more specific.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** A soft-focus film about a soldier coming home, ninety seconds long, for a mid-range hatchback.
> **Working:** A film about the fifteen minutes a driving instructor spends waiting outside a test centre, for the same car.

**Yields to:** `emo.brand_emotion` — always, and hardest to resist when the client asks for the borrowed feeling by name. Find the category's own friction, which always exists and is always more specific.  
`emo.borrowed_feeling`

### Emotion Delegated to the Music

**Also called:** letting the score do it, the emotional cue, scoring the gap
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** A scene whose emotional content exists only in the temp track — the writing supplies a situation, the music supplies the feeling, and nobody notices until the music is taken away.
**Effect on the audience:** A managed response rather than a felt one, with a distinctive after-taste: audiences call such scenes manipulative without being able to say why, because the instruction was audible.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere as a replacement. Music legitimately *amplifies* an emotion the scene already produces, and legitimately carries montage and title sequences where the writing has deliberately stepped back.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (commonest in brand film, trailers and documentary, and hardest to detect in the edit because the temp track is usually present from the first assembly)
**Avoid when:** Always in drama. The diagnostic is mechanical and reliable: **play the cut with the sound off.** If nothing happens, the scene is not written. The repair is `emo.earn_the_situation`, never a different track.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** She walks out of the building and along the river, four minutes, strings.
> **Working:** She walks out of the building and along the river, and at the bridge she takes out her phone, looks at it, and puts it back without unlocking it. Then the strings, if you still want them.

**Yields to:** `emo.earn_the_situation` — always in drama, and never a different track. The diagnostic is mechanical: **play the cut with the sound off.** If nothing happens, the scene is not written.  
`emo.score_does_the_work`

### Emotion Extracted From a Real Person

**Also called:** the pushed interview, the breakdown shot, testimony mining
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** Working a documentary subject until they break — repeating a question, returning to the wound for a better take, pushing in as they cry — and treating the resulting footage as the film's emotional achievement.
**Effect on the audience:** They see it. Audiences read interview pressure with surprising accuracy, and the moment they do, every factual claim in the film is downgraded with it.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere. It is separable from the legitimate case: a subject who chooses to go there, in their own time, with the option of stopping, filmed by someone who has already earned the room.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (worst in interview documentary, heritage documentary and true crime, and worst of all where the subject is recently bereaved and the interviewer has a delivery date)
**Avoid when:** Always, and the discipline belongs to production rather than to the edit — a shot you did not take cannot be cut in at three in the morning. See `emo.testimony_emotion` for the working method.
**Example:**
> **Failing:** "And how did that make you feel?" — asked for the fourth time, with the lens moving in on the pause.
> **Working:** "We can stop." — and then eleven seconds of nothing, and she starts again on her own.

**Yields to:** `emo.testimony_emotion` — always, and the discipline belongs to production rather than the edit: a shot you did not take cannot be cut in at three in the morning.  
`emo.extracted_emotion`

### The Emotion Written as a Stage Direction

**Also called:** the emotional parenthetical, directing from the page, adverbial acting
**This is a named failure, not a recommendation.**
**What it is:** Specifying the feeling in the script's instructions — *(devastated)*, *(with quiet fury)*, *she said, heartbroken* — in place of writing a scene that produces it.
**Effect on the audience:** None, because they never see the instruction. Its real victims are the performer, handed an unplayable result instead of an action, and the writer, who now believes the scene works.
**Used for and where it works best:** Nowhere in a shooting script. Narrowly legitimate where a line's meaning genuinely inverts and nothing in the text can signal it — *(meaning it)* after a run of sarcasm — and in radio and audio scripts, where the performer has no physical context to read.
**Best in:** formats: — | genres: — (a specific plague of feature and one-hour drama first drafts, and the single most common note actors give at a table read)
**Avoid when:** Always at an emotional peak. The diagnostic: delete every parenthetical and adverb in the scene and read it. Whatever stops working was never in the scene. The repair is `emo.leave_it_for_the_performer` and `sub.no_parentheticals`.
**Example:**
> **Failing:**
> MARWAN *(voice breaking, unable to look at her)*: You should have told me.
> **Working:**
> MARWAN: You should have told me.
> He picks up the plates. He takes them to the sink. He comes back for the glasses, one at a time.

**Yields to:** `emo.leave_it_for_the_performer` and `sub.no_parentheticals` — at any emotional peak. The diagnostic: delete every parenthetical and adverb in the scene and read it; whatever stops working was never in the scene.  
`emo.emotion_written_as_direction`
