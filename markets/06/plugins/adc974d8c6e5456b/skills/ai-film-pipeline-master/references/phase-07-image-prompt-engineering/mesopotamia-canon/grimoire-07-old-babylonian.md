## 7. The Old Babylonian Empire (Hammurabi's Era, approx. 1894-1595 BC)

Here is the visual lore breakdown for The Old Babylonian Empire (Hammurabi's Era, approx. 1894 BC - 1595 BC). Prepare your team for a world of deep bureaucracy, Amorite influence, and striking terracotta art. The visual palette here shifts from the stark Akkadian contrasts to warmer, richer ochres, heavily regulated urban spaces, and the gleaming black basalt of law.

### 1. Environment, Flora & Fauna

- Weather & Sky: The sky is a fierce, cloudless azure, often choked with low-hanging yellow dust from the plains. The sun casts a heavy, golden hour glow that dominates the visual mood.

- Terrain & Hydrology: The Euphrates is heavily managed, forming a massive, sluggish, olive-brown artery. The surrounding land is a patchwork of long, canal-fed plots - rectangles, right trapezoids and right triangles fitted to the watercourse and to old property lines, not a ruled checkerboard of equal squares. (Source: D. F. Mansfield, "Si.427: A Late Old Babylonian Field Plan," Foundations of Science, 2021, tablet Si.427, Istanbul Archaeological Museums, Old Babylonian period; and University of Pennsylvania Museum, "An Ancient Babylonian Map," The Museum Journal 7, 1916, penn.museum/sites/journal/530, checked 2026-09-23.)

- Flora: Alongside the ubiquitous date palms, vast fields of pale green, broad-leafed sesame plants wave in the heat. The air smells of roasted sesame and damp river mud.

- Fauna: Flocks of heavy-fleeced sheep and dark-haired goats dominate the pastures. A massive visual shift: the very first, rare, small-statured horses - referred to as "asses of the mountains" - begin to appear, distinguished by shorter ears and flowing, coarse manes.

### 2. Architecture & Infrastructure

- City Layout: Babylon is orderly but dense. Streets are narrow, shadowed canyons of packed dirt, winding between towering, two-story domestic houses with central, open-to-the-sky courtyards.

- Megaprojects (Palaces): Look to the nearby contemporary Palace of Mari for the architectural standard. Walls are immensely thick mudbrick, keeping the interiors cool and dim.

- Materials & Colors: The interiors of elite buildings explode with color. Walls are coated in bright, matte-white gypsum plaster and painted with vibrant, life-sized frescoes. The palette relies on bold, flat pigments: deep charcoal black, hematite red, and a striking, powdery Egyptian blue.

- Details: Courtyards feature baked-brick paving. Doorways are arched, and wooden doors are secured with complex wooden lock-and-pin mechanisms, a hallmark of Babylonian paranoia and property rights.

### 3. Demographics & Physical Traits

- Facial Features: The Amorite lineage of the ruling class introduces slightly different facial structures - prominent cheekbones, strong, curved noses, and deep-set eyes outlined in thick kohl.
- Skin Tones: Rich, warm bronze and olive.
- Hair & Beards:
  - Men: The rigid, boxy Akkadian beard relaxes slightly. Beards are full, oiled, and shaped into soft, rounded curves rather than sharp, rectangular tiers.

  - Women: Hair is incredibly elaborate, often gathered into heavy, dark nets or cauls at the back of the head, or worn in thick, twin braids resting heavily over the front of the shoulders.

### 4. Fashion & Textiles

- The Rolled Hem: The defining silhouette of the Old Babylonian elite. Men wear long, sweeping, draped garments made of fine, heavy wool. The key visual is the edge of the fabric: a thick, padded, deeply rolled hem that crosses diagonally over the chest and cascades down the leg.

- Headgear: Hammurabi and his contemporaries are instantly recognizable by their hats - a distinct, deeply brimmed, rounded "mushroom-shaped" cap pulled low over the forehead, made of stiffened felt or tightly woven wool.
- Colors & Jewelry: Garments are deeply dyed. We see mustard yellows, rich terracottas, and earthy browns. Jewelry remains heavy with gold, lapis, and carnelian, but features more intricate granulation (tiny gold beads fused to the surface).

### 5. Economy & Daily Life

- The Bureaucracy: The visual of daily life is the clay tablet. Scribes sit cross-legged in the dust, furiously impressing wedges into wet, gray-brown clay. A signature visual of this era, though not unique to it (`meso.world.economy_daily_life_6` shows the same envelope among the Old Assyrian merchants): "clay envelopes." Important tablets are sealed inside a hollow, secondary layer of clay to prevent tampering.

- The Tavern: A key social hub, often run by female "alewives." Dimly lit, smelling of sour, fermenting barley. Large, porous terracotta vats sweat moisture in the corners. Patrons drink thick, unfiltered beer through long, curved reeds (often tipped with bronze or gold to filter out the floating grain husks).

- Currency: Hacksilver is standard, weighed against precisely carved, gleaming black hematite weights shaped like sleeping ducks or heavy, stylized lions.

### 6. Religion & Art

- The Stele of Law: The ultimate visual reference is the Stele of Hammurabi. It is an imposing, 7.5-foot-tall slab of deep, polished black diorite/basalt. It catches the light with a smooth, mirror-like sheen. At the top, a crisp bas-relief shows the king standing before the sun god, Shamash, flames radiating sharply from the god's shoulders.

- Terracotta Plaques: Mass-produced religious art appears. Homes are filled with small, hand-sized, baked clay plaques (the color of burnt orange or pale beige) depicting protective deities, naked goddesses, or domestic scenes, often hastily painted with washes of red ochre and black.
- Symbols: The spade (marru) of the god Marduk begins to appear as a dominant visual icon, alongside the rod-and-ring of divine justice.

### 7. Transport & Mobility

- Watercraft: Similar to previous eras, but the river traffic is more regulated. Toll stations dot the canals. Heavy timber barges lashed with coarse rope are towed upstream by teams of men or donkeys walking along the muddy banks.

- Land Vehicles: Two-wheeled, solid-wood carts pulled by donkeys remain the primary logistics vehicle. The newly introduced horses are too precious for heavy labor and are ridden bareback by dispatch riders using simple, ring-snaffle bits.

### 8. The Military Machine

- The Ilkum System: The army is mostly conscripted farmers fulfilling their state duties. They look more standardized than Sumerian militias, but lack the terrifying, professional uniformity of later Assyrian machines.

- Weapons: The most iconic weapon is the early bronze sickle-sword (khopesh). The blade is cast bronze, curving outward like a question mark, with the cutting edge on the outside of the curve. The metal is polished to a warm, golden-brown hue. Socketed, narrow-bladed axes are also standard.

- Armor: Light and mobile. Most infantry wear thick, quilted linen tunics padded for protection, rather than metal armor. Helmets are simple, undecorated, conical bronze shells without the horned tiara that Naram-Sin alone wore as a mark of his own divinity - the Louvre's catalogue of his Victory Stele names that horned form only for the king, tagging an ordinary soldier's headgear with the separate, plain word for helmet. An ear-flap style is sometimes attributed to the Akkadian era in popular summaries, but no museum or academic source consulted here confirms ear-flaps for that period; this canon's own Neo-Assyrian chapter (grimoire-16) gives bronze ear-flaps to a helmet roughly 1,300 years later, and the two should not be read as the same thing. (Source: Musee du Louvre, Stele de la victoire de Naram-Sin, collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010123450, checked 2026-09-23; horned tiare a cornes recorded only for Naram-Sin, casque for the soldier.)
