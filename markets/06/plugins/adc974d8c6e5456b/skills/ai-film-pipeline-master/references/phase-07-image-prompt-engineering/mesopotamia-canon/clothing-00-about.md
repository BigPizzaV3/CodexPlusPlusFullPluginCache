# The Mesopotamian Clothing Library

_Twelve Hundred and Fifty Garments Across the Four Civilizations, with Assyria in its Three Ages_

Compiled for Y. Wardah. Every entry is a distinct, prompt-ready written description.

_This library gathers the dress of ancient Mesopotamia into one reference, divided into its four great civilizations: Sumer, Akkad and Babylon with two hundred and fifty garments each, and Assyria, the people who ruled longest, with five hundred across its three ages, the Old, the Middle and the Neo-Assyrian, twelve hundred and fifty in all. Within each civilization the garments are ordered by life and station: infants and young children, children and youth, men by age, women by age, the poor and the middling and the wealthy, the trades and professions, soldiers, court and royalty, priests and scholars, and the garments and materials particular to that people. The clothing is built only from what the Two Rivers truly used: woven wool and flax linen, felt and fleece, leather and reed fiber, the natural dyes of madder, weld and indigo and the rare costly purple, fastened with bronze pins, woven sashes and cords, finished with the tufted kaunakes fleece, fringes and tassels, and the protective amulets of carnelian, lapis, faience and shell. It holds no Islamic, Gulf, Ottoman or medieval veil forms, and nothing modern. Period-authentic Mesopotamian head-coverings do appear where the record documents them, including the Middle Assyrian legal head-shawl. Each description stands alone and may be dropped straight into an image or video prompt._

## What the age brackets are, and what they are not

The by-age files group garments under brackets — *Boy, 10 to 15*, *Woman, 30 to 45* — and **those are
wardrobe brackets, not documented ages.** They exist so that a costume can be found by roughly who is
wearing it, and they are this library's own organising device.

So a bracket is never evidence about a life stage. *"Scribe-Student Boy's Tunic (Boy, 10 to 15)"*
says what a young trainee wore; it does **not** say that scribal training began at ten or ended at
fifteen, and no line of narration should claim it does. The same holds for marriage, military service
and apprenticeship: where the real ages are known they are argued from texts, they differ by period
and by city, and the wardrobe is not where that argument lives. A test agent read one of these
brackets as a historical assertion, which is a fair reading of a number in a heading, and is why this
note now exists.
