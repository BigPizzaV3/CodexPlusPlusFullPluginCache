## Appendix. Glossary of Garment Terms

_A short reference of recurring Mesopotamian dress terms used throughout this library, given for accuracy when writing prompts._

**Wool.** The staple fiber of Mesopotamia, sheared from sheep and woven into most everyday cloth. Warm, workable and easily dyed, it clothed rich and poor alike.

**Linen (kitu).** Cloth woven from flax, finer and costlier than wool and prized when bleached pure white. The dress of priests, the wealthy and the cult statues of the gods.

**Felt.** A dense cloth made by matting and pounding wool rather than weaving it, used for caps, cloaks, padding and warmth.

**Kaunakes.** The signature early garment and look: wool worked into rows of hanging tufts that resemble a fleece, worn as tufted skirts and tiered robes by Sumerians, kings, priestesses and the gods.

**Birmu.** A richly multicolored and trimmed luxury textile named in tribute and trade lists, a display of color and weaving skill reserved for the wealthy.

**Sackcloth.** Coarse dark cloth, often of goat hair, used for sacking and for the rough wraps of the poor and of mourners.

**Fringe.** A border of hanging threads along a hem or edge. In Mesopotamia it marked status and identity (a man pressed his fringe into clay to seal a document) and was thought to carry protection.

**Tassel.** A bound bunch of cords hung from belt, cord or hem, weighting and finishing a garment, richest on royal dress.

**Fillet.** A narrow band tied around the brow to bind the hair, plain and woven for ordinary people, beaded or of metal for the adorned.

**Toggle-pin (tudittu).** A pierced bronze pin pushed through gathered cloth and held with a cord, the characteristic fastening of the Mesopotamian woman's garment at the shoulder or chest.

**Selvedge.** The finished self-edge of a woven cloth, often woven in a contrasting color to make a decorative stripe along the border.

**Madder.** A plant root yielding a warm red dye, the most common source of red for cloth. Colored cloth was a mark of means.

**Weld and saffron.** Plant sources of yellow dye, giving the saffron and gold tones seen in borders and festival garments.

**Indigo and woad.** Plant sources of blue dye, less common than red and yellow and so a sign of greater wealth in a garment.

**Purple.** A rare and costly dye reaching Mesopotamia through trade with the western coast, reserved for the very highest in the rare garments that bore it.

**Kohl.** A dark paste used to line and darken the eyes, worn by women and by the adorned for beauty and against the sun's glare.

**Carnelian.** A red-orange stone cut into beads for necklaces, collars and amulets, one of the most common precious bead-stones.

**Lapis lazuli.** A deep blue stone carried by long trade from the far mountains of the east, prized above almost all others for jewelry and inlay.

**Faience.** A glazed blue-green frit molded into beads and amulets, a cheaper bright substitute for turquoise and lapis, very widely worn.

**Diadem.** A thin band of gold or silver worn around the brow as a mark of nobility or royalty.

**Horned crown.** A crown ringed with rows of bull horns, the headpiece of the gods and of kings who claimed divine honor.

**Mural crown.** A crown shaped like a circuit of city walls and towers, worn by the queen, as on the relief of Ashurbanipal's queen in the vine garden.

**Lubustu.** The temple rite of clothing and ceremonially changing the garments of a god's statue, for which the finest sacred cloth was made.

**Lamashtu and Pazuzu.** Lamashtu, the demoness who threatened mothers and newborns, and Pazuzu, the demon whose head was worn as an amulet to drive her off. Their charms guarded infants and children in Babylonian and Assyrian times.

**Akitu.** The great New Year festival, the chief occasion for the brightest dyed cloth, the fullest jewelry and the finest dress of every class.
