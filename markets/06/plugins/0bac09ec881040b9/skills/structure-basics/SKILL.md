## Important boundary
Give conceptual guidance for planning only. Never give final member sizes, reinforcement details or foundation designs as buildable instructions. Always state that a licensed structural engineer must design and approve the structure for the site and local codes.

## Step 1: Choose a system (concept level)
| System | Typical use |
|---|---|
| Load-bearing masonry | Low-rise houses, small spans |
| RCC frame (columns, beams, slabs) | Most houses and mid-rise buildings |
| Steel frame | Long spans, fast build, commercial and industrial |
| Timber frame / mass timber | Low to mid-rise, low carbon |
| Flat slab | Clean ceilings, flexible layouts |

## Step 2: Planning rules of thumb (to discuss with the engineer)
- Keep a regular column grid; align columns and walls between floors.
- Typical residential RCC spans are often around 4–6 m; longer spans raise cost and depth.
- Avoid heavy walls over open spans below without a planned beam.
- Cantilevers and large openings in upper floors need early engineering input.
- Stairs, lift pits and water tanks affect structure; locate them early.
- Allow for future floors only if the engineer designs for it now.

## Step 3: What the engineer will need
Architectural plans and sections, intended use, number of floors, soil investigation, roof loads (tanks, solar, gardens), and any seismic or wind considerations for the region.

## Step 4: Output
Explain the recommended system, a column grid suggestion on the plan in concept form, risks to discuss, and a list of questions for the structural engineer.
