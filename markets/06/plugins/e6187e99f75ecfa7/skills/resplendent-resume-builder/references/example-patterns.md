# Example patterns and authoritative sources

Use this reference when the user asks for examples, templates, proven methods, or visual direction. It distills recurring practices from university career centers and public sample libraries. Learn from their structure and reasoning; do not copy another candidate's wording, facts, metrics, identity, or distinctive design.

## What the examples consistently support

- Familiar, single-column organization makes evidence easy to find and preserves reading order.
- Tailoring determines what receives space and prominence; a resume is not a complete career archive.
- Strong entries identify role and context quickly, then use concise evidence of action, method, scope, and consequence.
- Formatting earns attention through hierarchy, alignment, typography, and controlled whitespace rather than graphics or novelty.
- One page is common for students and concise consulting applications. Two pages are legitimate when seniority, specialization, research, or relevant evidence warrants them.
- Cover letters work best when they connect one or two examples to a specific employer need and add reasoning or context beyond the resume.
- Generative examples are starting points, not facts. The candidate's own evidence and voice control the final document.

## Original resume patterns

These are original patterns derived from source consensus, not copies of a source document.

### Experienced professional

Use for established individual contributors and managers.

1. Name and compact contact line.
2. Targeted professional headline.
3. Three- to four-line summary only if it clarifies level, domain, and differentiating evidence.
4. Experience in reverse chronology, with the newest two or three roles receiving the most space.
5. Supported technical or domain skills.
6. Education and credentials.

The summary interprets the record; it does not repeat a bullet or list soft skills. Recent bullets emphasize outcomes and judgment. Older roles can use fewer bullets when their value is progression or durable domain experience.

### Senior leader or principal specialist

Use when strategic mandate, operating scale, or organization-wide influence matters.

1. Name, target headline, and contact line.
2. Executive profile anchored in supported scope and value.
3. Optional selected highlights only when they add evidence not repeated immediately in Experience.
4. Experience showing mandate, decisions, cross-functional reach, constraints, and durable outcomes.
5. Earlier career summarized without erasing relevant progression.
6. Education, credentials, and selected board/advisory work if supplied.

Leadership verbs do not create executive positioning by themselves. The evidence must show what the candidate was responsible for, how decisions were made, and what changed.

### Technical or product professional

Use when both technical credibility and business or user outcomes matter.

1. Headline and summary that name the supported product or technical domain.
2. Skills grouped by meaningful domain, not a flat keyword wall.
3. Experience bullets that connect systems, users, product decisions, tradeoffs, delivery, reliability, adoption, or commercial effect.
4. Selected projects only when they add evidence missing from employment history.

Technologies belong in Skills when they help scanning and in bullets when they affected an implementation or decision. Do not repeat every tool in both places.

## Original editing example

The following example is fictional and demonstrates structure only.

**Before**

- Led product strategy for a cloud onboarding platform.
- Drove cross-functional product strategy and roadmap planning.
- Spearheaded product direction that improved adoption by 24%.
- Increased adoption 24% through product leadership.

**After**

- Set the cross-functional roadmap for a cloud onboarding platform, aligning product and engineering work around the highest-friction setup steps.
- Increased adoption 24% after the team simplified the guided setup path.

The revision keeps two distinct propositions: strategic alignment and an adoption outcome. It does not invent causality beyond the illustrative facts. In real work, confirm whether the candidate personally set the roadmap, whether simplification caused the result, and whether the percentage is defensible.

## Original cover-letter pattern

A useful letter normally contains:

1. A supportable reason this particular role or problem deserves attention.
2. One or two examples chosen for direct relevance.
3. Context the resume cannot show: diagnosis, decision criteria, tradeoff, collaboration, motivation, or lesson.
4. A clear connection to the employer's stated work.
5. A short close.

Use one page and roughly 250–400 words as the default range, not a quota. A shorter letter is better when the available evidence makes its point without filler.

## Source notes

The sources below were reviewed on 2026-09-27. Recheck them when current institutional guidance or sample availability matters.

- [MIT Career Advising and Professional Development — Sample resumes and cover letters](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/sample-resumes/): multiple industries, career stages, and composite examples.
- [MIT Career Advising and Professional Development — Resumes](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/resumes/): familiar formats, conservative readable type, relevant evidence, action verbs, keywords, and careful revision.
- [MIT Career Advising and Professional Development — ATS and page-economy guidance](https://capd.mit.edu/blog/2026/06/23/interphase-2026-resume-resources/): avoid tables, text boxes, stylized fonts, and unnecessary graphics; preserve readable whitespace; test parsing and common file types.
- [Harvard Mignone Center — Create impactful resumes and cover letters](https://careerservices.fas.harvard.edu/resources/hes-create-impactful-resumes-and-cover-letters/): factual, specific, scannable writing; balanced whitespace; role-specific examples; concise one-page letters.
- [Harvard Mignone Center — Create a strong resume](https://careerservices.fas.harvard.edu/resources/create-a-strong-resume/): tailored evidence, consistent formatting, and matching resume/cover-letter typography.
- [University of Pennsylvania Career Services — Sample resumes](https://careerservices.upenn.edu/phd-postdoc-sample-resumes/): use samples for structure, section order, and accomplishment language rather than copying; one- and two-page examples by purpose.
- [University of Pennsylvania Career Services — Cover letters](https://careerservices.upenn.edu/channels/cover-letters/): organization-specific motivation, stories that demonstrate relevant skills, and one-page formatting.
- [Yale Office of Career Strategy — CV-to-resume conversion guide](https://cdn.ocs.yale.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/77/2022/07/CV_Resume_Conversion-GSAS-2022.pdf): targeted professional narrative, evidence-backed summaries, and role-specific adaptation.
