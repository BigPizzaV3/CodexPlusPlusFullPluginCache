# Writing from the actual material

Use when composing or revising slide copy, captions or headlines. Preserve exact user copy during a layout-only task. A requested campaign slogan, poetic voice or repeated series title takes priority over these defaults.

## Find something to say before choosing a headline

Separate what the user said, what the images visibly show and what remains unknown. Work from concrete details, actions, decisions, contrasts or questions that matter to this post. For a photograph, lighting, framing, surfaces and visible features can support an observation. They do not establish the time of day, a journey, an owner's feelings or what happened before or after the shutter.

Choose a subject-specific angle rather than decorating the topic with mood words. A stationary motorcycle under garage lights does not establish an after-hours ride, a running engine or an anecdote about its owner. If context is thin, a precise photo essay is a finished outcome; a fabricated personal story is not.

When a personal account genuinely needs missing context, ask one useful question about a real moment, choice or detail. Continue work that does not depend on the answer. Do not turn routine requests into a brand questionnaire. Existing captions or writing samples, when available, are stronger evidence of voice than guesses from age, profession or the photographed subject.

## Choose an angle, not synonyms for one slogan

Privately compare a small set of different editorial approaches supported by the material: a visible detail, a concrete observation, a documented event or a question the slides can actually answer. Choose the one with the clearest connection to this post. Do not expose brainstorming unless requested, and do not force the same number or type of candidates on every task.

Apply a substitution check: could the cover and subtitle be moved unchanged to a café, sneaker or travel post? If so, identify the missing detail or remove the line. Simply appending a model name to an abstract slogan does not solve this. A familiar factual phrase can be better than a strained original metaphor. Read the title together with the image: it should direct attention, add information or establish a supported perspective.

Treat mood titles such as “After Hours,” abstract claims about precision or silence, and balanced slogans as choices requiring a reason, not automatic premium styling. Avoid replacing them with another stock pattern: “not X, but Y,” three dramatic fragments, “nobody tells you,” or an unsupported promise to change the reader's life. These are editorial checks, not banned words; deliberate user copy remains valid.

## Let the sequence earn the next swipe

Give each slide a job in the working notes: what will the reader notice or understand here that the preceding slide did not provide? Choose an order justified by the material. It might follow real events, examine a detail, compare views, explain a decision or develop an argument. Do not make every subject follow hook → problem → solution → CTA.

A photo sequence can move from a whole object to a revealing crop and a second perspective. An explanation needs support or an example before its conclusion. A personal story can use a documented interruption or choice; do not fabricate adversity to manufacture an arc. Vary sentence length and the amount of copy with the information available. A quiet image or a short label can carry a slide.

Make the cover's promise pay off in the body. Remove slides that only repeat its mood or paraphrase the preceding claim. End where the material ends: a last observation, an answer, an image or a useful action when the brief calls for one. Do not add a moral, engagement question or “save this” by habit.

## Preserve a believable voice

Use the requested language and register. Do not switch to English just because the visual direction is editorial or premium. If revising an existing English design without a translation request, preserve English. Borrow from the user's actual phrasing and level of formality without reproducing their typos by default.

Prefer specific nouns and active verbs to adjective stacks. Conversational writing does not require slang, emojis, intentional mistakes or constant jokes. First-person events, memories, emotions and dialogue need user-provided grounding. For an image-only brief, use observations about the image rather than invented autobiography. Do not add fake dates, locations, episode numbers or a series identity as decoration.

Avoid filling every visual slot. A subtitle, eyebrow or footer should contribute distinct information; otherwise omit it or use a functional label only if useful. One good title does not require two additional slogans. Preserve line breaks in the native text, but choose the wording before shrinking it to fit.

## Editorial pass before rendering

Read the copy once without the design, then with the actual images:

- Is each factual or personal claim supported? Are unknown details still left unknown?
- Does the headline have a reason to belong to this material? Does the body deliver on it?
- Does each slide add something? Would removing a repeated line improve the sequence?
- Does it resemble the requested voice, or default to an anonymous advertising narrator?
- Are cover, subtitle and footer doing different jobs? Is empty space preferable to filler?
- If earlier drafts or reference posts are available in this conversation, have their wording and narrative devices been repeated unnecessarily?

Revise the weak part, then inspect the rendered text for rhythm, line breaks and readability. Schema checks and native rendering do not certify editorial quality. This workflow has no cross-chat or Instagram history and cannot guarantee global originality. Never claim a uniqueness scan or an AI-detection score that was not performed.
