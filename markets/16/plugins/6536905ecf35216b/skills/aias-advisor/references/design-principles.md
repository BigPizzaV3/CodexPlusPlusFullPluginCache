# Applying the AIAS to assessment design

Adapted from the AIAS Advisor instructions supplied by Learning Innovation Practice, with operational clarifications for use as a plugin.

## Start with learning and evidence

Levels are task types, not permissions. Each describes a different kind of task and what it assesses. None is better than another. The AIAS applies to formative and summative work.

Changing only the wording of a brief does not redesign an assessment. The task, evidence, checkpoints and marking criteria must support the intended level. Apply the framework to the discipline and institution rather than treating an example as a universal rule.

Ask what the assessment is intended to demonstrate, then choose a level by that focus:

- Level 1: independent knowledge or skills where AI would invalidate the outcomes and genuinely controlled conditions are available.
- Level 2: planning, topic exploration, outlining, research and idea development are what the task assesses, whether or not AI is used.
- Level 3: work completed with possible AI assistance, assessed alongside the student's decisions in evaluating, changing and integrating AI outputs. The design must make unmodified AI output insufficient.
- Level 4: AI involvement is expected; a person alone and AI alone cannot reach the goal within the available time. Assess disciplinary judgement in directing AI and the resulting work.
- Level 5: creative or exploratory AI use is part of the intended learning. Students may co-design approaches and negotiate appropriate criteria.

## Controlled conditions and access

Do not describe an ordinary unsupervised take-home task as Level 1 simply because its brief prohibits AI. If independent performance matters, secure the relevant element or change what is assessed. Secure conditions can include demonstrations, practical work or other supervised tasks; an exam is not the only option.

For unsupervised Level 2 work, do not promise to police where AI use stops. Planning and idea development are assessed either way. If AI use in a later product would invalidate an assessment, reconsider the design or secure that element.

Any level may be run under secure conditions where suitable; Level 1 requires them. Discuss tools, accessibility, cost and institutional availability where AI involvement is expected. Level 4 requires guaranteed access to the necessary tools. Do not assume paid tools are available to every student.

## The Level 3 design claim

"AI alone will not reach the required standard" is a statement about the design of the task. It is not an assertion that AI is generally incapable of strong work. Before endorsing a Level 3 design, consider whether a current model could produce a passing response without student judgement.

The practical trial is to give the brief to a current model, keep its response unedited and mark it against the rubric. If it passes, strengthen the design. A single test is evidence about those conditions, not proof that every model or future attempt would fail.

Possible changes include:

- Using relevant material the model cannot access on its own, such as local observations, current class discussion or the student's own fieldwork.
- Requiring a defended decision, comparison with a real case or demonstration of disciplinary reasoning.
- Raising or clarifying the standard so that unmodified output falls short.

Do not assume local or personal material makes AI use impossible: students can supply that material to a model. The assessment must still make their judgement visible.

Assess both the quality of the disciplinary work and how AI output is evaluated and integrated. Assessing only the product can overlook judgement; assessing only the process can reduce the task to commentary about using a tool. Set weightings with the educator and their institutional grading system.

Where voice matters in a discipline, name it in the criteria. It is not part of the current official Level 3 statement and should not be imposed universally.

## The Level 4 design claim

Also ask whether a capable student could achieve the goal alone in the time available. If so, revisit why AI involvement is necessary and whether the task is sufficiently ambitious. Grade the subject knowledge and critical judgement used to direct AI, not simply the number of prompts or the appearance of AI use.

## Process evidence and authorship

Process evidence should be light and purposeful: make decisions, checks and reasoning visible. It is not proof of authorship. The old Level 3 expectation to submit original work alongside AI-assisted content is no longer part of the current level statement.

Explain that the AIAS authors favour assessment design over detection-led enforcement. Detector scores and version histories do not reliably establish authorship. Do not use them alone for summative misconduct decisions or infer cheating from a student's language background or writing style.

Relevant detection research: [Perkins et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41239-024-00487-w). Read the research before quoting a result or statistic; do not turn an unverified claim into a finding.

## Common mistakes to identify

- Attaching a level label without redesigning the task and criteria.
- Calling an unsupervised take-home task Level 1.
- Requiring AI while failing to provide equitable access.
- Using detection to enforce a level.
- Reintroducing older level wording from a research paper as the current scale.
