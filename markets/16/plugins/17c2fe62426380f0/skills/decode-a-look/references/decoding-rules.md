# Decoding rules

How to write a style so it survives being moved to another room. The test: could this description restyle a bedroom, a kitchen and a hallway, and would all three be recognisably the same look?

## The rules

- **No inventory.** Do not name pieces of furniture or objects: no "tulip table", no "four-poster bed", no "bar cart". Describe shape, proportion and silhouette instead: "low, long and rectilinear", "plump rounded volumes on recessed bases", "thin black lines against pale mass".
- **Nothing a renter cannot do.** Leave out building work: panelling, mouldings, built-ins, floor and ceiling finishes, arches, fireplaces, columns, architectural lighting. Where the look depends on one of these, say what it contributes (a colour, a pattern, a rhythm, a sheen) in a way that paint, textiles, rugs, lamps and freestanding pieces can deliver.
- **Floors become rugs, walls become paint.** A pattern or colour the photo has on the floor is carried by a rug; one it has on the walls by paint or removable wallcovering. Write it that way: "a black-and-white chequerboard rug", never "a chequerboard floor".
- **Only materials a movable thing can be made of.** If a material appears only as construction (a marble floor, plaster mouldings), carry over its colour, pattern or sheen and leave the material out. Do not invent a substitute material.
- **Specific in the abstract.** "Warm neutrals" is useless. "Chalk white and pale oak, with one saturated tomato red used once" is the level wanted.
- **Keep what makes it unmistakable.** If the description would also fit a different look, it is too generic.
- **Plain statements.** No "timeless", no "curated", no "elevated".

## What each field is for

- **Essence** — what the room feels like and what produces that feeling.
- **Palette, base** — the two to four colours that cover most of the room.
- **Palette, accent** — the one to three colours used sparingly.
- **Palette, balance** — rough proportions, where each colour sits (large surfaces, textiles, small objects), and how warm and how saturated the whole is.
- **Contrast** — how strong, and between what: light against dark, matte against gloss, plain against patterned.
- **Negative space** — how much floor and wall stays empty, how many objects sit on a surface.
- **Shapes** — curved or rectilinear, low or tall, heavy or thin-legged, large-scale or small.
- **Materials** — each with its finish: matte, glossy, nubby, veined, brushed.
- **Texture and pattern** — how textures are mixed, and the scale and density of any pattern.
- **Light** — quality and warmth, even or pooled, high or low.
- **Composition** — symmetry or asymmetry, rhythm, where the eye lands first, how things are layered.
- **Signature** — at most two gestures, described as colour, shape or placement, never as a product.
- **Avoid** — up to six things that would break the look.

## Reading a single image

Look, in this order, at: the largest colour areas; the second-largest; the smallest and brightest; what is matte and what shines; whether edges are straight or curved; how much is empty; where the light comes from and how warm it is; what the eye hits first.

## Reading many images

Group them by what they share. For each group note the mood tags, the colours, the dominant and accent materials, the textures, and the character of the seating, tables, storage and lighting. Then merge: deduplicate, choose colours consistently, keep the mood to three to six words. Something that appears in one image out of twenty is an outlier, not the style.
