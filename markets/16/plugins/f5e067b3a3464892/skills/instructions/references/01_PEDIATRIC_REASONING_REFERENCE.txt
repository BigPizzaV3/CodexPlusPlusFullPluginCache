PEDIATRIC REASONING REFERENCE

PURPOSE

Use this file as a conceptual reference for pediatric reasoning. It expands the core instructions but does not replace them.

1. PEDIATRIC PROBLEM REPRESENTATION

A useful pediatric problem representation compresses the case into a small number of discriminating dimensions:

- age and developmental stage
- acute, subacute, chronic, recurrent, or progressive course
- dominant symptom or syndrome
- severity
- growth and nutritional context
- hydration or perfusion when relevant
- key positive clues
- key negative clues
- important exposures or background

Avoid copying the entire history.

Ask:
Which facts materially change the differential?

2. AGE AS A DIAGNOSTIC VARIABLE

Age changes:
- what is physiologically normal
- disease prevalence
- symptom expression
- likely pathogens
- congenital versus acquired causes
- feeding pattern
- communication ability
- vulnerability to dehydration or respiratory compromise
- interpretation of laboratory values

Reasoning should therefore ask:
Would I interpret this finding differently in a neonate, young infant, toddler, school-age child, or adolescent?

3. DEVELOPMENTAL CONTEXT

Development changes both normal behavior and disease presentation.

Consider:
- feeding skill
- motor development
- language and communication
- toileting
- sleep
- puberty
- developmental regression

Regression is conceptually different from delayed acquisition and often changes the differential.

4. GROWTH

A growth trajectory is usually more informative than one isolated measurement.

When relevant, consider:
- weight trajectory
- length or height trajectory
- head circumference in younger children
- growth velocity
- crossing percentiles
- proportionality
- nutritional intake
- chronic disease clues

Always ask whether the reported growth problem is:
- true growth failure
- a measurement issue
- constitutional variation
- nutritional
- systemic
- endocrine
- gastrointestinal
- genetic or syndromic

Do not infer the cause from growth data alone.

5. FEEDING AND NUTRITION

Feeding history may include:
- breast milk or formula
- complementary foods
- quantity and frequency
- feeding duration
- choking, coughing, gagging
- vomiting
- food restriction
- appetite
- textures
- feeding behavior
- caregiver-child interaction
- dietary diversity

Mechanistic categories:
- inadequate intake
- impaired swallowing or feeding skill
- malabsorption
- excessive losses
- increased metabolic demand
- altered utilization

6. SEVERITY FIRST

Before refining a differential, ask whether the child may be unstable.

Conceptual red-flag domains include:
- respiratory compromise
- poor perfusion or shock
- altered consciousness
- severe dehydration
- significant bleeding
- severe neurologic signs
- very young infant with concerning systemic illness
- rapidly progressive symptoms

The educational priority is:
Stability and urgency before diagnostic elegance.

7. CHRONOLOGY

Chronology often discriminates better than symptom labels.

Ask:
- sudden or gradual?
- first episode or recurrent?
- progressive or fluctuating?
- relation to feeding?
- relation to infection?
- nocturnal?
- exercise-related?
- episodic with complete recovery?
- associated with developmental change or medication exposure?

8. DIFFERENTIAL ORGANIZATION

Avoid “list everything.”

Prefer three buckets:

A. Common and plausible
Conditions compatible with age, prevalence, and pattern.

B. Important not-to-miss
Less common conditions where delay matters.

C. Clue-driven alternatives
Conditions specifically suggested by unusual features.

For each important alternative ask:
- Why does it fit?
- What argues against it?
- What clue would discriminate it?

9. MECHANISM

Use:
Observed finding
→ physiologic disturbance
→ organ/system effect
→ symptom or sign

Mechanisms help transfer knowledge across cases.

10. FUNCTIONAL VS ORGANIC

Do not treat “functional” as meaning imaginary or unimportant.

Reason by:
- age
- chronicity
- growth
- systemic features
- nocturnal symptoms
- bleeding
- persistent vomiting
- focal findings
- inflammatory clues
- neurologic clues
- family history

A familiar benign pattern should not automatically override discordant red flags.

11. CONGENITAL VS ACQUIRED

Congenital disease may present late.
Acquired disease may present early.

Use onset and developmental history rather than a rigid age rule.

Consider:
- prenatal clues
- birth history
- newborn screening
- early feeding
- growth
- congenital anomalies
- family history

12. LABORATORY INTERPRETATION

For each test ask:

Known:
What exactly was measured?

Context:
What age range and physiologic state apply?

Validity:
Could sampling, timing, hydration, hemolysis, fasting status, medications, or handling affect it?

Meaning:
Does the result support a hypothesis or actually establish it?

Limit:
What can this result not exclude or prove?

Integration:
Does it fit the clinical picture?

Avoid:
abnormal value → diagnosis

Prefer:
clinical question → test meaning → integrated interpretation

13. IMAGING AND PROCEDURES

Before interpreting an imaging study or procedure, ask:
- why was it obtained?
- what question can it answer?
- what is its sensitivity to the suspected process?
- what findings are specific or nonspecific?
- does timing matter?
- are there operator or technical limitations?

14. CLINICAL CONTRADICTIONS

Actively search for data that do not fit.

Useful question:
“If my leading hypothesis were correct, what would I expect to see that is absent?”

Contradiction does not automatically exclude a diagnosis, but it should reduce overconfidence.

15. RED-FLAG REASONING

A red flag is not a diagnosis.

Its purpose is to:
- widen the differential
- increase urgency
- challenge a benign assumption
- prompt broader evaluation

Do not turn a single nonspecific feature into alarm without context.

16. COMMON REASONING TRAPS

Premature closure:
Stopping after finding one plausible diagnosis.

Anchoring:
Over-weighting the first impression.

Confirmation bias:
Seeking only supporting evidence.

Availability bias:
Choosing a diagnosis because it is memorable.

Pattern matching without mechanism:
Recognizing a familiar label despite discordant data.

Base-rate neglect:
Favoring a rare explanation without sufficient clues.

Over-testing logic:
Believing more tests automatically produce more certainty.

17. FINAL SELF-CHECK

Before concluding a learning case, ask:
- What is the strongest clue?
- What is the strongest counter-clue?
- What did age change?
- What mechanism best explains the pattern?
- What alternative remains reasonable?
- What data are missing?
- What could be misleading?
