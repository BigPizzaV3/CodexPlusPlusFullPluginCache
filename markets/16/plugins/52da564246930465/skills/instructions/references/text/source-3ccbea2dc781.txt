When you don’t have clear accountability, people will make assumptions. You want to build a
culture where results matter. Ambiguity is not your friend—specific results that you can measure
and celebrate are crucial. When people see clear metrics, they get excited. They see progress and
ask, “How can we push further?” Or if something isn't going well, they analyze it and find
solutions. Creating a culture where results matter is productive, and I encourage you to do the
same.

One thing I’ve always emphasized is personal responsibility. When I say I’ll do something, I put
my name on it. That’s part of my personal vision. In a company, every department should take
ownership of their work. For example, if someone in purchasing makes an ACH transfer, they
put their name on it, ensuring accuracy and accountability. If a mistake happens, they own up to
it and correct it. This kind of accountability fosters a positive culture where people feel engaged
and motivated.

Building a strong culture means keeping people connected. If you have remote employees, get
them on calls regularly—whether it’s weekly, biweekly, or monthly. When people feel isolated,
they disengage. But if they feel like part of a team, they get excited and work harder. Sharing
company objectives and achievements with them is crucial. If your company grew 20% year-
over-year, tell them! If a department improved their performance, acknowledge it. Recognizing
contributions creates a transformational work environment.

A clear organizational structure also helps with retention. Employees need to see a future within
the company. If someone starts in customer service, they should know what career paths are
available to them. People are naturally self-interested—they think about their careers and their
families. If they don’t see a future with your company, they’ll leave. Show them there’s a reason
to stay.

Accountability also means knowing who you report to and understanding your key metrics.
Personal responsibility should be part of the culture. At Amazon and eBay, people often say, “I
own this.” They may not literally own it, but they take full responsibility for it. The more
employees think this way, the more engaged they are.

Trust is also essential. Doing the right thing should always be the only option. If you can’t fulfill
a customer order, don’t promise it. If you commit to a deadline, meet it. Your brand should be
built on reliability—doing what you say you will do, when you say you will do it. This builds
long-term trust with customers and employees.

A safe and stable environment is another key factor. Employees should feel secure, even when
they make mistakes. Mistakes will happen. The key is to learn from them and move forward. I’ve
had team members make $5,000–$6,000 mistakes. Instead of punishing them, I treat it as a
learning experience. I tell them, “That was an expensive lesson, like a semester at Harvard. Just
don’t make the same mistake again.” Failing fast and learning quickly leads to growth. The only
unacceptable mistakes are the ones repeated over and over.

Another core principle is treating people the way you’d want to be treated. If you ever have to
fire someone, it should never be a surprise to them. If they’re not performing, you should already
be communicating with them about it. If their behavior or metrics aren’t meeting expectations,

Building a scalable business means moving away from a structure where everything goes
through you. Many startups begin with the founder as the bottleneck. They handle finance,
product sourcing, customer service—everything. But this isn’t sustainable. The goal is to define
clear roles and delegate effectively.

Start by defining your company’s future organizational chart. What does your business look like
in five years? If your goal is to build a $50 million company, you need to map out the necessary
departments and roles to support that growth. Some people aim to build and sell brands quickly,
while others want long-term stability. Your org chart will depend on your business model and
objectives.

As your company scales, you'll need to categorize your functions logically. A typical structure
includes:

   •    Operations: Includes logistics, supply chain, and fulfillment.
   •   Customer Service: Can be under operations or its own department.
   •    Marketing: Includes content, acquisition, and monetization.
   •    Sales: Manages business development and key accounts.
   •    Finance: Handles budgets, accounting, and legal compliance.
   •   HR: Manages hiring, training, and employee development.
   •    Technology: Oversees software, website development, and security.
Building Out the Marketing Department

Within marketing, you might break it down further:

   •    Content: Includes copywriters, graphic designers, and video producers.
   •    Acquisition: Focuses on traffic sources like PPC, SEO, and affiliates.
   •    Monetization: Works on email marketing, upsells, and customer retention.
Titles matter, even if they don’t matter to you. Employees see titles as a form of recognition and
career progression. Be intentional with titles, and avoid inflating them too early. If someone
starts as a manager but later gets moved down to a specialist role, they’ll see it as a demotion,
even if their pay increases. Start with lower titles and allow room for growth.

Once your org chart is established, define key performance indicators (KPIs) for each
department. If you don’t measure it, you can’t improve it. KPIs should be simple and focused.
For example:

   •    Marketing: Customer acquisition cost (CAC), return on ad spend (ROAS)
   •    Sales: Conversion rate, average order value (AOV)
   •   Customer Service: Response time, customer satisfaction score (CSAT)
   •    Operations: Order fulfillment speed, inventory turnover
Roy, for example, tracks his business through key metrics every single day. One important ratio
he watches is organic traffic versus paid traffic. He wants to ensure that organic traffic remains

strong so that ad spend doesn’t get out of control. If that ratio starts shifting too much towards
paid, he knows something is off and investigates.

Another metric he monitors closely is AOV (average order value). When they transitioned to a
mobile-responsive website, AOV dropped because they removed family item listings and
recommended products. This insight led them to make changes that restored AOV.

The goal is to build a self-sustaining business. Even if you don’t plan to sell, structuring your
company as if you will makes it more valuable. A business that runs independently is worth far
more than one that depends on you for every decision.

Finally, always refine your processes. Your org chart isn’t static—it evolves as your company
grows. Keep reviewing and adjusting it to fit your business needs. This will help you scale
efficiently and ensure long-term success.
