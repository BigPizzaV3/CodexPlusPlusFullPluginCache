# Investor frameworks: how the best investors judge founders, markets and bets

Ivan Landabaso's collected frameworks from the best investors: what they look for in the founders, the market and the bet, and how each shows up (or fails to) in a deck. Use these lenses to explain why a slide works or doesn't, and name the investor or framework when you use one.

## 1. The core equation

Exceptional investments = risky AND right AND contrarian (Wealthfront, Sarah Tavel, Andy Rachleff). The only winning quadrant is non-consensus and right. Consensus-and-right returns nothing special, because everyone is already in the deal.

Deck consequence: a deck that is entirely consensus ("AI agents for the enterprise", "the Uber of X") signals a crowded, priced-up round with no alpha, however polished. The coach should ask on every deck: what here is non-consensus, and where does the founder show why they are right about it?

Related lenses:
- **Secret knowledge** (Thiel, Floodgate): what do you believe that nobody else believes? A deck with no answer has no Insight slide, whatever it calls it.
- **Contrarian requires secrets**: being contrarian is only possible if the founder knows something. Ask for it on the Insight slide.
- **Courage** (Nick Chirls): in moments that need courage the market is far less efficient. Founders and investors who act when others hesitate get the price.
- **Inexplicable weird behaviour**: the biggest non-consensus opportunities hide in odd human behaviour (watching gamers play in dorm rooms). A deck that names a weird behaviour and explains it is doing something rare.
- **Where there's muck there's brass** (Paul Graham): boring-looking businesses hide remarkable returns; great founders get stuck in bad-but-good-looking ideas. Do not penalise an unglamorous market.

## 2. The market lens

- **Rachleff's law**: great team plus lousy market, market wins. Lousy team plus great market, market wins. Great team plus great market, something special. Markets > founders > product for most investors (Elad Gil: "I've seen great founders repeatedly get crushed by a terrible market").
- **Currents, not bodies of water** (why now): a market is a current. The stronger the current, the higher the odds. Size matters less than the pull. The Why Now slide must show the current: what changed, how fast, why it is accelerating.
- **Fast-moving water** (NFX): read the river for leading indicators, not lagging ones. Technologies, concepts and behaviours starting to take off. A Why Now built on lagging stats (last year's industry spend) is weak.
- **Inflection recency** (Floodgate): has a recent event opened a breach? Loft harnessed the iPhone GPS chip. A change in legislation, a platform shift, a cost curve crossing. Name it, date it.
- **Thunder lizards** (Floodgate): radioactive eggs that grow into Godzillas that eat entire industries. Does the market allow a monster?
- **Aspirin vs vitamin**: is the problem painful enough that people pay now? Vitamin decks read fine and do not close.
- **Popular, growing, urgent, expensive, mandatory, frequent** (YC's problem filter): six properties of a great problem. Score the Problem slide against them: millions have it, 20%+ yearly growth, wanted now, expensive to solve, mandatory (a law changed), recurrent (hourly, daily).
- **Beware "checking the box" traction** (YC): companies riding market hype (horizontal AI agents for the enterprise) show traction that is the hype's, not theirs. Ask what stays if the hype cools.
- **Liquidity quality** (Benchmark, Bill Gurley): how impressive are the early growth and engagement numbers on their own, independent of TAM and of your assumptions about the world. Facebook went small in universities with phenomenal engagement while MySpace went everywhere. Yelp started with NYC nightclubs. Uber started with black cars in SF. The Traction slide should show a bright fire in a small place, not a warm glow across a wide one.
- **Bottoms-up** (Peter Fenton): "throw that crystal ball out"; you cannot predict, you can recognise when lightning strikes. The best story provides an insight that makes the investor think about the world differently (Eric Vishria).

## 3. The founder lens

The consistent traits across Sam Altman, Ron Conway, YC, Creandum, Floodgate, Naval, Cowen and Gross, and Ivan's own list:

- **Obsession and intensity**: cannot stop thinking about the problem; insane desire to work; speed, energy, drive. Shaan Puri: intensity = common sense × focus × insanity.
- **Speed**: Altman, "I have yet to meet a slow-moving person who is very successful." Look for what they accomplished between touch points. In a deck: what shipped, when, how fast, dated.
- **Formidable**: from executing well so often. Shaan Puri's test: "God, I would hate to be competing against this guy in the same market."
- **Communicators**: infectious, can sell, hire, inspire. But articulateness alone is overrated (Cowen and Gross), especially by smart people. Judge what they have built, not how they talk.
- **Independent thinker, secret holder**: sees the world differently, can explain the idea maze in detail, knows something about the future that is not consensus.
- **Living in the future** (Floodgate, founder-future fit): the founder notices what the future is like and has a burning need to build for it.
- **Life's work, mission-driven** (David Senra, Positive Sum): is this their forever business? Durability of the founder predicts durability of the company.
- **Founder-market fit** (NFX): obsession with the specific market that lets them endure the decade without burning out.
- **Compounded self-improvement** (Altman, Cowen): detectable improvement each time you meet them. Rate of improvement beats current ability.
- **High agency, thinks very big**: am I almost scared of how big they are thinking? "Is this person a force of nature?" (Altman).
- **Openness plus disagreeableness** (Cowen and Gross): disagreeable enough to pursue what others discourage, open enough to take feedback. Plus stamina, sturdiness (gets things done day after day), generativeness (vitality, runs combinations of ideas), happiness.
- **A language of one's own**: founders who have wrestled with a problem long enough to invent their own vocabulary for it (Thiel's "technological stagnation").
- **The jockey brings the horses** (Alan Patricof): did people follow them from their last job? Strongest cheap signal of leadership.
- **Extraordinary magnitude** (YC application): "something impressive that each founder has built or achieved." Magnitude, not type.
- **The 10-year test** (Creandum): strip away credentials. Would I take orders from this person for ten years? No, pass. Yes, move fast.
- **3H / Naval**: high integrity, high energy, high intelligence, and do I enjoy spending time with them. Ethics: any whiff, avoid (Andreessen).
- **Avoid the naive perspective** (Robert Greene): what qualities might they lack, what dark side am I ignoring?
- **Ivan's outlier traits**: chameleonic; extreme high agency; thinking very big; unusual passion projects; early signs of entrepreneurship; extreme intensity; moved cities a lot when young; can tell a story about a market they know phenomenally well.
- **The Silicon Valley bar**: invest in founders who make you feel slow; you should feel intimidated by their IQ and drive.

Deck consequence: the Team slide is where most of this either shows or doesn't. The coach should check whether the slide gives evidence of magnitude (built or achieved), speed (dated shipping), followership (who came with them), and market obsession (time in the maze), rather than titles and logos. Advisors carry none of these signals.

## 4. The bet lens

- **PMF is the only thing that matters** (Andreessen). Elad Gil: ultimately the focus is product-market fit. A deck that talks strategy before showing any fit is out of order.
- **Single miracle** (Elad Gil): each startup gets one miracle. Two miracles multiply small odds. Count the miracles a deck is asking for (new technology AND new behaviour AND new regulation).
- **Durability over growth** (Thiel): why will this still be the leading company in 10 years? Growth is measurable now, so it gets overvalued; survival dominates the value equation.
- **Unfair advantage** (YC, five kinds): founder expertise (1 in 10 in the world who can solve it), market growing 20%+ (weakest alone), product 10x better (2x or 3x is not enough), $0 acquisition (word of mouth, not paid), monopoly as you grow (marketplaces, network effects). Score the Insight and Competition slides against these five and say which ones the deck actually claims with evidence.
- **Distribution edge** (Thiel): superior distribution alone can create a monopoly; a superior product alone cannot. A deck with no distribution plan is a deck with no plan.
- **Positive anomaly**: anything anomalous that is uniquely advantageous. Name it.
- **Exponential bet with a clear moat** (Founders Fund): 80% founders and moat, 20% market size. Maximise the upside, do not minimise the downside.
- **Index Ventures' five**: exit above $1B within ten years; revenue potential; velocity (something in the core product makes it grow faster while acquisition costs fall); profitability potential (variable, fixed, marketing); predictability.
- **Instrument of discovery** (Matt Lerner): early-stage success depends on pace and quality of learning. Scientist mode (NFX): do they want to learn, can they learn fast? A deck that shows what was learned from the last three months signals this.
- **Running the tape forward**: do the founders already have a plan for the next stage (Series A relationships, named milestones)? The Ask slide is where this shows.
- **Inversion** (Munger): how does this business die? A deck that names its own killer risks and how it handles them reads as more credible, not less.
- **Memoria económica**: what if there is a crisis? Watney in The Martian: no new potatoes are coming. Does the plan survive a closed funding window?
- **What could go right?**: always push this question. Paul Graham on investing: the job is to understand how a company could win, not to poke holes. The coach should end every review with the strongest version of the case, not only the holes.
- **Head, chest, gut**: look for a yes in each, or a contraction in any.
- **Regret minimisation**: on my deathbed, would I regret not investing?
- **Subversive** (Thiel): so what? Does it change the world meaningfully?

## 5. Firm lenses (how specific investors decide)

- **Founders Fund**: 80% best founders on exponential bets with clear moats, 20% market. No prediction games. Best companies, best price, most capital. No boards; strategy dinners on the one unique moat. Trust the gut, refine judgement. The talent filter: a referral from someone they consider talented becomes top priority.
- **Benchmark**: bottoms-up, not thesis-driven. Liquidity quality. Insight that changes how you see the world. Rachleff's law, and also: sometimes great products create huge markets, getting to PMF is seldom straightforward.
- **YC**: great problem first (six properties), solution second, avoid solution-in-search-of-a-problem, five unfair advantages. Best founders: execute, formidable, communicate, internal drive despite failure.
- **Floodgate**: thunder lizards, founder-future fit, value stack, inflections.
- **Index**: the five questions above, all about the path to a $1B exit.
- **Elad Gil**: back the best people on the biggest problems; billion-dollar potential; a little bit not obvious; single miracle; don't fear the single dominant vision.
- **Creandum**: genuinely ambitious enough; can explain how and why an industry will change; would I work for this person for a decade.
- **Thiel**: durability, secrets, distribution, monopoly.
- **a16z / Andreessen**: PMF is the only thing that matters.

## 6. The questions investors ask (a deck should answer most of these without being asked)

From YC's application and Ivan's qualification list:

- What are you creating, in the most matter-of-fact way possible?
- Who writes the code? Is any of it done by non-founders?
- How long have the founders known each other, and how did they meet? Have they all met in person?
- How is the cap table split? What do founders own?
- In one or two sentences, the most impressive thing each founder has built or achieved.
- What stage, what progress, how long full-time?
- Why this idea? What specialised knowledge do you have?
- What evidence that there is demand?
- Who are the competitors, and what do you know that they don't?
- How do you make money, and how much could it be?

A deck that answers eight of these ten without being asked is doing its job.
