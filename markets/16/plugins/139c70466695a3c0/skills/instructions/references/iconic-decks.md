# Iconic pitch decks: what each one teaches

Breakdowns of famous startup decks from the PitchMagic library. Use them as concrete examples when coaching ("Airbnb's problem slide does this in two lines..."). Each deck has a full page with the slides at the link. Quote the lesson, not the whole breakdown.

## Airbnb (2008, Seed, raised $600K)
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/airbnb
The original AirBed&Breakfast deck: a marketplace for booking a room in someone's home instead of a hotel.

Slide order: Welcome → Problem → Solution → Market Validation → Market Size → Product → Business Model → Adoption Strategy → Competition → Competitive Advantages → Team → Press → User Testimonials → Financial

What works:
- Slide 2 names three problems in three short sentences, each one a real reason a traveler would switch.
- Slides 4 and 5 prove demand with numbers from existing behavior on Couchsurfing and Craigslist before sizing the market.
- Slide 14 ties the ask to one milestone, 80,000 transactions in 12 months, so investors know what the money buys.

Lessons to steal:
- Point to people already doing a clumsy version of your idea, the way Airbnb used Couchsurfing and Craigslist, as your best early proof.
- Show the revenue math in one line, users times fee equals revenue, instead of a full spreadsheet.
- Keep each slide to one idea and a title that says what it is, so a busy investor can skim the whole deck in two minutes.

## Uber (2008, Seed, raised )
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/uber
Garrett Camp's first UberCab deck: an invite-only black-car service you book from your phone.

Slide order: Cover → Cabs in 2008 → Medallion System → UberCab Concept → 1-Click Car Service → Key Differentiators → Operating Principles → UberCab Apps → UberCab.com → Use Cases → User Benefits → Environmental Benefits → Fleet → Initial Service Area → Technology → Demand Forecasting → Overall Market → Market Composition → Target Cities → Potential Outcomes → Smartphones 2008 → Future Optimizations → Marketing Ideas → Location-Based Service → Progress to Date

What works:
- Slides 2 and 3 explain why taxis are bad with hard numbers, a 14mpg car and a $500k medallion next to a $31k driver salary.
- Slide 20 gives a best, realistic and worst case, which tells investors the founder has thought about how it could go wrong.
- Slide 25 lists small concrete steps already done and the exact next milestones, which makes a pre-product idea feel real.

Lessons to steal:
- You can pitch before you have a product if you show clear progress, a first city, and what you will do with the money next.
- Explain the rule or cost that makes the current market bad, the way UberCab used the medallion system, so the opening is obvious.
- Cut the extras, since slides on fleet, environment and side markets add length without making the core case stronger.

## Coinbase (2012, Seed, raised $600K)
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/coinbase
Brian Armstrong's seed deck from YC Demo Day, when Coinbase was a hosted bitcoin wallet.

Slide order: Cover → What Bitcoin Is → Global Currency → Bitcoin Growth → Early Adopters → Too Difficult to Use → Product → Analogy → Signups → Transactions → Vision → Contact

What works:
- Slide 8 explains the whole company with one analogy, Coinbase is to bitcoin what iTunes is to MP3, which any investor in 2012 would get at once.
- Slide 6 shows the problem instead of describing it, with screenshots of how hard bitcoin tools were to use.
- Slides 9 and 10 put real traction, 20% daily signup growth and $65,000 in 5 weeks, near the end where it answers the obvious doubt.

Lessons to steal:
- If your market is new, spend the first slides teaching what it is in three words or fewer per point before you pitch your company.
- Use one familiar analogy to make a strange product feel obvious.
- A short deck with one strong traction chart beats a long deck with many weak slides, especially for a Demo Day style pitch.

## Notion (2013, Seed, raised )
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/notion
Ivan Zhao's 2013 seed deck, published by Notion itself: an all-in-one tool for notes, docs and wikis.

Slide order: Cover → Background → Mission → Problem → Solution → Demo → Markets Overview → Design Tools Today → Design Tools with Notion → Enterprise Today → Enterprise with Notion → Marketplace Today → Marketplace with Notion → Marketplace Economy → Timing → Roadmap

What works:
- Slide 2 frames the whole opportunity with one ratio, 2 million developers building for 80 million knowledge workers, shown as simple icons.
- Slides 8 to 14 use a today versus with Notion pair for each market, so every claim comes with a before and after picture.
- Slide 15 answers why now by naming past attempts, Hypercard and OpenDoc, and saying what changed.

Lessons to steal:
- If your vision is big and abstract, pick one small first market, like Notion did with design tools, and show it on the roadmap.
- Use before and after diagrams instead of paragraphs when your product changes how people work.
- Name the earlier companies that tried your idea and explain why the timing is different now.

## Dropbox (2007, Seed, raised $1.2M)
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/dropbox
Dropbox's 2007 seed deck: files that sync across every computer, with nothing to think about.

Slide order: Cover → Storage Is a Mess → Problem → Current Solutions → Perfect World → Solution → Demo → Why Now → Why Better → Competitors → What Others Got Wrong → Technical Advantages → Team → Business Model → Platform → Customer Acquisition → Press

What works:
- Slides 3 and 4 name the pain and the bad workarounds people already use, email attachments and USB drives, so every investor recognizes the problem from their own life.
- Slide 8 answers why now with four concrete trends and one sharp comparison to search before Google.
- Slide 10 uses a simple checkmark table that shows at a glance that no competitor does everything Dropbox does.

Lessons to steal:
- Add explicit why now and why better slides, since those are the two questions investors ask most.
- Show what people do today instead of your product, because the workaround proves the pain is real.
- Put your distribution plan in the deck, as Dropbox did with free accounts and viral sharing on slide 16, since a good product alone is not a plan.

## YouTube (2005, Series A, raised $3.5M)
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/youtube
YouTube's 2005 deck to Sequoia: a place for anyone to upload and share video.

Slide order: Cover → Company Purpose → Problem → Solution → Market Size → Competition → Product Development → Sales and Distribution → Team → Metrics

What works:
- Slide 3 lists four specific, technical problems with video in 2005 that anyone who had tried to email a video would recognize.
- Slide 5 makes the case for timing with two shifts, cheap video recording and home broadband, instead of a big market number.
- Slide 10 ends on traction with one bold claim, that YouTube had already overtaken every competitor since launch.

Lessons to steal:
- Follow a simple order of purpose, problem, solution, why now, competition, model, team and traction, and you cover what investors need.
- Explain why now with the changes in technology that make your product possible today.
- Don't wait for perfect design, since a clear story in plain text can raise a round.

## LinkedIn (2004, Series B, raised $10M)
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/linkedin
Reid Hoffman's 2004 pitch to Greylock, which he later published with his own notes: a professional network.

Slide order: Cover → What LinkedIn Is → Problem → Networks vs Directories → eBay Analogy → PayPal Analogy → Google Analogy → LinkedIn Analogy → Network First → Series A Plan → Growth vs Plan → Market Share Feb 2004 → Market Share Aug 2004 → Competitor Map → Groups → Results So Far → Partnerships → Barriers to Entry → Network Enables Revenue → LinkedIn Revenue Model → Free Plus Paid → Market Size → InLeads → Search Ads Market → Opportunities → Customer Quotes → Jobs Market → Network Plus → Subscription Market → Pricing and Launch → Business Model → Model Assumptions → 5-Year Financials → Team → Board and Investors → Summary → Closing Line

What works:
- Slides 5 to 8 explain a new idea with three companies investors already understood, eBay, PayPal and Google, and then put LinkedIn in the same pattern.
- Slides 10 and 11 show the Series A growth projection first and then the actual line blowing past it, which proves the team beats its own plan.
- Slide 30 gives exact prices and launch dates for each revenue line, so the question of how a free product makes money gets a concrete answer.

Lessons to steal:
- If your business model is new, compare it to companies investors already know and show the same pattern.
- Show the plan you pitched last round next to what actually happened, because beating your own forecast builds trust fast.
- If your product is free today, name each future revenue line with a price and a launch date.

## BuzzFeed (2008, Series A, raised $3.5M)
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/buzzfeed
Jonah Peretti's 2008 investor deck, later shared with Ad Age: a media company built on how content spreads socially.

Slide order: Cover → Where We Are → Where We're Headed → Editorial Content → Algorithmic Content → User Generated Content → New Front Page → Platform for Buzz → The Web App → Optimization → Leaderboard → Revenue Model → Ads as Content → BuzzFeed Ad Model → Distributed Promotion → Custom Promotion → Trend Targeting → Competitors → How Big Can This Get? → Team → Contact

What works:
- Slide 2 puts traffic, team size and the $60K monthly burn on one page, so an investor knows exactly where the company stands.
- Slide 3 turns the plan into four specific goals, including proving the revenue model within a year and the exact roles to hire.
- Slides 13 and 14 show a pattern investors already knew from Google and YouTube and then show the same layout on BuzzFeed.

Lessons to steal:
- Open with a plain status slide that lists your traffic, team and burn, because honest numbers early make the rest of the deck easier to trust.
- Follow the status slide with a short list of what the money will do, written as goals you can check a year later.
- When your business model is new, point to a bigger company already doing a version of it.

## Peloton (2011, Seed, raised )
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/peloton
Peloton's early seed deck, made public as a court exhibit: studio cycling classes streamed to a bike at home.

Slide order: Email Cover → Title → Concept → Spin Studios Exploding → Social Status → Music Content → Class Browser Mockup → Join Class Mockup → Live Class View → Rider Stats → Leaderboard → Join Class Mockup → Market Size → What Is Protectable → Value Chain Phase 1 → Value Chain Phase 2 → Prototype Plan

What works:
- Slide 3 explains the whole product with one three circle diagram that anyone gets in a few seconds.
- Slides 7 to 11 use simple mockups to show the rider experience step by step, from picking a class to seeing your rank against friends.
- Slides 15 and 16 split the plan into software first on existing bikes and custom hardware later, which makes a hardware company look less risky to start.

Lessons to steal:
- You don't need a finished brand or product to pitch, clear mockups of the core experience can carry an early deck.
- Point to a trend people can already see, like the spin studio boom on slide 4, instead of only quoting market reports.
- If your end product is expensive to build, show a cheaper first phase that tests the idea.

## WeWork (2014, Series D, raised )
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/wework
WeWork's 2014 growth-round deck: shared offices sold by the desk, as a community.

Slide order: Cover → Company Overview → Space as a Service → Millennials → Fundamental Shifts → Work Is Changing → New Ecosystem → The WeWork Effect → Technology → First Mover → Unit Economics → Growing Rapidly → Member Base → Value for Members → Value for Landlords → Landlord Partners → Pipeline → Capital Efficiency → US Opportunity → Membership Profile → Mobile App → Mobile Usage → Social Features → Services → Services Demand → Services Revenue → WeWork Everywhere → WeLive → WeLive Concept → WeLive Savings → Landlord Funding → Urban Campuses → WeLive Opportunity → Five Year Forecast → Leadership → Closing

What works:
- Slide 2 puts the most important facts on one page, including members, locations, revenue per member and occupancy.
- Slide 11 shows the numbers for every single location, so an investor can check that the margins hold across cities and not just on average.
- Slides 14 and 15 show why both sides win, members save 25% and landlords get more value from their buildings.

Lessons to steal:
- Put a key facts slide near the front so an investor sees the business in 30 seconds.
- Show unit economics at the level of one store, one customer or one cohort, not just the company total.
- If you run a marketplace or have partners, show what each side gets out of the deal in plain numbers.

## Alan (2018, Series A, raised €23M)
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/alan
Alan's Series A deck, led by Index: health insurance rebuilt as a simple app for French companies.

Slide order: Cover → We Are Alan → Positioning → Why Series A → Our Product → Company Signup → Employee Signup → Admin Tools → Health Plan and Price → Alan Card → Simple Reimbursement → Health Journal → Customer Service → User Love → Our Market → Health Market → Disability Upsell → Europe → Competitors → Key Metrics → Unit Economics → Contracts Signed → ARR Growth → Distribution → Acquisition Channels → Seasonality → ARR Targets → Execution → Proud Of → Capital Efficiency → Team Overview → Founders → Engineering Team → Insurance Team → Design, Support, Growth → How We Work → Seed Round → Next Steps → 2017 Roadmap → Underwriting With Data → Team Plan → Vision

What works:
- Slide 4 states why the company is raising in three plain goals before showing any product.
- Slides 6 and 7 prove the product is simple by counting the steps, with company and employee signup each done in 5 minutes.
- Slide 26 explains the market's odd seasonality up front, so an investor reads the flat months as normal and not as a warning sign.

Lessons to steal:
- Say what the round is for on one of the first slides, in a few short goals.
- If your pitch is simplicity, show it by counting steps or minutes instead of just saying the product is easy.
- Explain anything strange about your market, like seasonality or regulation, before an investor spots it and worries.

## TravelPerk (2021, Series D, raised $160M)
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/travelperk
TravelPerk's Series D deck: business travel booking and management from Barcelona.

Slide order: Cover → Agenda → Mission → Company story → One platform → Easy booking → Inventory → Support → One-stop shop → Reporting → Who it serves → New products → Leadership team → Values → Thank you

What works:
- Slide 3 states the problem with a contrast everyone knows, booking a holiday is fun and booking a work trip is not.
- Slide 4 packs six years of funding, customers, launches and acquisitions into one timeline, so momentum is visible at a glance.
- Slide 11 splits the value by buyer, which matters because the traveler, the travel manager and the finance team all have to say yes.

Lessons to steal:
- Open with a problem your reader has felt personally, in one or two sentences, before you show any product.
- If your product serves several people inside one customer, give each of them their own column and their own reason to buy.
- A single timeline slide is a cheap way to show progress even when you only have a few milestones.

## Voi (2019, Series B, raised $85M)
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/voi
Voi's Series B deck: shared e-scooters across European cities.

Slide order: Cover → Q3 results → Germany → Footprint → User growth → Cohorts → Tech and ops → Hardware → Regulation

What works:
- Slide 2 puts six headline numbers and two growth charts on one page, so the investor gets the whole story in one look.
- Slide 6 shows cohorts instead of just totals, which answers the question of whether riders come back.
- Slide 9 treats city regulation as a moat and shows tenders already won, not just a plan to win them.

Lessons to steal:
- Lead with your best numbers on slide 2 and write the takeaway as the slide title.
- If your business depends on repeat use, show retention by cohort, even if the lines are messy.
- Name your biggest outside risk, like regulation, and show what you have done about it so far.

## Aircall (2020, Series C, raised $65M)
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/aircall
Aircall's Series C deck: a cloud phone system for sales and support teams.

Slide order: Cover → Ambition → Problem → Current options → Solution → Why customers buy → Integrations → Ecosystem → Growth engines → Retention → Cohorts → Market → Thank you

What works:
- Slide 3 makes the problem obvious with one row of logos, every business tool got rebuilt except the phone.
- Slides 4 and 5 map the three existing options and then show Aircall keeping the best parts of each, which makes positioning easy to follow.
- Slide 10 leads with two retention numbers, 87% gross and 135%+ net, which is what growth-stage SaaS investors check first.

Lessons to steal:
- Show the problem as a pattern people already accept, then point at the one spot the pattern missed.
- Map the categories your buyer compares you against and say plainly what each one lacks.
- Once you have paying customers, put retention and cohort charts in the deck, even simple ones.

## Tinder (2012, Incubator, raised )
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/tinder
Note: This deck was made inside IAC's Hatch Labs incubator, not for an outside funding round.
The original Matchbox deck from inside IAC's Hatch Labs, before the app was renamed Tinder: a dating app built on mutual opt-in.

Slide order: Cover → Meet Matt → The moment → Problem → Solution → Like or Not → Matches → Messages → Under the hood → Revenue

What works:
- Slides 2 to 4 tell the problem as a short story about one person, which is easier to remember than a market statistic.
- Slide 4 names the real problem in three words, fear of rejection, and the product answers exactly that.
- Slides 6 to 8 walk through the app in the order a user would see it, so the idea is clear without explanation.

Lessons to steal:
- Tell your problem through one specific user in one specific moment.
- Name the emotional problem, not just the functional one, if that is what really stops people.
- Show the product as a short sequence of real screens instead of describing features.

## Snapchat (2014, Brand deck, raised )
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/snapchat
Note: This is a deck for advertisers, not a fundraising deck. It's still a sharp example of selling an audience in a few slides.
Snapchat's 2014 deck for brands and advertisers, which leaked in 2014: how the camera app sold its audience.

Slide order: Cover → History and stats → Philosophy → Product overview → Snap → Story → Chat and Here → Our Story → How it works → Your turn → What to snap → Get the word out → Examples → Stats and accounts

What works:
- Slide 2 gives a short origin story and a few big usage numbers before asking anything of the reader.
- Slides 5 and 6 define each feature in one line, which helps readers who have never used the app.
- Slides 11 and 13 show real brand Snaps as examples, so a marketer can copy them the same day.

## Facebook (2004, Media kit, raised )
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/facebook
Note: This is a 2004 media kit for advertisers, not a fundraising deck. Facebook's early funding (Peter Thiel's $500K in 2004) wasn't raised with it.
Thefacebook's 2004 media kit for advertisers, from when it was a network for college students.

Slide order: Cover → Slide layout → Opening quote → What it is → User profile → Profile extras → Social networking → Across schools → Expansion → Ivy League → Other schools → Expansion plan → Audience → Users by school → Users by role → Users by gender and age → Site usage → Monthly traffic → Press quote → User growth → Services → Advertising → Targeting → Rates → Contact → Closing quote

What works:
- Slides 10 and 11 list the exact launch date at each school, which proves fast spread better than any adjective could.
- Slide 17 leads with engagement, 65% of users visit every day, which is the number an advertiser cares about most.
- Slides 3, 19 and 26 use quotes from student newspapers as outside proof of demand.

Lessons to steal:
- When you are early, show engagement as a share of users, like daily visits, instead of only the total count.
- A dated list of launches or signups is simple and convincing proof of growth.
- Borrow credibility from third parties who wrote about you, and quote them in their own words.

## Tesla (2011, Investor deck, raised )
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/tesla
Note: This is a public-company investor presentation, not an early fundraising deck.
Tesla's 2011 investor presentation, after its IPO: the plan from the Roadster to a mass-market electric car.

Slide order: Cover → Team → Engineering Growth → Toyota → Panasonic → Daimler → Roadster → Model S → Model S Specs → Reservations → Platform → Timeline → Fremont Factory → Asset Purchases → Fremont Progress

What works:
- Slides 4 to 6 give one slide each to Toyota, Panasonic and Daimler, so outside partners do the work of proving the technology.
- Slide 10 shows Model S reservations rising every quarter and adds that the sales team was not even pushing them.
- Slide 11 explains the long term plan with one picture, a single platform under a sedan, a cabriolet, a van and an SUV.

Lessons to steal:
- Show demand before the product ships, the way slide 10 counts paid reservations for a car that did not exist yet.
- Let credible partners vouch for you with real numbers, like the $50M from Toyota and $30M from Panasonic on slides 4 and 5.
- Put your plan on a dated timeline investors can check later, as slide 12 does with alpha build, beta build and first deliveries.

## Square (2011, Unverified, raised )
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/square
Note: This is the version widely shared online as Square's pitch deck. Its origin is disputed: it has been described as a student project rather than a deck Square used.
The Square deck that circulates online: card payments for small merchants through a reader plugged into a phone.

Slide order: Cover → Proposition → Team → Market → Product → Value to User → Customer Acquisition → Competition → Financial Model → Why Invest Now → Exit Strategies → Appendix Index → Assumptions → Model Detail → Comparables → Valuation Multiples → Concerns → Partnerships → Vision → Market Analysis

What works:
- Slide 2 fits the whole pitch on one page, from 2.75% per swipe to a free app and card reader.
- Slide 6 compares Square with traditional card processors and online payments on ease, cost and benefit in one simple grid.

- Price your product in one number on an early slide, the way this deck repeats 2.75% per swipe.

- Add a concerns and responses slide so the hard questions get handled on your terms.

## Shopify (2015, Investor deck, raised )
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/shopify
Note: No startup-era Shopify pitch deck is public. The closest is its public-company investor deck, which Shopify posted itself.
Shopify never published its early fundraising deck. What's public is its investor presentation from after the 2015 IPO.

Slide order: 

What works:
- It opens with the first store on the platform, Snowdevil in 2004, so the story starts with a real merchant instead of a market chart.
- It shows pricing plainly, with plans at $29, $79 and $179 a month.
- It gives partners their own section and shows app developers, theme designers and agencies as part of why merchants stay.

## Waze (2008, Series A, raised $12M)
Page: https://pitchmagic.ai/decks/waze
Note: Waze's fundraising deck has never been made public. Decks shared online as 'the Waze pitch deck' are later advertising decks.
Waze raised a $12M Series A in 2008 for its crowdsourced maps, but it never published the deck it used.

Slide order: 

What works:
- There is no verified Waze fundraising deck, so there are no slides to judge, and the decks labeled as the Waze pitch deck online are later advertising decks.
- The idea fits in one sentence, drivers share road data as they drive and everyone gets a better map.
- The product gets better as more people use it, which is the kind of loop investors look for in any deck.
