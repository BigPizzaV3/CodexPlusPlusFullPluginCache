# The JRcmo Marketing Laws

Every law is named **JRcmo Law #N — Name**, from JRcmo Law #0 to JRcmo Law #36 (37 laws).

A LAW is how marketing works whether you like it or not — a smart competitor would be forced to agree. Every law is taught the same way: **WHAT** is true, **WHY** it's true, and **HOW** to use it. Then you go do it.

These laws come from 25+ years of real campaigns: $1.5B+ in ad placements since 1999, 100,000+ campaigns and 300+ marketing plans.

---

## The governing law

## JRcmo Law #0 — The Thinker and the Doer
- **What:** Writing the strategy is one thing. Someone has to execute it. Nothing gets done unless you have both the thinker and the doer.
- **Why:** You can get exactly the right plan and still get nowhere. A plan doesn't run itself, and most marketing fails in the gap between knowing what to do and someone actually doing it.
- **How:** For every recommendation, name who will do it, by when, and which number they'll report back. If nobody owns the doing, line up a doer (in-house, an agency or a build team) before you start. A plan without a doer is a wish.

## The core laws

## JRcmo Law #1 — Two Meanings of ROI
- **What:** "Return on Investment" is a backward-looking ratio. "Result of Investment" asks the forward-looking question: what changed because you invested that would not have happened otherwise?
- **Why:** Attribution tells you who got the credit. Incrementality tells you what the money actually caused.
- **How:** Track conversion rate, sales velocity, retention, price realization and CAC/LTV. A campaign with zero leads can still produce a result — it can prove the website is the failure point.

## JRcmo Law #2 — The Four Core Marketing Abilities
| Ability | What the buyer needs | Barrier |
|---|---|---|
| DEPEND | Trust | Risk |
| LIKE | An emotional pull toward you | Emotional resistance |
| RELATE | "This is for someone like me" | Relevance |
| KNOWLEDGE | Understanding | Confusion |
- **What:** A purchase happens only when all four are present.
- **Why:** Over-investing in one can't make up for missing another.
- **How:** Use it as a diagnostic grid. When sales stall, find the missing ability and fix that barrier first.

## JRcmo Law #3 — Leverage Marketing Strategy
- **What:** Small, controlled changes to a proven driver produce outsized gains without adding cost, risk or complexity.
- **Leverage hierarchy (highest → lowest):** Message > Audience > Offer > Experience > Spend. Spend is the LOWEST lever — it amplifies whatever is already broken upstream.
- **How:** Change ONE variable at a time, measure the lift against a baseline, lock the gain, then move to the next.

## JRcmo Law #4 — The Law of Platitudes
- **What:** What everyone agrees with persuades no one.
- **Acid Test:** Could a direct competitor say this without changing a word? If yes, delete it.
- **How:** Replace platitudes with copy that is Specific, Risk-bearing, Verifiable, Segmented and Opinionated.
- **Dead words (never use):** Lowest Prices, Best Service, Highest Quality, Most Professional, Largest Selection, Biggest in State, Family Owned, Most Reliable, In Business Since 19XX.

## JRcmo Law #5 — The Law of Thirds
- **What:** Roughly a third converts at each stage — impressions to leads, leads to qualified, qualified to closed.
- **Realistic benchmarks:** Impression → Click 0.5–3% · Click → Lead 10–30% · Lead → Close 20–35%.
- **How:** Work BACKWARD from the closed deals you need. Improving impression→click from 2% to 3% cuts the impressions you need by 33%. Improving lead→close from 30% to 40% cuts the leads you need by 25%.

## JRcmo Law #6 — The Law of Diminishing Returns
- **What:** Each additional dollar produces less than the one before it.
- **Warning signs:** CPA rising faster than volume · frequency above 3–5 · revenue flat while spend climbs.
- **How:** Judge by INCREMENTAL cost (the last dollar), not BLENDED cost (the average, which hides saturation). Scale until incremental CPA reaches your allowable CPA.
- **The one exception — the 48-Hour Haunt:** high-frequency retargeting for 24–48 hours after a site visit is allowed to break the frequency cap.

## JRcmo Law #7 — The Girlfriend Effect (Relevance Before Identity)
- **What:** Nobody cares who you are until they care what you can do for them. Don't lead with your name, logo, founding year or credentials.
- **First-Sentence Test:** Cover the opening line. If it names the company, the founder, years in business or the service category before naming something the reader wants, it fails.
- **How:** Open with the reader's outcome or tension. The ad sells the solution; your name shows up at the end as the domain. They learn who you are after they trust you.

## JRcmo Law #8 — The Check-Writing Test
- **What:** Marketing is tied to revenue or tied to nothing.
- **The test:** When the owner signs the monthly check, what number does their mind land on? If it lands on a number they care about, they sign without friction. If it lands on nothing, the account is lost no matter how good the work was.
- **How:** Build every plan backward from revenue to leads. Agree on one KPI the owner will remember at signing time, get it in writing, and make it revenue. "5 million impressions" is never a finish line.
- **KPI Lock:** open every meeting with "this is the main KPI — has it changed?", close by confirming it, and report against it next time. If the KPI moves, the work moves with it.

## JRcmo Law #9 — Pricing Obscure
- **What:** An odd price ($277, not $300) signals you worked out the real cost and pushed it down.
- **Why:** A lower entry price creates commitment and consistency — people who start small tend to continue.

## JRcmo Law #10 — Outcome Without Consequence
- **What:** A headline that states a neutral outcome tells the reader nothing. "Stop backups now" fails — it doesn't say what happens if you don't.
- **How:** Name the DAMAGE the reader is about to suffer. "We can fix your pipes without tearing out your floors."
- **Test:** Does it create a tear, a scare, or a pain point — or is it just a description of the service?
- **Companion — pain and consequence beat speed and features:** buyers aren't afraid of slow; they're afraid of damage.
- **Companion — headline-to-visual congruence:** if the headline says "don't dig up your floors," the image can't be a pipe.

## JRcmo Law #11 — The Default Wins
- **What:** Showing up consistently in the right place beats being clever occasionally. Be present often enough and you catch the demand that's already there.
- **How:** Make the action you want the easy default for the buyer. Commit to consistency (posting, SEO, showing up) — compounding presence drives the result, not any single clever move.

## JRcmo Law #12 — Stale Proof Is False Proof
- **What:** Proof only counts if it's specific and current. An undated testimonial or an old stat ("doubled in 2019") reads as false — if it were still true, you'd have a current number.
- **How:** Pin every claim to a specific, current metric and a date. If you can't show a fresh number, don't lean on a stale one.

## JRcmo Law #13 — Fresh Data or No Data
- **What:** If you don't keep updating the strategy, you never generate new data to make decisions from. Stale data leads to stale decisions.
- **How:** Keep the strategy live. Change one thing, read the result, decide, repeat.
- **Where it sits:** Fresh data is the principle behind JRcmo Law #33 — Test Before You Invest.

## JRcmo Law #14 — Clarity of Conversions
- **What:** Something set as a "conversion" isn't necessarily a lead. Engagement goals (time on page, pages per visit) are fine to track, but they are not leads.
- **How:** Before reading any conversion number, find out what it's actually measuring. A real lead is always a whole number.

## JRcmo Law #15 — Don't Trust the Zero
- **What:** Zero reported conversions doesn't mean nothing happened.
- **Example:** Joshua's own Google Ads showed zero attributed conversions for years — yet shutting them off took leads to zero. The ads educated buyers who came back later through other channels.
- **How:** Before you kill a channel showing zero, test what happens to total leads when it's off.

## JRcmo Law #16 — Don't Trust the Fraction
- **What:** A conversion count with a decimal (0.44, 0.62) means the platform is counting something that isn't a real conversion. You can't have a fraction of a person.
- **How:** When you see a decimal, find what's being tracked (page views, engaged sessions, time on page) and demote it to secondary or remove it.
- **The exception:** Fractional numbers are fine on goals you deliberately set to measure engagement (pages per visit, time on site), as long as a separate conversion counts real leads as whole numbers. Never judge leads from the fractional goal.

## JRcmo Law #17 — Own the Keys
- **What:** The business must OWN one central account that everything connects to — domain, hosting, analytics, ad accounts, tracking, every login.
- **Why:** So no agency, consultant or employee can ever hold the business hostage.
- **How:** One dedicated Google account owns everything. The owner holds the password from day one. Nothing connects around it.

## JRcmo Law #18 — Speed to Lead
- **What:** Speed wins. The business that responds first usually wins the customer.
- **Caveat:** Speed only helps once the data is right — fast follow-up on the wrong leads just loses money faster.

## JRcmo Law #19 — AI Cites What It Can Quote and Verify
- **What:** AI assistants (ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Google's AI answers) don't rank pages; they assemble answers from passages they can lift cleanly and confirm elsewhere. If your answer can't be quoted in a sentence or two — or nobody else says the same thing about you — you aren't in the answer.
- **Test:** Cover everything but the first 150 words of the page. Could an AI answer the searcher's question from just that? If not, it fails.
- **How:** Answer the question plainly up top, keep your business facts identical everywhere online, and date your claims.

## JRcmo Law #20 — The Curse of the Confident Owner
- **What:** The owner is usually the best salesperson the business has, face to face. But they're so deep in what they know that they can't put it into their marketing. That's the curse of knowledge, and it leaves a gap between the business and the buyer.
- **Why:** The business stands on one cliff, the buyer on the other. Marketing's job is to build the bridge they walk across. The bridge is made of trust, but not the word "trust": platitudes fall into the gap, and specific, checkable details hold weight.
- **The Plank Test:** For every claim, ask "could the buyer check this before they cross?" If yes, it's a plank. If no, it's decoration and it breaks under weight.
- **How:** Record the owner's best sales pitch and turn their own words into the website and ads. Then build the bridge: the gap is the four barriers from JRcmo Law #2 (risk, confusion, relevance, emotional resistance), and the bridge hangs from four cables (Depend, Like, Relate, Knowledge). Write two checkable planks for each doubt: a number, a date, a name, a written term. Delete any line a competitor could say word for word (JRcmo Law #4). Put each plank where the doubt shows up: the price explanation near the button, the guarantee next to the form, the dated result next to the claim it proves.

## JRcmo Law #21 — Own One Word
- **What:** Buyers file every business under one word or tight phrase, and call whoever owns that word when the need shows up. Try to own several words and you own none: you get filed under the generic category.
- **Why:** A business that markets ten services at equal volume becomes "another [category] company" and loses each specific call to the competitor who owns that one word. AI assistants repeat what the web says consistently about a business (JRcmo Law #19), so five versions of what you do means AI trusts none of them.
- **The test:** Ask five customers, "In one word, what are we known for?" If their answers don't match each other and don't match your homepage headline, you don't own a word yet.
- **How:** Ask five customers and five staff the one-word question. Pull your top three competitors' homepage headlines, and ask ChatGPT and Perplexity what you're known for. Score each candidate word: tied to your best margin, unclaimed by a competitor, already used by customers. The owner signs off, then the word goes into the homepage headline, Google Business Profile, ads and every directory listing. Re-check in 90 days. Owning a word doesn't mean you stop selling everything else; the other services get found after the buyer lands and trusts you.

## JRcmo Law #22 — Better Is Invisible. First Is Remembered.
- **What:** Buyers can't verify "better," so they ignore it. They remember who was first to own an idea in their mind. If a market already has a leader, don't claim to be better: name a new category and be the first to define it.
- **Why:** Every competitor says they're better, which makes "better" a platitude (JRcmo Law #4). When buyers can't tell providers apart, they compare the one thing they can measure: price. The business that owns a category gets asked for, not compared.
- **The test:** Remove your company name from your pitch. If the only claim left is "better," it fails.
- **How:** List what buyers fear in your category, from call recordings and leads that didn't close. Find the attribute competitors bury: usually what buyers care about most and the industry talks about least. Name it in plain words a buyer could type into Google or ask an AI, ideally around the damage it helps them avoid (JRcmo Law #10). Publish the first clear "What is [category]?" page, answered in the first 150 words. Repeat the name everywhere: ads, Google Business Profile, sales calls.
- **Examples:** "Better plumbing service" is invisible. "Fix your pipes without tearing out your floors" is remembered.

## JRcmo Law #23 — Uneducated Buyers Buy on Price
- **What:** Most buyers can't judge workmanship, process or expertise on their own. If nobody teaches them what to look for, they compare the one number they can see: price. The business that teaches buyers how to judge the work sets the standard every competitor is measured against.
- **Why:** Buyers aren't cheap; they're uninformed. A homeowner comparing three roofing quotes can't see the underlayment, flashing or insurance coverage, so they pick the lowest number. "Too expensive" is usually the wrong loss reason: nobody showed the buyer why the cheaper quote costs more in the end.
- **The test:** After reading your page, could a buyer ask a competitor a question that competitor would struggle to answer? If not, you informed the buyer but didn't educate them.
- **How:** Build a Buyer's Yardstick: "The 5 Questions to Ask Before You Hire Any [Category]." Each question is one you answer well and the average competitor answers badly, and each is tied to a consequence ("Ask if they pull the permit. If they don't, the insurance claim can be denied."). Ship it as a page, a one-page PDF and a sales-call script, and attach it to every quote. Never name a competitor. Track close rate on quotes sent with the Yardstick versus without, and how often price is the loss reason.
- **Informing vs. educating:** "We use premium materials" informs. "Ask every roofer what underlayment they use. If it's 15 lb felt, your roof has half the life of synthetic" educates.

## JRcmo Law #24 — Trash Talking Politely
- **What:** Never name or insult a competitor. Describe what your whole category does by default as a neutral fact, put your difference right next to it, and let the buyer reach the conclusion.
- **Why:** The buyer probably hired one of those competitors before. Insult that company and you tell the buyer they chose badly, so they defend the decision and you sound unprofessional. Naming a competitor also gives them free awareness, and a specific, measurable claim about them that you can't prove carries legal risk. Broad boasts are generally treated as puffery; specific claims about a named company are not.
- **The rules:** No names, no insults, no numbers about anyone else.
- **Three moves:** The only move ("The only fractional CMO who shows you the strategy before you hire"). The category default ("Most [category] do X by default. You get Y."). The structural tell ("An agency gets paid to run ads. A CMO gets paid when the ads pay off.").
- **How:** Write the category default as a neutral fact. Put your difference in the same or the next sentence. Strip every name, insult and number about anyone else. End on what the buyer gets. This isn't legal advice: have an attorney review any direct comparison.

## JRcmo Law #25 — Say It. Place It. Land It.
- **What:** All marketing comes down to a strategic message and a tactical placement. **Say It:** the message isn't what you say, it's what the buyer perceives. **Place It:** placement bends the message, because every channel has its own rules. **Land It:** the message and the placement only pay off when they land with the right buyer.
- **Why:** A good message in the wrong place, or the right place with a weak message, both fail. Figure out what the buyer is already thinking, say it back to them, and they agree and engage.
- **How:** Write the message from the buyer's side first. Then adapt it to each channel's rules: radio needs the phone number and website said 3–4 times in 30 seconds; a search ad has one line to land. Judge every placement by whether it landed with the right buyer, not by how many people saw it.

## JRcmo Law #26 — The Law of the Knockdown List
- **What:** "Marketing money must be spent in inverse proportion to uncertainty." Disqualify prospects cheaply first, and spend the expensive money only on the ones still standing.
- **Why:** Dividing the budget evenly across every prospect wastes most of it on people who were never going to buy.
- **How:** Build a curated list of prospects with a high likelihood of needing what you sell. Set the budget from net profit per close, not lead volume. Back-load the spend: early touches cost pennies and knock out the "already have it," "just bought" and "don't care" prospects, so only the survivors get the expensive touch.

## JRcmo Law #27 — Apples vs. Watermelons
- **What:** Your close rate tells you what kind of deals you're winning. A close rate around 80% usually means apples: easy, low-value deals. Around 30% means watermelons: bigger, harder deals worth far more.
- **Why:** A high close rate feels like success but often means you're only picking the easy fruit. The money is in the watermelons.
- **How:** Look at deal size alongside close rate. Aim for a close rate around 30% on watermelon-sized deals, and use JRcmo Law #5 (the Law of Thirds) to plan the leads it takes.

## JRcmo Law #28 — The Untrained AI Intern
- **What:** AI doesn't check everything until you've trained it the right way. It grabs the first source, stops, and reports back with confidence.
- **Why:** An AI asked about an ad account checked one data source, found a different account, and reported that as the answer. It never checked the second tool where the real account was. The answer sounded certain and was wrong.
- **How:** Give AI standing rules, and have a person check the work. First rule: make the AI check every connected source and tool, one after another, before it's allowed to say something isn't there. Put this law first in any presentation: every other law depends on AI work being checked.

## JRcmo Law #29 — The Buyer's Three Questions
- **What:** Every buyer is silently asking three questions: Why own it? Why not? Why you?
- **Why:** If your marketing skips any one of them, the buyer fills the gap with doubt, a competitor or the price.
- **How:** Check every page, ad and pitch answers all three. Deliver it in four steps: **Break It** (interrupt the everyday pattern), **Hook It** (spark curiosity), **Teach It** (show how to judge the choice), **Ask It** (one clear call to action). Match the message to the buyer type: ready, shopping or unaware.

## JRcmo Law #30 — Marketing Can't Outrun Operations
- **What:** Unanswered phones, bad reviews or staff who don't know the offer will waste any marketing budget.
- **Why:** Marketing only brings buyers to the door. If the door is locked, more marketing just sends more people away unhappy.
- **How:** Before you raise spend, call your own phone line at busy hours, read your latest reviews, and ask front-line staff what the current offer is. Fix what breaks first.

## JRcmo Law #31 — You Can't Click What You Can't See
- **What:** Buyers move through a show-up sequence: (1) you get seen, (2) being seen turns into clicks, (3) clicks engage and convert.
- **Why:** Each step needs a different fix, and changing the wrong one wastes time and money.
- **How:** Find the failing step first, then change only that. (1) Not seen? Check search impression share, Search Console impressions and rankings. (2) Seen but not clicked? Your titles and meta descriptions are platitudes; rewrite them. (3) Clicked but not converting? Fix the page with Break It, Hook It, Teach It, Ask It (JRcmo Law #29).

## JRcmo Law #32 — The Rearview Month
- **What:** This month's results were built 2–4 months ago.
- **Why:** Judging this month's work by this month's numbers leads to cutting what's working and keeping what isn't.
- **How:** Keep a Marketing Logbook: a monthly snapshot of the work done. When results move, look back 2–4 months in the log to find the cause.

## JRcmo Law #33 — Test Before You Invest
- **What:** Don't bet the budget on one channel before the market has voted with money.
- **Why:** Without fresh data (JRcmo Law #13) you're guessing. A rising tide raises all ships, so channels help each other, but some still leak.
- **How:** Split the monthly bucket (for example $10,000) across 4–5 channels, such as Google Ads, SEO, Meta, Reddit ads and ChatGPT ads, each with a logical budget. Pour more into the best revenue producer, fix the leakers if you can, and cut the one leaking too much, moving its money to the winner.

## JRcmo Law #34 — Budget Blindness
- **What:** Nobody is watching the money.
- **Why:** It cuts both ways. One business approved $4,000 and spent $2,557 of it on a single channel. Another thought it was spending $500–600 a month and spent $0 for three weeks, because a "maximize conversions" setting starved an exact-match campaign.
- **How:** Have someone compare actual spend with approved spend every week, by channel. Track the direction of results, not just the source, and diagnose in order before changing anything.

## JRcmo Law #35 — The Law of Simplicity
- **What:** Decide for the client. Lay out the best option, the direction and why, so the owner only has to say yes, no, or modify.
- **Why:** An owner handed sixteen decisions to approve stalls. An owner handed one recommendation moves.
- **How:** Turn every list of questions into a recommendation with a reason. Present it as Yes / No / Modify, and give options A, B and C when the choice is real.

## JRcmo Law #36 — The Tiebreaker
- **What:** When prices are equal, the buyer chooses whoever names the specific thing they care about that nobody else claims.
- **Why:** Look-alike offers push buyers to choose on price or the first name they saw. A specific, unclaimed promise gives them a reason that isn't price, and bold wording built on a true fact makes it memorable.
- **How:** (1) Put your price next to the top three competitors', fees included. (2) List what buyers care about and mark who claims each. (3) Take what's true about you and unclaimed. (4) Say it well: bold wording anchored to the checkable fact. (5) Say it often, in every channel. For example: "Anyone can say it. Here are three things they can't."
- **The test:** Cover the logos on your page and your top three competitors' pages. If a buyer can't tell which is you from one sentence, the tie isn't broken.
