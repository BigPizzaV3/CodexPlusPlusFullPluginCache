# Coaching by age

The fundamentals stay the same from 6 to 18, but attention, coordination and what players enjoy change fast, and with them the length of a practice and of each block, how much the coach talks and who leads. The bands are guides, not walls: plan for what the group can do today.

## 6–8
- **What they are like:** Lots of energy, a few minutes of focus. They learn by playing and copying. Balance and eyes-up dribbling are still hard. Being noticed matters more than the score.
- **A good practice:** About 45 minutes (30–60).
  - Blocks of 5–8 minutes. Change while it is still fun.
  - About half in games: tag and dribble games, 1-on-1, 2-on-2 on a small court.
  - A ball for every child, lines of three at most, one focus with one cue.
  - Nobody sits out: a caught player becomes a tagger or rejoins at once.
- **Teach:**
  - Skills: dribbling with both hands and eyes up, jump stops, catching, layup rhythm.
  - Team: pass, then cut to the basket.
  - Defense: stay between your player and the basket.
- **How to talk:** Under 30 seconds. Show it once, give one cue, start. Use stories ("eyes up like a periscope"). Praise effort by name.
- **Leave for later:** Help defense, zones and presses, set plays, full-court 5-on-5 in practice.

## 9–11
- **What they are like:** A prime age for learning skills. New moves stick fast, and they start to understand why. They want to get good and care about friends and fairness.
- **A good practice:** About 60 minutes (45–75).
  - Blocks of 8–12 minutes, the longer ones for games.
  - About 40% technique, 60% games. Each technique block leads into a game that needs the skill.
  - Weak-hand work every practice, three or four players per basket, plenty of 3-on-3.
- **Teach:**
  - Skills: everything with both hands, jump stops and pivots, layups from both sides, form shooting close in.
  - Team: spacing (about 3.5–4.5 meters apart), pass and cut, the give-and-go.
  - Defense: stance and slides, guarding the ball, closeouts with hands up. Help defense as an idea, no rotations yet.
- **How to talk:** Stops under 45 seconds. Make one point, show it, play. Ask "what did you notice?" Praise effort and progress, and celebrate wobbly weak-hand tries.
- **Leave for later:** Set plays (principles first), full help rotations, zone defense, long-range shooting, fixed positions by size.

## 12–14
- **What they are like:** Ready for reads, team defense and the why behind a play. Many are growing fast, so a smooth player can turn clumsy for a while, and bodies change at different times. Confidence swings.
- **A good practice:** About 75 minutes (60–90).
  - Team concepts get blocks of 10–15 minutes.
  - About half in live play: 2-on-2 to 4-on-4, and scrimmages with a rule that rewards the day's focus.
  - A 10-minute movement warm-up. Fitness comes from games, never from running as a consequence.
  - Players lead the warm-up and keep score.
- **Teach:**
  - Skills: footwork on the catch, shooting form, finishing through contact.
  - Team: screens, away from the ball first; cutting when the defender watches the ball; a simple 5-out offense.
  - Defense: help on a clear trigger, rotations, recovery to your own player.
- **How to talk:** Give the reason in one sentence. Praise in front of the team, correct one to one, and compare a player only with their own past. Tell a fast-growing player the skills will catch up.
- **Leave for later:** A thick playbook, fixed positions, heavy lifting, lots of full-court 5-on-5.

## 15–18
- **What they are like:** Almost adults. They can train hard, handle tactical detail and own their development. School, jobs and friends compete for their time. They stay where they improve, are heard and belong.
- **A good practice:** About 90 minutes (90–120).
  - Team offense and defense get 15–20 minutes a block, at game speed.
  - About half in live play, with a clock and a score in the scrimmage.
  - Players run the warm-up and pick a closing game. Conditioning sits inside live play.
- **Teach:**
  - Skills: still in every practice, with decisions at game speed.
  - Team: reading the defense, not running patterns (pick-and-roll reads, drive-and-kick). Two or three sets run well.
  - Defense: shared rules for help, rotations and the box out.
- **How to talk:** Partner more, direct less. Explain the why, ask what they saw, give real choices, set team goals together. Talk about roles early and honestly: what the player does well, then the next step.
- **Leave for later:** A long list of sets; advanced actions before the basic reads are solid.

## Mixed ages and mixed levels
- Set the level for the youngest, and keep an easier and a harder version of every drill ready: weak hand only, fewer dribbles, a defender.
- A player far ahead gets the harder version and a job, such as demonstrating, and stays with the group.
- Small-sided games make mixing easier: mix teams often, match similar sizes for 1-on-1 and contact drills.
