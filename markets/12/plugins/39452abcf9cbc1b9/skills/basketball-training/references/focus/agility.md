# Agility

Agility is changing speed or direction in response to something: a defender, a pass, a loose ball. Quick feet need quick eyes, so train both halves — the footwork and the read — in short bursts that feel like play.

## By age

- **6–8:** Play: tag, freeze tag, red light green light with a dribble, "copy me" games. The jump stop — a landing on two feet at once — and the athletic stance — knees bent, chest up, on the balls of the feet — come through the games. No long ladder sequences, no timed drills.
- **9–11:** A great age for footwork and reactions. Teach five basics by name: athletic stance, first step, jump stop, defensive slide, plant and cut. Add color calls, mirror drills and simple 1-on-1 and 2-on-2. Short ladder work always ends with a reaction.
- **12–14:** Growing bodies can feel clumsy for a while. Be patient and keep praising effort. Keep working on landing, stopping and cutting, add strength work with their own body, and make the cues game-like: a defender's hips, a pass. Sore knees or heels mean stop and tell a parent.
- **15–18:** Reading and anticipation now make the biggest difference. Game-speed reaction drills, 1-on-1 and 2-on-2 with real decisions, and questions that help players see cues earlier. Strength and power work, ideally with a qualified coach.

## Progression

1. **Alone:** Footwork on a known pattern: carioca — a sideways run where the trail foot crosses in front, then behind — defensive slides, a ladder or a T-drill. Short, crisp reps.
2. **On a signal:** Add a "go" at the end: a clap, then a pointed hand or a cone color. Look, decide, go.
3. **Against a partner:** Mirror drill: one leads, one follows, switch every 10 seconds. Then a half-speed dribbler against a sliding defender.
4. **In a game:** Live 1-on-1 in a lane, or 2-on-2 and 3-on-3 with a rule such as "every catch is a jump stop". Tag does the same job for the youngest.

Move up when most players react cleanly. Step back when feet get messy.

## Coaching points

- "Stay low for two steps." Fixes popping up on the first step.
- "Feet apart, push with the back foot." The defensive slide — side to side, feet never crossing.
- "Knee over the toes." For cuts and landings: lower the hips and land softly.
- "Watch the hips." The ball and the head can fake. The hips show where the dribbler is going.
- "You read that early!" Praise good reads, not only speed.

## Common mistakes

| What you see | What to say |
|---|---|
| Pops straight up on the first step | "Stay low for two steps." Put a cone two steps away as the mark |
| Steps backward before going forward | Start on the balls of the feet. Cheer every clean first step |
| Crosses the feet on a slide | Slow it down. "Feet apart, push with the back foot." |
| Knees cave in on cuts and landings | "Knee over the toes." Slow down, lower the hips, land softly |
| Eyes on the feet | Give a visual cue — a raised hand, a color call — so the eyes come up |
| Quick through the cones, beaten in games | End the pattern with a reaction, then play 1-on-1 |

One fix per practice. One clear cue, repeated, beats five corrections at once.

## In a practice

- **Minutes:** 8–12, two or three practices a week. For 6–8, tag and chase games are the agility work.
- **Where:** Right after the warm-up, while legs are fresh. Speed work is not conditioning: tired, messy reps teach messy feet.
- **Shape:** One footwork drill, one reaction drill, then a game that uses the same movement.
- **Bursts:** 3–6 seconds each, with at least as long to walk back.
- **Short lines:** Groups of two to four.
- **Stop:** When feet get sloppy or knees cave in. That is enough for today.
- **Pairs well with:** Individual defense (staying in front of a dribbler), cutting, and ball handling with eyes up.
