# Conditioning

Basketball fitness is the ability to repeat short, hard efforts — sprint, stop, jump, change direction — and recover quickly. Young players build it by playing: a practice with no lines and plenty of full-court play is already a conditioning session.

## By age

- **6–8:** Conditioning is play: tag games, dribble races, relays. No laps, no line drills. Change the game every 5–8 minutes and praise effort and energy, not speed.
- **9–11:** Fitness through skill, with the ball in their hands almost all the time: full-court dribbling, 2-on-1 and 3-on-2 games, small-sided scrimmages. Pair players of similar speed so every race is close.
- **12–14:** Transition drills and full-court small-sided games do the work. A short running set now and then, framed as training. Growing bodies need care: sore knees or heels mean stop and tell a parent.
- **15–18:** Game-speed drills plus short, planned sets before and early in the season: 17s — 17 sideline-to-sideline sprints against the clock — or line drills. Players set their own targets. Ask about the whole week: many play for school and club.

## Progression

1. **Alone, with a ball:** Full-court dribbling with both hands and layups on the run. Count the team's makes in two minutes, then try to beat the score.
2. **With a partner:** Races and relays against someone of similar speed. Passing and finishing at speed in threes.
3. **Against defenders:** 2-on-1 and 3-on-2, continuous: short sprints with a decision at the end and quick rotations.
4. **In a game:** 3-on-3 or 4-on-4 full court, where a basket within 5 seconds of a rebound or steal counts double. Or 2-on-2 where the pair that scores stays on.

From about 15, add one or two sets of 17s or line drills — baseline to each line of the court and back — with full rest between sets. Line drills are also known as suicide sprints. With players, say "line drills".

## Coaching points

- "Beat your own score." A personal or team target, never one time for everyone.
- "This gets us ready for the fourth quarter." Say why. Running is training.
- "First one back!" Celebrate the sprint back on defense, not only the basket.
- "Everyone drinks." Water and real breaks, most of all in a hot gym.
- "Tired is fine. Pain or feeling sick means stop." Nobody runs through it. Tell a parent.

## Common mistakes

| What you see | What to say |
|---|---|
| Focus drops, or a game is lost, and the baseline looks tempting | Running is training, never a consequence. Name what you want to see and start a new game |
| Players waiting in long lines | Smaller teams and quick rotations. Nobody waits more than a minute |
| Effort fades late in games | Add five minutes of full-court play, not a block of running |
| Sloppy decisions when tired | Practice decisions at game speed: 2-on-1 and 3-on-2 |
| Players leave practice exhausted | Exhaustion is not the goal. A little tired, very sweaty and eager to come back is |
| Players drag at the start of practice | It may be too much basketball this week. Go lighter: shooting games, shorter scrimmages |
| A player hangs back or asks to come out | Check in privately. It may be a sore body, a hard week at school or nerves |
| The same players finish last in every run | Waves of four to six, personal targets, and nobody runs extra for a teammate |

## In a practice

- **Minutes:** None set aside under 12. The running happens with a ball, inside drills and games, and a full-court game closes practice.
- **From 12:** A short set now and then for 12–14. One or two sets for 15–18, early or in the middle of practice.
- **Never last:** End with a shooting game or shout-outs, so practice closes on something players like.
- **Skip the sets:** The day before a game or tournament, and after a heavy game weekend.
- **Across the season:** Start with shorter games and longer rests, then stretch the games and trim the rests. Ease off before a big tournament.
- **Pairs well with:** Transition, finishing and ball handling at speed.
