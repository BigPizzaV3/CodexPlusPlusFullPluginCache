## Slide 2: Using this reference

Section: Introduction. PDF page: 2.

### For diagnosis

- Choose one system in focus and a specific concern.
- Trace actual decisions, resources and communication.
- Record evidence, conflicting accounts and unanswered questions.

### For design

- Work through the seven steps and revisit earlier choices.
- Use the blank templates as prompts for local agreements.
- Test the proposed arrangements in real work before expanding them.

**Reading note:** The reference supports inquiry. It provides no numerical viability score or guarantee of organisational success.

## Slide 3: Viability is an ongoing organisational question

Section: Foundations. PDF page: 3.

### Capacity to continue

- The organisation fulfils a purpose in a changing environment.
- Its operating units can respond to the variety they encounter.
- The whole maintains enough cohesion to act together.

### Capacity to change

- People detect changes that current routines do not handle.
- They can consider alternatives and commit resources.
- Identity and policy give the organisation continuity as it adapts.

**Reading note:** A VSM map describes regulatory functions and relationships. Evidence is still needed to judge how they work.

**Intellectual sources:** Stafford Beer; Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

Section: Foundations. PDF page: 4.

| Field | Working prompt |
| --- | --- |

| Boundary | Which activities and decisions belong inside the inquiry? What sits outside? |

## Slide 5: The environment has several relevant parts

Section: Foundations. PDF page: 5.

### Present operating environment

- Customers or beneficiaries with different needs.
- Suppliers, partners and shared infrastructure.
- Current obligations, constraints and resource conditions.

### Wider and future environment

- Emerging needs and alternative ways to meet them.
- Changes in technology, society and resource availability.
- Other actors whose choices could change the organisation's purpose or options.

**Reading note:** Define the relevant environment for each operational unit and for the whole. It extends beyond a customer list.

**Intellectual sources:** Stafford Beer; Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 6: Recursion connects capable units

Section: Foundations. PDF page: 6.

### Diagram description

- R+1: a federation of cooperatives contains R0: one repair cooperative.
- R0 contains R-1: district repair hubs. R-1 may contain R-2: local service teams, if those teams form viable units.

## Slide 8: The VSM functions at one recursion level

## Slide 9: S1: primary operations

Section: Functions. PDF page: 9.

### Purpose

- Operational units deliver the primary activities of the system in focus.
- Each unit engages with its own relevant environment.
- Local management regulates delivery and adapts within agreed boundaries.

### Diagnostic evidence

- Clear outputs and recipients for each unit.
- Resources and decision authority consistent with accountability.
- Local feedback and a working route to request help.
- Results visible without routing every local decision through the centre.

**Reading note:** A unit's label on an organisation chart does not establish that it can function as a viable unit.

**Intellectual sources:** Stafford Beer; Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 10: S2: coordination

Section: Functions. PDF page: 10.

### Purpose

- Reduce avoidable interference between operating units.
- Provide shared conventions where units depend on one another.
- Help units resolve routine conflicts without constant intervention.

### Possible mechanisms

- Capacity calendars and scheduling protocols.
- Compatible information definitions and handover rules.
- Peer coordination and agreed conflict-resolution routes.
- Standards whose purpose and exception process are clear.

**Reading note:** S2 needs participation and usable mechanisms. A coordination meeting alone does not demonstrate coordination.

**Intellectual sources:** Stafford Beer; Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 11: S3: internal cohesion and resource decisions

Section: Functions. PDF page: 11.

### Purpose

- Manage the whole's current operating capability.
- Negotiate resources and accountabilities with S1 management.
- Resolve issues that exceed local authority or affect the whole.

### Diagnostic evidence

- Resource agreements tied to actual commitments.
- Visibility of dependencies, constraints and results.
- Intervention criteria that preserve ordinary local autonomy.
- Decisions that consider the performance of the whole.

**Reading note:** S3 needs a reliable dialogue with S4 so that present commitments and future options remain connected.

**Intellectual sources:** Stafford Beer; Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 12: S3*: direct assurance and operational reality

Section: Functions. PDF page: 12.

### Purpose

- Complement routine reports with direct information about operations.
- Test whether reported conditions match actual work.
- Bring overlooked constraints and weak signals into view.

### Possible mechanisms

- Observation of work and direct conversations.
- Sample reviews, audits and trace-through checks.
- Independent checks of a particular claim or risk.
- A feedback route that records findings and corrective action.

**Reading note:** S3* is distinct from S3's resource and intervention role. Its mandate should prevent a second chain of routine management.

**Intellectual sources:** Stafford Beer; Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 13: S4: intelligence and adaptation

Section: Functions. PDF page: 13.

### Purpose

- Explore the wider environment and plausible futures.
- Develop an understanding of the organisation and its changing position.
- Form and test options for adaptation.

### Diagnostic evidence

- Signals beyond current performance reports.
- Explicit assumptions, scenarios and alternative responses.
- Experiments with learning questions and decision dates.
- A continuing exchange with S3 about feasibility and resources.

**Reading note:** S4 connects outside and future concerns to the organisation's present capabilities. Its role extends beyond preparing a strategy document.

**Intellectual sources:** Stafford Beer; Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 14: S5: identity and policy

Section: Functions. PDF page: 14.

### Purpose

- Express the identity, purpose and policy of the whole.
- Set the boundaries within which other functions act.
- Resolve fundamental tensions between present operation and future direction.

### Diagnostic evidence

- A usable statement of purpose and policy.
- Clear authority for decisions about identity and governing principles.
- A route to hear the S3-S4 debate without taking over all its decisions.
- A process for revising policy when its assumptions fail.

**Reading note:** Map the policy function explicitly. A senior title alone does not show that S5 is functioning.

**Intellectual sources:** Stafford Beer; Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 15: The algedonic channel

Section: Functions. PDF page: 15.

Exceptional signals need an agreed route to a level that can respond.

| Design element | Working agreement |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger | Describe unacceptable states or exceptional opportunities that warrant urgent attention. |
| Destination | Name the responsible recipient and backup at the appropriate higher level. |
| Response | Specify acknowledgement, decision authority and an action time suited to the situation. |
| Escalation | State what happens when the recipient cannot respond or the condition worsens. |
| Closure | Confirm recovery or resolution with the originator and review the threshold. |

**Reading note:** Algedonic signals supplement routine channels. A threshold and a reliable response matter more than the label on the alert.

**Intellectual sources:** Stafford Beer; Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 16: Variety and requisite variety

Section: Foundations. PDF page: 16.

### Variety

- The distinguishable states or situations relevant to a system.
- A practical inquiry asks which differences demand different responses.
- More data does not automatically mean more useful regulatory capacity.

### Requisite variety

- Effective regulation needs responses capable of handling relevant disturbances.
- Capacity depends on skills, authority, information, resources and timing.
- A design can reduce avoidable demands or expand appropriate responses.

**Reading note:** Conceptual foundation: W. Ross Ashby, An Introduction to Cybernetics (1956), especially chapters 7 and 11.

**Intellectual sources:** W. Ross Ashby; Stafford Beer. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 17: Variety engineering in everyday work

Section: Foundations. PDF page: 17.

Choose the intervention after identifying the demand that exceeds response capacity.

| Intervention | Example | Question to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Attenuate avoidable demand | A common intake form prevents repeated clarification. | Does it remove waste while preserving important differences? |
| Increase response capacity | Local staff gain training and authority for recurring decisions. | Can they now respond safely and promptly? |
| Improve the channel | A queue separates urgent exceptions from routine updates. | Do signals reach someone able to act? |
| Change the structure | A unit takes responsibility for a coherent customer need. | Does the new boundary reduce costly dependencies? |

**Reading note:** These are original application examples. Attenuation should preserve information needed to protect purpose and outcomes.

**Intellectual sources:** W. Ross Ashby; Stafford Beer. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 18: Subsidiarity combines autonomy with support

Section: Foundations. PDF page: 18.

### Local authority

- Locate decisions close to the information and consequences.
- State boundaries, required outcomes and resource commitments.
- Give units room to select methods appropriate to their environment.

### Accountability and help

- Make results and material exceptions visible to the containing system.
- Escalate when a decision exceeds agreed authority or capacity.
- Provide support that helps the unit regain or strengthen its capability.

**Reading note:** Review centralisation decisions when capability, risk or dependencies change. Autonomy is a designed relationship.

**Intellectual sources:** Stafford Beer; Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 19: The seven-step application method

Section: Seven-step method. PDF page: 19.

The sequence below follows Martin Pfiffner. Iteration is expected.

| Step | Focus | Concrete output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | Segmentation of operational units | A justified boundary and operational-unit design |
| II | Manageability | A plan for demand, response capacity and channels |
| III | Success-critical tasks | A short, explicit inventory of continuing tasks |
| IV | Central and decentral allocation | Task parts assigned across recursion levels |
| V | Design of S2 to S5, including S3* | Mandates and mechanisms for each function |
| VI | Communication channels | Named signals, recipients, timing and response |
| VII | Representation | An understandable map, role mandates and RASIC |

**Reading note:** Pfiffner's English edition calls Step III "Mission-Critical Tasks". This reference uses "success-critical tasks" (SCTs).

**Intellectual sources:** Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 20: Step I: segmentation of operational units

Section: Step I. PDF page: 20.

### Inquiry

- Confirm purpose and the relevant external needs.
- Describe current units, dependencies and management boundaries.
- Develop credible alternative segmentations, such as geography, customer group or service.

### Decision

- Compare the options against purpose and environmental differences.
- Consider whether each unit can regulate its own work.
- Record tradeoffs, assumptions and the evidence needed to revisit the choice.

**Reading note:** Output: a proposed set of S1 units, the rationale for the boundary and the issues that require a further recursion-level inquiry.

**Intellectual sources:** Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 23: Step I example: district repair hubs

Section: Step I. PDF page: 23.

### Proposed design

- A fictional cooperative serves local households through district hubs.
- Each hub owns the customer relationship and repair outcome.
- Each hub manages a team, equipment capacity and an operating budget.

### Why this may fit

- Travel distance and local demand differ between districts.
- Most repair decisions can remain close to the work.
- Shared purchasing and specialist assistance still need agreements.
- Evidence on demand and dependencies must confirm the design.

**Reading note:** Illustrative design assumption. It is not a finding about a real organisation.

## Slide 24: Step II: manageability

Section: Step II. PDF page: 24.

### Demand on management

- How different are the units' environments and operating situations?
- Which interdependencies create recurring conflicts?
- How quickly do conditions change? Which decisions wait too long?

### Capacity to respond

- Which decisions can units resolve themselves?
- Which coordination mechanisms reduce recurring interference?
- Can the whole process exceptions at the required speed?
- Are reporting and assurance channels accurate enough to act on?

**Reading note:** The count of units is only one factor. There is no universal maximum span or layer count in this reference.

**Intellectual sources:** W. Ross Ashby; Stafford Beer; Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 25: Manageability evidence template

Section: Step II. PDF page: 25.

| Demand or bottleneck | Observed evidence | Candidate change | Expected effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Decision class | Volume, delay, rework | Authority, skill or rule | Observable outcome |
| Shared dependency | Conflict episodes | Coordination mechanism | Less interference |
| Exceptional condition | Signal and response times | Escalation or support | Earlier effective action |
| Missing information | Decisions made with gaps | Direct observation or channel | Better-grounded decisions |

**Reading note:** For each change, name an owner and a review date. Keep the baseline evidence so that the effect can be examined.

**Intellectual sources:** Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 26: Manageability can improve in several ways

Section: Step II. PDF page: 26.

### Possible changes

- Remove approval steps that add no useful regulation.
- Improve local capability and access to expertise.
- Agree peer coordination for recurrent dependencies.
- Improve the clarity and speed of exception reporting.

### Design caution

- A flatter structure can increase the demands on the remaining channels.
- Standardisation can hide meaningful differences if applied too broadly.
- New reporting can consume the capacity it was intended to support.
- Test the change against actual work and failure conditions.

**Reading note:** Choose a change for its expected regulatory effect. Fewer layers is not an end in itself.

**Intellectual sources:** W. Ross Ashby; Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 27: Step III: success-critical tasks

Section: Step III. PDF page: 27.

### Where tasks come from

- Promises and continuing needs in the environment.
- The regulation of each unit's operating activity.
- Overlaps between units' environments and resources.
- Adaptation, identity and obligations of the whole.

### A useful SCT

- Describes work that must recur or remain available.
- Names an observable result and the condition that calls for it.
- Has a clear recursion level and purpose contribution.
- Can be allocated without hiding materially different task parts.

**Reading note:** A project may establish an SCT mechanism. The ongoing task remains after the project closes.

**Intellectual sources:** Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 30: SCT priorities, consequences and evidence

Section: Step III. PDF page: 30.

### Priority discussion

- What fails if this task is absent or unreliable?
- How soon would the consequences matter?
- Can another mechanism currently cover the need?
- Which uncertainty would most change the decision?

### Record the judgement

- State the evidence and the people consulted.
- Separate observed failures from plausible risks.
- Identify dependencies between proposed improvements.
- Keep the task inventory small enough to discuss, with detail linked beneath it.

**Reading note:** Priorities reflect the organisation's context and policy. The number of SCTs is a facilitation choice.

**Intellectual sources:** Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 31: Step IV: allocation across recursion levels

Section: Step IV. PDF page: 31.

### Allocation inquiry

- Which task parts benefit from local information and response?
- Which parts require common policy or shared capability?
- Where do consequences cross the unit boundary?

### Allocation agreement

- Specify the work at each level and the decision authority.
- Define the result each side owes the other.
- Provide an exception route and a way to request support.
- Record why this allocation is preferable under current conditions.

**Reading note:** Allocation can divide task parts. A broad label such as "central" hides who decides, delivers and checks.

**Intellectual sources:** Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 35: Step V: design of the regulatory functions

Section: Step V. PDF page: 35.

### Map the work

- Group task parts by the function they serve.
- Make S2, S3, S3*, S4 and S5 explicit at the chosen recursion.
- Identify the roles and mechanisms that already do useful work.

### Design the mechanisms

- Clarify purpose, authority and inputs for each function.
- Provide people, time and tools matched to demand.
- Define outputs and the recipient who can use them.
- Check for missing functions and competing mandates.

**Reading note:** One committee can serve several functions, provided its agenda, authority and outputs make those functions clear.

**Intellectual sources:** Stafford Beer; Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

## Slide 39: Step VI: communication channels

Section: Step VI. PDF page: 39.

### A usable channel

- Carries a signal that the recipient can interpret.
- Reaches someone with the capacity and authority to respond.
- Arrives soon enough for the intended action.
- Includes feedback that confirms understanding and closure.

### Design properties

- Capacity matches normal demand and exceptional bursts.
- Definitions and context survive translation between roles.
- Cadence and response time match the pace of the work.
- Access, reliability and backup routes suit the information.

**Reading note:** A channel includes people, meaning and action. Naming a software tool or a recurring meeting leaves much of the design unresolved.

**Intellectual sources:** Stafford Beer; Martin Pfiffner. Bibliographic details appear below.

| R0 with R+1 and R-1 | Policy, commitments, capability and exceptions | Maintain coherent autonomy across recursion levels. |

**Reading note:** The endpoints are functional roles. They can span several people and mechanisms.

## Slide 43: Transducers preserve meaning between roles

Section: Step VI. PDF page: 43.

### Diagram description

- A fictional feedback loop starts with customer experience: "The repair took longer than promised."
- A structured operating signal captures the commitment, actual time, cause and local response.
- Decision context identifies where the delay occurs, its consequences and options.
- The response to the work states a clear action, owner, new commitment and feedback. The loop reconnects with customer experience.
- At each translation, ask what meaning was lost and whether people can act on the response.

**Reading note:** A transducer changes the form of a signal so another role can use it. Test what meaning the translation preserves, omits or distorts.

## Use of the method

A diagram, a completed matrix or a questionnaire response is evidence about an organisational design, not proof of viability. Record assumptions, test relationships in actual work and revise the design as its environment changes. The working conventions in this reference, including one accountable role per active task, are explicit template choices.
