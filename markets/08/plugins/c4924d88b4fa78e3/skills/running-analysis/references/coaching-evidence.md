# Coaching evidence: what it supports and what it does not

A short, source-traceable reference for common questions. Each entry gives:

- **Claim:** what the evidence supports;
- **Use:** how to use it;
- **Limits:** its weaknesses.

None of it is a universal rule. Each runner's own data and circumstances come first. Performance Signal does not claim professional coaching or medical credentials.

Sources were checked against publisher, PubMed or repository records on 29 October 2026. Where the DOI was not confirmed, the entry says so.

## Training intensity distribution

- **Claim:** Well-trained endurance athletes commonly do most sessions at low intensity and a minority at high intensity (about 80/20 by sessions). Elite distance runners often train in a pyramidal distribution in preparation phases and a more polarised one near competition.
- **Use:** It is reasonable to suggest that most running be easy and hard sessions be deliberate and limited. Present polarised and pyramidal patterns as options.
- **Limits:**
  - The evidence is mostly descriptive studies of trained or elite athletes, with few controlled trials in recreational runners.
  - Distributions depend on how intensity is measured (sessions or time, heart rate or pace).
- **Sources:**
  - Seiler S. What is best practice for training intensity and duration distribution in endurance athletes? *Int J Sports Physiol Perform* 2010;5(3):276-291. doi:10.1123/ijspp.5.3.276
  - Casado A, González-Mohíno F, González-Ravé JM, Foster C. Training periodization, methods, intensity distribution, and volume in highly trained and elite distance runners: a systematic review. *Int J Sports Physiol Perform* 2022;17(6):820-833. doi:10.1123/ijspp.2021-0435

## Weekly load progression and injury

- **Claim:** In a randomised trial of 532 novice runners, a graded programme built on the "10% rule" did not reduce running-related injuries compared with a standard programme: about 21% and 20% injured.
- **Use:** Suggest gradual, individual increases and attention to pain. Do not present 10% per week, or any fixed number, as proven protection.
- **Limits:**
  - The trial was in novice runners preparing for a 4-mile event.
  - It does not show that rapid increases are safe.
- **Source:** Buist I, Bredeweg SW, van Mechelen W, et al. No effect of a graded training program on the number of running-related injuries in novice runners: a randomized controlled trial. *Am J Sports Med* 2008;36(1):33-39. doi:10.1177/0363546507307505

## Acute:chronic workload ratios

- **Claim:** Ratios of recent to longer-term training load have been promoted for injury risk. Their conceptual and statistical basis has been strongly criticised.
- **Use:** Do not present a computed load ratio as an injury-risk or readiness score. Describe week-to-week changes in plain numbers instead.
- **Limits:** This is an active scientific dispute; avoid both overclaiming and dismissing load monitoring altogether.
- **Source:** Impellizzeri FM, Tenan MS, Kempton T, Novak A, Coutts AJ. Acute:chronic workload ratio: conceptual issues and fundamental pitfalls. *Int J Sports Physiol Perform* 2020;15(6):907-913. doi:10.1123/ijspp.2019-0864 (DOI from repository record)

## Cadence (step rate)

- **Claim:** In a laboratory study of 45 healthy recreational runners, increasing step rate by 5-10% at the same speed reduced energy absorption at the knee (and at the hip at +10%).
- **Use:** Mention a small cadence increase as one option for runners interested in reducing knee and hip loading, especially with a coach or clinician for an existing problem. Do not set a universal target cadence such as 180.
- **Limits:**
  - A biomechanical study on a treadmill, not an injury-outcome trial.
  - Cadence rises naturally with speed, so compare cadence at similar paces.
  - Some sources report cadence per leg, so check units before comparing (the tools report units).
- **Source:** Heiderscheit BC, Chumanov ES, Michalski MP, Wille CM, Ryan MB. Effects of step rate manipulation on joint mechanics during running. *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 2011;43(2):296-302. doi:10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181ebedf4

## Heart rate: maximum and zones

- **Claim:** Age-predicted maximum heart rate (for example 208 - 0.7 × age) is a population average with wide individual variation.
- **Use:** Do not assign the runner a maximum heart rate or zones from age. Use observed values as observations; suggest a proper test or a coach if zones matter.
- **Limits:** The equation describes groups, not individuals.
- **Source:** Tanaka H, Monahan KD, Seals DR. Age-predicted maximal heart rate revisited. *J Am Coll Cardiol* 2001;37(1):153-156. doi:10.1016/s0735-1097(00)01054-8

## Heart rate: sensor accuracy

- **Claim:** Wearable heart rate accuracy varies by device and activity. A chest strap agreed most closely with ECG. Wrist devices were less accurate, particularly at higher intensity for some devices.
- **Use:** Before interpreting a spike, a flat line or an implausible value, consider a sensor artefact. Check the device source if the runner asks.
- **Limits:** The study tested specific 2016-2017 devices in a laboratory; current devices may differ.
- **Source:** Gillinov S, Etiwy M, Wang R, et al. Variable accuracy of wearable heart rate monitors during aerobic exercise. *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 2017;49(8):1697-1703. doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000001284

## Heat, hydration and heart rate rising during a run

- **Claim:** Heat stress raises thermoregulatory and cardiovascular strain and impairs endurance performance. Heart rate tends to rise at a constant pace in the heat and with dehydration.
- **Use:** Offer heat or hydration as one possible explanation for heart rate drift or a slower pace, but only as a possibility. The tools do not provide weather.
- **Limits:**
  - A broad review, not a way to attribute a specific run.
  - Other causes (fatigue, pacing, terrain, illness, sensor) can look the same.
- **Source:** Périard JD, Eijsvogels TMH, Daanen HAM. Exercise under heat stress: thermoregulation, hydration, performance implications, and mitigation strategies. *Physiol Rev* 2021;101(4):1873-1979. doi:10.1152/physrev.00038.2020 (DOI from secondary listings)

## Pacing strategy

- **Claim:** Researchers describe several pacing profiles (negative, positive, even, all-out, parabolic, variable). For events longer than about two minutes, a fairly even distribution is commonly associated with good performance.
- **Use:** Describe the runner's actual split profile, and frame a fade as one profile with several possible causes.
- **Limits:** The source is a descriptive review across sports; course, wind and tactics matter.
- **Source:** Abbiss CR, Laursen PB. Describing and understanding pacing strategies during athletic competition. *Sports Med* 2008;38(3):239-252. (DOI not confirmed; citation from repository and indexing records.)

## Race-time prediction

- **Claim:** In 2,303 recreational runners, Riegel's formula was reasonable up to the half marathon, but it underestimated marathon times and was off by 10 minutes or more for half the runners.
- **Use:** Give equivalent times only as rough ranges, explain the assumptions (similar training, comparable conditions), and never as guarantees, especially when predicting a marathon from short races.
- **Limits:** Survey data from self-reported races.
- **Source:** Vickers AJ, Vertosick EA. An empirical study of race times in recreational endurance runners. *BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil* 2016;8:26. doi:10.1186/s13102-016-0052-y

## Symptoms and when to get medical advice

- **Claim:** Professional screening guidance treats exercise-related symptoms suggestive of cardiovascular disease (such as chest discomfort, unusual breathlessness, fainting or palpitations) as a reason to stop and seek medical clearance.
- **Use:** If the runner reports such symptoms, do not give training advice that continues hard exercise; recommend medical assessment, urgently for severe or current symptoms. For pain or injury, suggest easing off and seeing an appropriate professional. Never clear the runner to train.
- **Limits:** Screening guidance is for professionals and does not replace individual medical advice.
- **Source:** Riebe D, Franklin BA, Thompson PD, et al. Updating ACSM's recommendations for exercise preparticipation health screening. *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 2015;47(8):2473-2479. doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000000664 (one index lists issue 11; the consensus reprint gives issue 8)

## Topics not covered

There is no researched reference here for:

- nutrition plans;
- strength programmes;
- injury rehabilitation;
- women's health;
- altitude.

For these, give only general, clearly hedged guidance and suggest a qualified professional.
